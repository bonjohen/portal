A side project, built with Claude Code

Hundreds of open trials, one patient, and the line of fine print that decides

When someone in my family was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia, I wanted to know which clinical trials might matter for her. The answer is buried in eligibility criteria, and the trial list changes every week. So I built a watch that reads it for us and turns it into questions for her doctors.

The problem

ClinicalTrials.gov lists over 500 active AML studies of phase 2 or later. Each one has pages of inclusion and exclusion criteria. For an older patient already on treatment, most trials are ruled out by a single line: an age cap, a requirement that the patient is untreated, a mutation she doesn't have. Finding those lines by hand, and checking again every week as trials open and close, is more than a family can keep up with.

Her oncologists are the ones who decide. What we can do is arrive at appointments with better questions.

How it works

  1. Gather, twice a day

    A scheduled GitHub Actions job pulls from free public sources: ClinicalTrials.gov, PubMed, Europe PMC preprints, FDA drug shortages and ClinVar. It records what is new or changed since the last run.

  2. Rank, without throwing anything away

    Trials are sorted by checks in a fixed order: age, myeloid disease, enrolling now, non-intensive treatment, not transplant-focused, a site near home, phase 3 or 4. Missing a check moves a trial down. It never removes it.

  3. Read the fine print

    Claude reads each trial's eligibility text against her de-identified profile and returns a verdict: blocking criterion, depends on a fact we don't have yet, or nothing blocking found. Every verdict must quote the exact line it relies on. Code checks that the quote really appears in the record, and rejects the verdict if it doesn't.

  4. Get a second opinion where it matters

    For the blockers that matter most (trials near home, or ranked high), a stronger model reads them again. Where the two disagree, I look at the record myself and note which one stands.

  5. Publish questions, not answers

    The result is a private page with the open questions for her care team, who should answer each one, and a link to the evidence. "Was she tested for NPM1?" is something we can ask. "She should enroll" is never something the page says.

Ground rules

Questions for the care team

The page frames every finding as a question for her oncologist. It never recommends a treatment or says she qualifies.

Quote it or drop it

A verdict without a verbatim quote from the record is rejected and retried. A person can check any verdict against its source in seconds.

Only re-read what changed

Verdicts are cached with the exact text they were based on. A trial is read again only when its record changes, an answer about her changes, or a 90-day audit comes due.

A fixed budget

The review runs on my own Claude subscription. Each daily refresh gets at most 15% of a 5-hour usage window, and it stops early if the window runs high.

Her records stay home

Lab reports never go to GitHub. The profile in the repository holds only the facts the matching needs, with no name or dates of birth, and the repository is private.

A person decides

Model disagreements, new data sources and every change to the search go through me first.

The most useful finding was simple. Because she had already started treatment, most first-line trials no longer apply. The few that accept patients already on her drugs, sometimes only within one or two cycles, are the ones to ask about now.

It also looks for what it is missing

Each run, the watch proposes one new source or search, tests it live against the public APIs, and logs what it would have found that we didn't already have. I accept or reject it. Some of what has come out of that log so far:

AcceptedPhase 1 trials with a site nearby, ranked last. It found 33 early-phase studies the main search never saw.
AcceptedPreprints from medRxiv and bioRxiv, which show results weeks or months before a journal prints them.
AcceptedClosed trials of her drugs that have posted results on ClinicalTrials.gov, often before any paper.
AcceptedExpanded-access programs, which let a patient outside a trial get an unapproved drug through her own doctor. It found four for AML that are open now.
No yieldEvery blood-cancer study at her own hospital, whatever its label. All 22 studies it would have added were for other cancers, so it wasn't added.

What I learned

  • Making the model quote its source did more for trust than any prompt wording. A verdict you can check in five seconds is one you can actually use.
  • Order matters more than filters. Trials that miss a check go lower on the list instead of disappearing, and some of them came back as relevant once we knew more about her.
  • Medical records are messy. Real eligibility text uses a dozen ways to write "Inclusion Criteria", and the parser had to learn each one from real records.
  • A second model is worth it where a mistake costs the most, and nowhere else. Its disagreements were the most useful thing for me to review.
  • Writing a design doc first, then a build spec, then a phased plan made the AI-assisted work far easier to steer than prompting as I went.
