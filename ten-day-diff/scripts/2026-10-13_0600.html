Muse Wonder, Torvalds' Claude Code patch, and Xandr fined

Oct 13, 2026 · covers Oct 3 to Oct 12

This is The Ten-Day Diff for Tuesday, October 13, covering October 3 through October 12. Most of what's new this time landed over the weekend and on Monday, so the picks lean late in the window.

Technical - AI/software

Meta released Muse Wonder and Muse Wonder Full on October 12. It's the first model from Meta Superintelligence Labs that Meta calls frontier class. Meta's model card puts both close to the top closed models on general benchmarks, and claims a lead on MemoryBench, a test of memory and continual learning. The catch is access. Only the smaller Muse Wonder is on the Meta Model API, at $4 per million input tokens and $20 per million output. Wonder Full stays inside the Muse Code subscription. For you, that makes it a fourth vendor worth a bake-off on long-running agent work, since "active memory" is the whole pitch. Just don't plan an integration around Full.

On October 11, Phoronix reported that Linus Torvalds has a branch in his own tree, named noref-filemap-read-with-llm, that changes how page-cache reads work. Reads no longer take folio references, which cuts the contention you get when many threads do small reads from one file. His cover letter says Claude Code wrote the code, the test programs, a review pass, and the first draft of the letter itself, under his direction, and that he ran the benchmarks himself. Coverage puts small-read throughput at up to three times faster in his tests. It isn't merged, and it may or may not make the 7.4 merge window. What matters here is less the speedup than who signed it. That's a strong data point for the "directed agent, human owns the result" way of working.

Technical - Materials science

From UCLA and Auburn, in Nature Communications on October 12: the team measured surface acoustic waves in thin flakes of NiPS3, a 2D antiferromagnetic semiconductor, using Brillouin light scattering. A single Rayleigh surface mode slows down noticeably as the material crosses its Néel temperature. They trace that to magnetic order changing the elastic stiffness, and finite-element models reproduce it. It's a clean, non-contact way to read magnetoelastic coupling in a van der Waals magnet, and it points toward strain-based spintronics and phononic devices you could tune with a magnetic field.

On October 9, a group led by Nanjing Normal University, with Tsinghua and A*STAR, published a photodetector whose response time you can set with bias voltage, anywhere from 614 nanoseconds to 8.28 seconds. That's about seven orders of magnitude. At low bias it behaves as a fast detector. At higher bias the transport shifts from tunneling to trap-assisted, and the slow response acts as a physical reservoir for in-sensor neuromorphic computing. It covers visible through mid-infrared. One device doing both fast imaging and on-sensor processing is an interesting edge-AI story.

And from the University of Birmingham, announced October 7 in Angewandte Chemie: a rare-earth metal-organic framework called UoB-116 that holds up to 16 different metals in one crystal structure, a record for a MOF. Shifting the ratio of dysprosium to lanthanum moves magnetic response, surface area, and CO2 uptake, which falls from 5.72 to 1.23 millimoles per gram as you go from all dysprosium to all lanthanum. The idea is a programmable porous material. You tune the recipe instead of discovering a new framework each time, which is very much a design-of-experiments problem.

DIY electronics

CNX Software reviewed the PlugMate and PlugClaw on October 12. These are thumb-sized Android computers on a MediaTek Helio G80 with 128 GB of encrypted storage, about $199 and $149 on sale. You plug one into a phone or PC and it runs an isolated Android with its own virtual identifiers. PlugClaw adds an Ubuntu VM running the OpenClaw agent. The verdict was mixed. The agent worked but slowly, it got stuck on a Cloudflare wall, the units run hot, and the security patch level is from August 2025. Worth reading as a reality check on "agent in a dongle" hardware.

Nuvoton's NuMaker-IoT-MA35D0-A2, nicknamed Chili Pro, got written up on October 7. It's a 45 millimeter square $49 board with an MA35D0 chip: two Cortex-A35 cores, a Cortex-M4 for real-time work, 256 MB of DDR3L in the package, gigabit Ethernet, and CAN FD on the headers. It runs Yocto or Buildroot with Linux 6.6, plus TrustZone and secure boot. If you want Linux and a real-time core on one small, cheap industrial board, this is a tidy option.

Data regulation

Norway's data protection authority fined Microsoft's Xandr 92 million kroner, roughly 8.6 million euros, on October 12. It's the regulator's largest fine ever. The finding is that Xandr processed and shared Grindr users' data, including data revealing sexual orientation, through ad auctions without valid consent. Microsoft says the conduct predates its purchase and is reviewing the decision. For anyone moving behavioral or enrichment data through a pipeline, it's another reminder that downstream parties get fined too.

Also on October 12, Thailand's Personal Data Protection Committee issued non-binding guidelines on how its PDPA applies to AI. They cover the full lifecycle, from training data to deployment, and take on prompts and outputs, the duties you have when you use a third-party AI provider, and the hard problem of deletion requests once data is in a trained model. If you build on hosted models for customers in Southeast Asia, the vendor-responsibility section is the one to read.

And California's privacy agency, CalPrivacy, announced its first Delete Week on October 12, built around DROP, the free Delete Request and Opt-out Platform. More than 560,000 Californians have registered. For GTM teams that buy enrichment data from registered brokers, that number is the size of a deletion wave about to work its way through those vendors' records.

Claude for GTM engineering

HCLTech and Anthropic announced a Global Premier Partnership on October 12. HCLTech is setting up a dedicated Anthropic business unit and center of excellence, will build Claude and Claude Code into its AI Force engineering platform, and HCLSoftware will embed Claude in products including BigFix and AppScan. No contract value was disclosed. For GTM, it's one more large integrator selling Claude, Cowork, and Claude Code into enterprises, and that changes who shows up as a partner on your deals.

Also on October 12, Anthropic described new defenses against model distillation, and reports say they cut the data harvested by illicit distillation attacks by 90 to 99 percent. The measures restrict access to reasoning data and use classifiers aimed at networks of fraudulent accounts. If you run high-volume evals, or pipelines that lean on extended thinking output, watch for any behavior change on new or low-history API accounts.
