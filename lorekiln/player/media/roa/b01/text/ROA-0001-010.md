Episode 10: The boy who did not fall

Card in a locked cabinet, undated, in a fine old hand: "Lion skin, very old. Not for loan. It will not burn."

On Wednesday evening, the last at the dig, the cold came down off the hills before the sun was fully gone. Amy Doukas knelt at the mouth of the cave with a tray of potsherds on her knees and a roll of bubble wrap under her arm, and her breath showed white in the floodlight. The finds went to Thessaloniki in the morning. The body in the clay would go with them, strapped to a proper frame.

Stavros Petridis came up from the trench with a crate on his shoulder, his face red as a brick and his arms dry as paper. He set the crate down on the stack and pulled the thermometer from his shirt pocket.

"Thirty-eight point four," he said, reading it. "Going down. I'll be a normal person by Christmas." He laughed before the sentence was out, as he always did.

"Sit down for ten minutes," Amy said.

"I've been sitting down all my life. It's very dull." But he sat on the crate, and fanned himself with his hat, and checked the thermometer again when he thought she wasn't looking.

She wrapped the last sherd, fitted it into its slot and closed the tray. Then she sat on a rock and laced her boots tight against the cold, left and then right, the right always slower, because the prosthetic needed her to watch it. She pulled the second knot firm and stood up.

The lights came up the track at that moment, as if the night had waited for her to be ready.

There were two trucks, and the first was the white pickup with the canvas back. Their headlights went off at the bend below the camp, and they came on in the dark with only the sound of their tyres on the stones. Petrakis looked up from his clipboard by the tents and said, "Who the hell is that, at this hour?" in the voice he kept for permits.

Men climbed down. Amy counted six, and then a seventh by the second cab. They carried rifles the way other men carry tools, low and easy, and they didn't hurry. The guard the university paid stood under the floodlight with his radio in his hand. One of them took the radio from him, put a hand flat on his chest and moved him aside, and nobody said a word.

Two of them walked Petrakis to the tents and pushed him down until he knelt in the dust. He kept hold of the clipboard. Amy saw his knuckles go pale on it.

She didn't run. Her legs wanted to, but something older than her legs told her to stop, so she stopped, in the shadow of the stacked crates, with one hand on the top crate and her breathing slowed to almost nothing. A torch beam swept along the stack and went over her as if she were another box. It came back some minutes later and went over her again.

Stavros stood up from his crate and walked into the floodlight.

He didn't raise his hands. He went across the bright ground with his easy, uneven step, favouring the left knee, and stopped between the rifles and Petrakis, and stood there.

"He's got a survey to finish," Stavros said. "Take me instead, I'm cheaper to feed."

The nearest man hit him in the face with the butt of his rifle. Stavros went down on one knee and got up. The man hit him again, in the stomach this time, and Stavros folded over and straightened, and wiped his mouth, and stood where he had stood. A third blow put him on the ground. He got up from that one too, slowly, the way a man gets up from a chair.

"What's wrong with him?" one of the others said. He had stopped what he was doing to watch. "Why doesn't he stay down?"

Nobody answered him. From the crates Amy could see the blood on Stavros's lip, and the smile above it, and she knew the answer, because he had told the whole crew on the first night, laughing, between two spoonfuls of beans. He couldn't feel it. He had never felt any of it. That was all.

Behind her two men came out of the cave carrying the body.

They had made a stretcher out of tent poles and a tarpaulin, and the clay-sealed shape lay along it, long and wrapped and grey, far too long for any stretcher made for a man. They carried it carefully, which was the worst of it. A third man came behind them shoving crates out of the way, and one of the crates hit Amy in the shoulder and knocked her flat on the rock.

She lay where she fell. Her elbow had opened on the stone, and she felt the warm run of blood down her forearm and the sting that came after it. The man didn't look down. He was watching the body go past.

The tailgate of the first truck dropped with a bang. They slid the stretcher in and lashed it to the rails.

Every measured part of her said stay. Stay down by the tents with Petrakis and the bleeding boy who kept getting up. She had no plan, and her phone had no signal up here. But nobody else would know where the body went, and the men were looking at Stavros, all of them, as if he were a dog that wouldn't die. She told herself she only wanted the licence plate. She would read the plate and get out at the first stop.

She climbed over the tailgate and lay down in the dark beside the stretcher.

The last thing she saw before they closed it was Stavros, upright in the floodlight, blood down his shirt, smiling at Petrakis on the ground. "It's fine," he was saying. "Honestly, Yannis, it's fine. I don't feel it. It's not the same."

Then the tailgate came up, and the truck began to move.

It was black inside the back, and loud, and it smelled of diesel and wet canvas. Amy found a metal rail along the side and took hold of it with the prosthetic, because her left hand was slick with blood. The fingers closed. She couldn't tell how hard they held, only that they held, and she thought of a dashboard at seventeen and the second before the glass, and she made herself think of something else.

She counted instead. A left turn at the bottom of the track, then the asphalt, smoother. She counted seconds in her head and lost them on the potholes and started again. Eleven minutes, more or less. A long right bend. A village, by the sound, and a dog barking.

There was a small window at the front, into the cab, and a green glow from the dashboard came through it. She raised herself on her elbow and looked. Two heads, a cigarette, a road map folded on the dash. Under the map, propped against the windscreen, there was a card with a blue stripe and a photograph of a white building. She knew the card. She had one in her own wallet. It was a gate pass for the Pallene Research Campus.

She lay back down and stared at the dark.

She couldn't shake a picture of the road from a height. The truck was a small box below her, its lights out, crawling along a pale line through the pines toward the canal, and the second truck was a smaller box behind it, and she could see where the line ran and where it ended. It was the cold, she thought, and a week of short sleep. She shut her eyes and the picture stayed.

She put her good hand on the clay to steady herself.

It was warm, as it had been every time she read it in the cave, and then, under her palm, it was warmer. Not warm like a stone that had sat in the sun. Warm like something under a blanket. She told herself the difference was the engine, the exhaust running under the bed of the truck, and she believed it, because there was no other answer that she could write down.

The tyres changed their note. The road went hollow beneath them and rattled, iron and concrete, and through the cab window she saw the lamps of a bridge go by, one and then another, and water below them black as oil. The canal at Nea Potidaia. They were crossing onto Pallene.

Under her palm the clay was warmer than it had been, and getting warmer.

The front wheels came down off the bridge onto the far road. The back wheels followed. And under her hand, inside the clay, something drew a long, slow breath.
