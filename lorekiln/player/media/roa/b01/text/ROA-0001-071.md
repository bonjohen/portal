Episode 71: The name on the stern

Insurance schedule of the Vardis Foundation, 1998, item 1: "Gold ring, dark stone, worn by the founder. Not to be valued."

On Monday at first light, aboard the Anthousa at her winter mooring in Nea Fokea, Nicholas Vardis sat on the edge of his bunk with two fingers on his wrist and counted.

He had done it every morning for fifteen centuries, under other names, in other beds. It was the first thing he did and the last thing he thought about at night, and he had never once told anyone. The ring on his other hand sat heavy and quiet. The cabin smelled of diesel and cold brass, and through the porthole the sky over the harbour was the grey of a gull's back.

Sixty-one. Sixty-two. Then nothing, where a beat should have been, and then sixty-three, as if his heart had stopped to think and gone on.

He sat with his fingers on his wrist until he had counted a full minute. It didn't happen again. He took his hand away and looked at the ring. The ring slowed his years. He had known that a long time. It had never stopped them, and this winter, for the first time, he could hear them going.

He dressed slowly and went up on deck.

The quay at Nea Fokea was a short curve of stone with a harbour office at the end of it, and two fishing boats tied up below it, and nothing else in December. One of the boats had come in while he slept. It was small and blue and old, and the family who owned it were landing what it had brought, which wasn't much. Two crates of grey mullet, half full. A few bream.

The girl was gutting them on an upturned crate at the edge of the quay. She was twelve, Maria, with her hair under a wool hat and her sleeves pushed up, and she did it fast, the knife in and along and the guts over the side to the gulls, and she didn't say a word to anybody. Her brother was hosing down the deck. Through the harbour office window Vardis could see her father at the counter with a pencil, doing sums aloud, fish against diesel, and the sums not coming out.

Vardis knew the family the way he knew the weather here. He wintered in this harbour because of them, though they didn't know it.

On the sixth of November 1978 a stranger had gone into the sea off the point below the old tower, in a storm, and a Nea Fokea fisherman named Vardis had gone in after him. The fisherman didn't come out. The stranger did, and took the drowned man's name for his own, as he had taken names before, with papers made that winter and dated to a birth in Piraeus in 1946. The girl's great-grandfather had been the drowned man's cousin. The yacht was named for someone else, a woman in Constantinople in the year 532 who had looked at the gold in his hand and wouldn't take it.

He came down the gangway. The girl's father had come out of the office, and was waiting for him at the bottom of it.

He was a man of forty with a face the wind had been at all his life, and he held his cap in both hands, which he didn't need to do. Everyone in Nea Fokea knew who Vardis was. The rich old man with their name, whose yacht was longer than their house.

"Mr Vardis," he said. "A word."

It was the diesel. The catch didn't cover it any more, he said, not since the summer, and the bank wouldn't hear him. If he could have enough to see the boat to the spring, he would pay it back by Easter, every euro, with interest if Mr Vardis wanted interest. He said it all quickly, looking at the stones, the way a man says a thing he has said in his head all night.

Vardis wanted to give it to him. He had more money than he could spend in ten lifetimes, and the man was asking for less than the yacht drank in a week. He felt the wanting in his chest like the missed beat.

And he knew what would follow. Whatever he gave, someone near him paid for. It had always been so. He couldn't have explained it to a fisherman on a quay, and he had never once tried to explain it to anybody.

"I'm sorry," Vardis said. "I can't."

The man looked at him. Then he put his cap on and spat on the stones between them, carefully, not on Vardis's shoes, and went back into the office. The girl went on gutting fish. She hadn't looked up, but her knife had stopped for a moment, and started again.

His driver took him down the coast road at nine. Vardis sat in the back with his stick between his knees and watched the shuttered beach towns go by, their tavernas closed and their chairs stacked, with the pine scrub running down behind them to a grey sea. The campus came up on the right between Kalandra and Poseidi, a hundred and forty hectares the university owned and the Foundation had largely paid for, and the guard at the gate lifted the barrier without asking for his pass.

The Foundation kept a room on the second floor of the administration block. It had a desk, two chairs, a safe in the wall and a window that looked down over the staff car park toward the pines. He sat at the desk with his coat still on and took stock, as he had every morning of his life.

The giant had been loose for six weeks. He had walked the plain and broken a gate and wept in a theatre, and for three weeks he had grieved, because the Muse he loved had died in the amphitheatre south of Poseidi a few breaths before he reached her. He lay on the long table in the secure lab on Tuesdays and slept in the faculty hall with his face to the wall.

The girl, Amy Doukas, had a warm right hand where a carbon-fibre one had been a week ago. Vardis had seen it on the quad on Wednesday, and had stood still on the path and watched her flex it in the cold.

And on his desk, in a blue folder, was Sofia Andreou's draft agreement for the giant. A name, a roof, protection. He had pushed it through every committee for a month. The faculty meeting on Friday had approved its terms. He wanted the giant close, on the campus, where an old man could sit by the long table on Tuesdays and watch him heal.

He thought of the bones, when he thought of them at all, as the find. The thing the girl had ridden in with.

He read the draft through. He read it again after lunch, slowly, the way Sofia read.

At half past one there was shouting under the window.

Two men in lab coats stood in the car park beside a small red car and a white van, both talking at once. Vardis watched one of them push the other with both hands, and the second man go backwards into the van's wing mirror, and the mirror snap and hang by its wire. They looked at it. Then they began again.

He watched them on and off all afternoon. They didn't go back to their labs. Other people came out to them and went away again, and the two men stayed by the van as if they had been planted there.

At seven the car park was dark except for its orange lamps. The conservator, Zoe Lambrinou, came down the row toward her car with her keys in her hand, and slowed.

The two men were still shouting. Their voices had gone hoarse. And under the hoarseness Vardis heard a sound he had last heard in the Hippodrome in January 532, when a crowd that had come to watch the races began, quite slowly, to want something else.

It was the sound of a quarrel that meant to grow.

One of the men stepped back to the open boot of the red car. When he turned round he had a tyre iron in his hand.
