Episode 28: How anyone would know

On Wednesday morning the crack along the spine of the clay was still a hairline, and Amy Doukas bent over the long table with a pair of digital calipers and measured it.

It ran from between the shoulder blades halfway down the back, a dark thread in the grey, so fine she had to angle the lamp to see it. She chose a place near the top where the edges were clean and stuck a tiny square of yellow tape beside it, and wrote A on the tape in pencil. Then she set the jaws of the calipers across the crack at point A and waited for the prosthetic fingers to close, and read the number, and wrote it down with the time.

08:15. Spine crack, point A. 0.4 mm.

She photographed it with the scale bar. She would measure it again at noon, and at four, and every time after that until it stopped or she understood it.

Stavros sat at the console beside Lefteris in the pelt, with his feet tucked under the stool, watching the scan come up in slices. He was cool. He kept saying so. He had a paper cup of coffee in one hand and was waving the other at the screen, and at a joke of his own he laughed and the cup went over, all down the front of his T-shirt.

"That's very hot," he said, interested. "I think that's very hot. Is it?"

"It's coffee," Lefteris said. "Yes."

Stavros undid the knot of the forepaws and let the pelt down off his shoulders onto the stool behind him. He pulled the wet T-shirt off over his head and went to his bag for another.

Amy saw his back.

Across both shoulders, where the fur had lain, there were red marks. They weren't bruises. They lay in broad bands, darker at the top of the shoulders and fading toward the shoulder blades, as if the skin there had been in the sun too long.

"What's that?"

He looked over his shoulder, as far as he could, which wasn't far. "What's what?"

"Your shoulders. They're red."

"Oh." He pulled the clean shirt down over them. "That's the bag. I carry my whole life in that bag, it's murder on the strap." He held up the bag to show her, a heavy canvas thing with one broad strap, and grinned. "Mum always said I'd wear a groove in myself."

The bag had one strap, and he wore it on the left. The marks were on both shoulders. Amy looked at the bag, and at him, and he was grinning, and he was cool, as he had been every day since Monday in the pelt.

She let him.

At noon the crack at point A was 0.5. At four it was 0.7, and she drew a small graph in the margin of her notebook and didn't like the way the line went. A crack in drying clay should slow as it goes. This one was getting faster.

That evening she walked out to the old block near the sea with her notebook under her arm and climbed the stairs to Professor Scott's rooms. The sun was going down over the gulf, and his windows were full of it.

He let her in without surprise, as if he had expected her for days. She sat on the hard chair by the window where Stavros had sat on Sunday, and she opened the notebook on her knee and read him her columns.

The clay had been warm in the cave, and warmer on the bridge. The ribs had moved a millimetre in a week. The pelt had brought a boy's fever down by nearly a degree and a half in two minutes. It turned one blow in forty seconds, every trial, to the tenth, and let the rest through. And this morning a crack had opened along the spine that hadn't been there at noon the day before.

She closed the notebook.

"On the first morning," she said, "in the lecture, I asked you how anyone would know how it went. The wind, and where his shadow lay. You said it was the right question. You never answered it."

Scott sat with his hands folded on the head of his black stick. For a long time he didn't say anything, and the light went orange on the wall behind him, on the photograph from 1968.

"I was told it," he said at last. "By the last men who had it from their grandfathers, who had it from theirs. There were still a few of them then, on this coast. Old men who sang it at weddings. I wrote it down, because I could see there would be nobody left to sing it." He looked at her. "I'm a chronicler, Miss Doukas. I have been one for two thousand years, give or take. Somebody has to keep the record, and it turned out to be me."

Amy didn't move.

"And this," he said, lifting the stick a little off the floor, "is the spear that killed Odysseus. His son made the throw. He didn't know who he was throwing at." He set the stick down again. "I took it up in the spring of the year Caesar died, forty-four before Christ. I've had it in my hand ever since."

She found that she had picked up her pen. She wrote down what he said, word for word, in her small careful hand, the way she wrote down everything.

"The clay," she said. "You said it was never meant to be a coffin."

"It wasn't." He turned to the window. "When it was over, the dogs had him. His followers went out on the plain at night and gathered every piece of him they could find, and they wrapped him and sealed him in clay mixed with the ash from their own hearths. They meant to carry him home across the canal, onto his own soil, so that he could be born again. They didn't get him home. They got him as far as the cave." He was quiet a moment. "Somebody else got him the rest of the way, three thousand years late."

She wrote that down too.

She didn't believe a word of it. She sat across from him with the notebook on her knee and tried to decide what he was. Either he was ill, she thought, or he was telling her the truth, and she couldn't measure either. The trouble was that everything he said fitted her numbers better than anything she could check. That was the worst of it. A lie ought to fit worse.

When she stood up to go, he walked her to the door, and held it, and then didn't let it go.

"Miss Doukas. Don't tell Zoe Lambrinou any of this."

"Zoe? Why would I tell Zoe?"

"Don't." His voice was very low. "Not any of it. Please."

"Why not?"

He looked at her, and opened his mouth, and closed it again, and he didn't say why. He only held the door until she went through it.

On the stairs she stopped under the light and looked at her notebook. Halfway down the page, in her own hand, in the column where she kept her measurements, she had written a date. It was the year he had given for taking up the stick, forty-four before Christ, with a spring beside it. She had written it down as if it were a measurement, and underlined it.
