Episode 12: Thinner each morning

At four that afternoon the professor's office on the third floor of the old arts building held eleven students, and four of them sat on the floor. The rain had gone and the sun had come out over Thessaloniki, and the narrow room was warm and smelled of dust and old pipe tobacco. The three sisters stood among the students on the floor, unseen, and nobody's elbow touched them.

The girl stood by the door with her back against the bookcase. Melpomene had expected that. It was the nearest thing the room had to a back row.

The professor sat on the corner of his desk with the black stick across his knees.

"This morning was the giants," he said. "This afternoon is the men who beat them. I want you to notice that none of them ends well."

Clio, beside Melpomene, made a small sound that might have been agreement. She had written all their endings down.

He began with Heracles, and the robe his wife had sent him, which she had been told would keep him faithful. He told how it stuck to the skin and burned, how it went into him like fire into pitch, and how in the end Heracles built his own pyre on Mount Oeta and lay down on it, because the fire was better than the thing he wore.

Melpomene had stood beside a great many pyres. She had stood beside that one. She didn't close her eyes when the professor told it, because she had never closed her eyes at a burning, and she wasn't going to begin in a warm office for a story.

A laugh came from the floor by the window, loud and sudden.

It was a boy, long-legged, folded up with a big bottle of water between his feet. His face was red, though the others in the room had only loosened their collars, and his skin was dry. There was no shine on his forehead at all.

"Sorry," he said, still laughing. "I'd take that over an August afternoon in the trench, that's all. At least he got to burn on purpose."

The others laughed with him, because it was easier than not.

Melpomene didn't laugh. She went closer and looked at him the way she had once looked at kings in the last act, when they still thought the play was a comedy. The heat stood in him like heat in a closed room. It had nowhere to go. A body is meant to weep it out through the skin, and his couldn't, and he sat there pink and cheerful and full of it, and joked about burning.

"That one," she said quietly.

"That one what?" said Urania.

"Nothing yet."

The professor was looking at the boy too, and at the bottle between his feet. He looked a moment longer than a teacher needed to. Then he went on to Odysseus.

He told how Odysseus got home, and how few people knew the way he died. A son by Circe, grown on her island, came to Ithaca looking for his father. There was a fight on the shore, and the son killed a man without knowing who he was, with a spear his mother had given him.

The professor's hand closed on the black stick across his knees. The knuckles came up white. Melpomene saw it, and saw the girl by the bookcase see it, because the girl watched hands. Then he leaned the stick against the desk and folded his arms, as if he had decided to have nothing to do with it.

"His own son," he said. "The story doesn't say whether he knew, at the end. I've always hoped he didn't."

Clio leaned close to Melpomene. "He has never let himself love anyone," she said. "Not once. I've had him on the loom since before the city had walls, and there is no one beside him in any panel."

"I know why," Melpomene said.

Clio waited. Melpomene didn't say any more. She looked at the stick leaning against the desk, black and shining, and she kept what she knew where it was.

He talked about Orpheus after that, who had played his way down into the dark and come out without what he went for, and then the seminar was over. The students got up off the floor in a scrape of bags. The red-faced boy drank half his bottle in one go and nodded to the girl by the door on his way out. The sisters went out with them through the closed door, and Melpomene looked back once. The girl was still standing against the bookcase with an envelope in her good hand, waiting for the room to empty.

That night in the dream space the fire was lower than it had been at dawn.

The three of them sat round it under the long canopy of stars. Clio had her scroll open on her knees. Urania was lying back with her hands behind her head, naming the stars to herself under her breath, a habit she'd had since the first night there were stars to name.

Melpomene didn't lie back. She sat with her hands round her knees and looked into the embers, and she remembered.

After Heracles, after the plain, when the dogs had been at what was left of him and the followers came out with their lamps, she had stood on the hill and watched them search. They went from one lamp to the next, calling to one another, finding him in pieces in the scrub. And she had seen, before any of them came near it, a finger bone lying alone in the dark grass. It moved. It crept a hand's width toward the hills, toward Pallene, the way a root creeps toward water, and then a lamp came near and a man knelt and picked it up and wept over it, and it was still.

She had never told anyone that. She didn't tell them now.

"The girl has to be taught," she said instead.

"She will be," said Clio. "That's why we chose her."

"Taught the way I taught every playwright who ever sat at a table by a candle and didn't know why he was crying. By a sorrow. Understood." Melpomene turned her face from the fire. "Use a tragedy as her guide. If there isn't one ready, then make one."

Urania sat up.

"No," she said.

"You saw her this morning. You heard her. She wants a figure she can write down. A tragedy is the one thing that can't be argued with. Give her a loss she can't measure, and she'll understand."

"She has had a loss," Urania said. Her voice was soft, as it always was, and it didn't bend. "She has a hand that isn't hers. I won't add to it. If we show her anything it will be a kindness, and we'll tell her what it costs, and let her choose."

"A kindness she won't believe in."

"Then I'll make her believe it. I've told you both what I'll give."

They looked at Clio. They always did, in the end. Clio rolled her scroll up slowly, and held it against her ribs in both hands, the way she held it when she was afraid.

"Tomorrow at dusk," she said, "the three of us will show ourselves at the dig. Urania will make her vow to the girl's face, so that the girl has heard it said. We'll show her a kindness first. That's my ruling."

Urania lay back down. Melpomene said nothing. She had lost, and she knew how to lose. She had taught it to a hundred playwrights.

After a while the other two slept, as much as they ever slept, and Melpomene sat on alone by the embers.

She held up her right hand against the glow. She meant only to look at it. The red light came through her palm, faint, the way a lamp shows through a curtain, and she could see the shape of the embers through her own fingers. That morning it had not been so. That morning her hand had been a hand.

She was thinner each morning. She had known it for a long time and not looked. Now she looked, and knew that her sisters had more time than she did, and that she had less than any of them thought.
