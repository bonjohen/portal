Episode 17: Onto his soil

On Wednesday evening, the last at the dig, the three sisters stood on the slope above the camp and watched the sun go down behind the hills. The body was going north to Thessaloniki in the morning, strapped to a frame in a university van, and Urania wanted the girl to see them once more before it went.

"Once," she said. "Only so that she knows we're still here."

"She thinks we were a dream," said Clio.

"Then she'll see the dream again, and wonder."

The air turned cold all at once, and the floodlights came on over the trench with a hum. Below them the girl was kneeling at the mouth of the cave with a tray on her knees, wrapping the last of the sherds. The red-faced boy, Stavros, came up from the trench with a crate on his shoulder and sat down on it and read his thermometer and laughed.

Melpomene saw the trucks before anyone in the camp did. There were two of them, coming up the track from the road, and at the bend below the camp their headlights went out, and they came on in the dark.

"Sisters," she said.

Men climbed down. They carried rifles low and easy, the way other men carry spades. One of them took the guard's radio out of his hand and moved him aside. Two of them walked the survey lead to the tents and pushed him down until he knelt in the dust, and he held on to his clipboard as if it were the last thing he owned.

Then Stavros stood up from his crate and walked into the floodlight.

He didn't raise his hands. He crossed the bright ground with his easy, uneven step, favouring the left knee, and stopped between the rifles and the man on his knees, and stood there.

"He's got a survey to finish," he said. "Take me instead, I'm cheaper to feed."

The nearest man hit him in the face with the butt of his rifle.

Melpomene had watched a thousand heroes. She had stood in the wings of every stage that mattered and at the edge of every field, and she knew what courage looked like from the side. It looked like a man who was afraid and went on anyway. She had seen it in the shake of their knees and the white of their lips, and it was the most beautiful thing she knew, because it cost them so much.

Stavros went down on one knee and got up. The man hit him in the stomach, and he folded over and straightened and wiped his mouth and stood where he had stood. A third blow put him on the ground, and he got up from that one too, slowly, like a man getting up from a chair.

There was no shake in his knees. His lips were bloody, but not white. He was smiling. His courage was perfect, and it cost him nothing he could feel, and Melpomene, who had never wept at a hero, felt her face go wet.

Urania started down the slope.

"I'll split it," she said. "I'll show myself to them, all of me, and they'll drop their guns and run."

Clio caught her by the wrist. "No."

"They're beating a child."

"Your vow is for the hand," Clio said, "and for nothing else. If you spend yourself here, you'll have nothing left for her. We can't. You know we can't." Her other hand held the scroll so hard against her ribs that it bent.

Melpomene wanted it too. She wanted it as much as Urania did, for the boy, and she stood beside her sisters and did nothing, and so did they, and they watched him take the blows.

Behind the crates the girl had gone still.

Melpomene knew that stillness now. The torches went over the girl twice and saw a box. Two men came out of the cave carrying the body between them on a stretcher of tent poles, and a third came after them shoving crates aside, and a crate knocked the girl flat on the rock. She lay where she fell. The men slid the stretcher into the back of the first truck and lashed it down, and the girl got up and climbed over the tailgate after it, into the dark.

"She's gone with him," Urania said.

"With it," said Clio.

Melpomene didn't correct her. She was already going.

She left her sisters on the slope and went down the hill after the truck, faster than a truck, and she was standing on the bridge at Nea Potidaia when its lights came round the last bend. The canal lay black under her. The lamps along the bridge stood in a row, each in its own small fog.

Below the bridge, at the quay on the Pallene side, the white yacht lay where she had seen it tie up a week ago. A door opened on her deck, and an old man came down the gangway alone, in a good coat, with one hand on the rail. He walked to the edge of the quay and stood there in the cold, looking up at the bridge.

Melpomene looked down at him, and felt a weariness come off him like cold off a stone.

She had felt that weariness once before. It had been in Constantinople, long ago, from a man in a crowd. She couldn't say any more about it than that. It was the tiredness of someone who had been walking for a very long time and couldn't sit down.

The wires that ran along the bridge were humming. She hadn't noticed them until then, the power lines on their tall posts, carrying light down onto Pallene. They hummed in the cold, low and steady, and the longer she listened the less it sounded like wires. It sounded like a mother singing to something new, close, over and over, the way a woman sings to a child that hasn't learned yet what singing is.

The truck came onto the bridge.

On the quay the old man lifted his hand and turned the ring on his finger, once, slowly, the way a man winds a watch. Melpomene saw the gold of it in the lamplight. The truck rolled over the bridge above him, its tyres loud on the iron, and the old man turned the ring again.

The front wheels came down off the bridge onto Pallene. The back wheels followed.

A small heat began low in Melpomene's chest, as if someone had opened the door of a warm room close by.

And then, from inside the clay in the back of the truck, she heard her name.

It wasn't shouted. It wasn't even said. It came the way a sleeper says a name, slow and patient, from under a great weight of sleep, and it was her name, and the voice that said it was the one she had missed for three thousand years. She had not heard it since the morning on the plain. She had heard it then, at the end, across the water.

Melpomene stood on the bridge with both hands over her mouth.

The truck didn't stop. Its tail lights went down the road and into the pines on the Pallene side, and grew small, and went out among the trees. The old man on the quay was already climbing back up his gangway. The wires went on humming.

She didn't answer the voice in the clay. She stood on the bridge in the cold and kept her hands over her mouth until the heat in her chest was all that was left of the sound, and she didn't answer, because if she answered him once she would never stop.
