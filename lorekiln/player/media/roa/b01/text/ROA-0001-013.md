Episode 13: His pieces back

At nine the next morning the crew's minibus left Thessaloniki going south down the coast road in a grey drizzle, and the three sisters rode in the back seat, behind the bags. Nobody had asked them to squeeze up. Nobody had needed to.

The girl sat halfway along with her phone held up over the noise of the engine. She was playing the professor's lecture from the year before, the week on the giant's death, and his dry unhurried voice came into the bus as if he were standing in the aisle.

"You have to understand the problem Heracles had," the voice said. "Alcyoneus could not die. Not while he stood on his own soil."

Clio closed her eyes. Urania looked out at the rain. Melpomene didn't look at the girl at all. She looked at the boy driving.

It was the red-faced boy from the seminar. He drove the way she supposed he did everything, cheerfully and a little too fast, with his window down although the morning was cold and the drizzle blew in onto his shoulder. A thermometer was clipped in his shirt pocket like a pen. His hands lay easy on the wheel, and across the knuckles of the right one ran a cut, two days old perhaps, gone pink and swollen at the edges and shiny in the middle. It was going bad. Anyone else would have been nursing that hand. He drummed it on the wheel to the rhythm of the wipers.

"So you don't beat him," he said to the windscreen. "You move him."

"Shh," said the girl.

"And then," said the professor's voice, "the part the books leave out. The dogs got at the body. And his people went out after the dogs, at night, with lamps, and they gathered every piece of him. A finger, a tooth. As if each one mattered."

Melpomene sat very still. She saw the lamps in the scrub again, and a finger bone in the grass.

"I'd want my pieces back," the boy said, and laughed, alone, at a joke nobody else had caught.

He looked at his own hand on the wheel as he laughed, straight at it, and didn't see the cut. Melpomene watched his eyes go over it and on to the road. It was as if the hand were a picture of a hand.

"Look at him," she said.

"I am looking," said Urania.

"He can't feel it. That hand is going bad, and he doesn't know it's there. He is all the way through a tragedy and he hasn't noticed he's in one."

"We're here for the girl," Clio said, with her eyes still closed.

Somewhere past the halfway mark the road came out of the hills, and through the rain they saw the long low line of Pallene lying in the sea. The boy took one hand off the wheel and pointed at it, the cut hand.

"That's mine," he said, "Kalandra, forty minutes from the bridge if the road's dry." He laughed. "My mother will want me home every Sunday, and she won't get me once."

The camp stood on the mainland just north of the canal, on a strip of scrub above the shore. By four in the afternoon the drizzle had stopped, and the tents stood pale against the grey rock, with the long trench under its scaffold and the floodlights on their poles not yet lit.

The survey lead came out of the finds tent with a clipboard, looked at the girl's hand before he looked at her face, and put her on nights. She argued with him, with figures. She'd had them ready since the bus. He went back into his tent, and the figures stayed outside with her.

The boy came past with a box of tools against his chest. His face had gone the dry, even red of a roof tile, though the afternoon was cold and the others were in their coats.

"Nights are good," he told her. "Nights are cool." Two of the crew by the trench laughed, and he laughed with them, and somebody called "Stavros, the levels," and he went off whistling to find them.

Nobody looked at his hands. Melpomene looked, and wished she hadn't.

"Stay by her," Clio said. "All of us. Until dusk."

"I know."

"I mean it, sister."

"I know you do," Melpomene said, and when Clio turned back to the girl, she went down the slope toward the water.

She had not meant to go. Then she was going, and she didn't stop. From the edge of the trench she had seen the bridge over the canal at Nea Potidaia, and the low land beyond it, and her feet had known the way before she let them choose it.

The canal ran where the line had run, the line Heracles had driven him across. In those days there had been no canal, only the narrow neck of the land and the sea close on either side, and a stretch of scrub where his soil ended and other ground began. Men had cut through it since and put water in it, and a road over it on iron, and nobody who crossed it knew they were crossing anything.

Melpomene crossed it. She walked over the bridge in the thin light with the cars going through her, and stepped down off the far end onto Pallene, and stood on his ground.

She had come here before, more times than she could count. She came when there was no one left to watch and nothing left to grieve but this, and she stood where his last step on his own soil had been, and grieved for him. It was the oldest thing she did. It was older than the theatre.

But the place had changed again. Below the bridge, on the Pallene side, the shore had been cut back and faced with stone, and there was a quay now, wet and dark, with iron rings along its edge. A white yacht was coming in to it, slow and careful, with men on her deck holding ropes.

The name on her stern was Anthousa.

Melpomene stood at the top of the steps above the quay and watched her tie up. A gangway came out. A deckhand ran down it to hold it steady, a young man in a blue jersey, and after him came an old man in a good coat, slowly, with one hand on the rail.

The old man stepped safely ashore. In the same moment the deckhand slipped on the wet stones, went down hard on his hand and broke his wrist.

Melpomene heard it break. It was a small sound, like a green stick. The old man stood on the quay with both feet planted, dry and steady, and the deckhand sat on the wet stone beside him, holding his arm and screaming.

The others ran to the boy in the jersey. The old man didn't. He looked down at him with a face she couldn't read, and then he looked away, out over the canal toward the mainland, toward the hills where the camp was, and stood quite still with his hand on his chest.

The deckhand went on screaming. It was a terrible sound, and an honest one. His body was telling him what had been done to it, as loudly as it could, so that he would never do it again.

Melpomene thought of the red-faced boy at the wheel with his cut going bad, drumming it in time with the wipers. He couldn't scream. Nobody had ever given him the sound.

She had come to grieve for one, and she had found two more to grieve for, and the place of his last step lay under the dressed stone of a rich man's landing.

She turned back toward the bridge. The light was going.

Clio was waiting for her halfway across, standing in the traffic with her scroll under her arm. She didn't scold. She didn't ask where Melpomene had been. She had always known where Melpomene went.

"The sun is going down," she said. "And the girl is at the trench."
