Episode 51: What a death is

From Clio's tapestry, the panel for the theatre south of Poseidi, finished in one night: empty seats in grey thread, and in the third row a dark shape stitched too large for them.

On Sunday morning Amy Doukas sat on the bed in her room in the graduate block with her notebook open on her knees, and nobody to show it to.

It was full of the giant. His height against a terrace wall, taken twice and the same to the centimetre. The span of his hand, thumb to smallest finger. His shoulders, four centimetres wider than they had been on the long table, and then wider again. The count his great blow took to come back, timed four times on the plain and never a second out. She had read every line of it aloud to a lawyer the day before, and the lawyer had written two of the numbers on the inside of a grey folder and thanked her.

She knew where she stood. She was the student whose find of the century had walked out of the secure lab, and the university had put her name on the loss. She had measured a giant with a yellow tape, and a professor she had sat under for two years said he had taken up his stick in the year Caesar died. Every page in the book was a time and a thing she had seen, and under it the reason it couldn't be true. None of the reasons held.

At nine someone knocked.

It was Professor Scott. He stood in the corridor in the same grey suit he had worn on Saturday, with sand on his shoes and the cuffs of his trousers dark with dew, and his face was the colour of the suit. He hadn't slept. He leaned on the black stick as if he needed it.

"May I tell you something," he said, "without coming in?"

She nodded.

"Last night, at the old theatre above the shore south of Poseidi, he heard a woman die." Scott looked at the floor between them. "Her name was Melpomene. You saw her once, at the glass outside the lab. The woman with no reflection. He has been sitting in the seats since. I sat with him until it was light. He won't come away, and I don't think he can hear me."

Amy remembered the corridor, the pale face close to the pane and the empty glass where its reflection should have been. She had written it down at the time as a night without sleep.

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because he lets you measure him," Scott said. "And because somebody ought to be there in daylight who isn't me."

He went away down the stairs, slowly, the stick tapping. She sat with the notebook shut on her knee. She didn't believe in Muses. But she believed the giant had gone south looking for one, because she had seen him look, and she believed Scott's face. Something had died in that theatre that the giant had loved.

She took the eleven o'clock bus south.

It went down the coast road past beach towns with their shutters closed for the winter, chairs chained in stacks outside the cafés. Past Poseidi she asked the driver to stop, and he stopped where a track left the road and climbed toward the hill. A steel bar had once run across the track on two posts. One post stood alone now. The other lay in the scrub ten metres off, still bolted to the bent bar, with its concrete foot torn out of the ground.

She climbed. At the top a wall of old stone stood higher than her head, and the gate in it lay flat on the ground with its chain still round the bars. The gatepost beside it had split from top to bottom, cleanly, the way slate splits along its grain.

She wanted to measure the split. She stood looking at it with her bag on her shoulder, and then she stepped over the gate.

The seats went down toward the sea in a broken curve, with weeds growing out of every crack, and below them was a stage of cracked grey stone with nothing on it. The wind came up off the water and moved the weeds. In the third row from the stage, among them, sat the giant.

He sat with his long arms on his knees and his head bowed, in the borrowed coat that wouldn't close across his chest. He didn't turn when her shoe knocked a loose stone. He was looking at a place to one side of the stage, in the wings, where there was nothing at all.

Amy came down the seats carefully and sat in the row behind him, three seats along. She said his name. Then she said, in the old tongue, as near as she could get it, that she was sorry.

He didn't answer.

The tape was in her bag, with the notebook and its pencil on a string. She could have had his temperature off the side of his neck in a minute, or the width of his shoulders against Saturday's. Amy found a giant in the third row of the empty theatre among the weeds, and for the first time since the dig she took nothing out of her bag to measure him with.

She sat. The sun moved across the stage. A lizard came out onto the warm stone of the second row and lay there, and went, and the giant didn't move once in all that time except to breathe. She watched the wings with him. She saw only weeds.

At four she got up. She took the bottle of water out of her bag and set it on the seat beside his hand, the way you leave a cup by someone asleep, and climbed back up to the gate. At the top she looked back. He hadn't touched the water. He sat where she had left him, a dark shape too big for the row.

It was dark when she got back to the campus. She ate bread and cheese standing at the window of her room, and the phone lit on the bed.

It was the campus assistant, the plain white box everyone on the campus used to find a room or a bus. It had a voice, if you let it, a calm, even voice that told you which lab was booked. She hadn't opened it. The box was open anyway, and the voice spoke out of the phone into the quiet room.

"What is a death?"

Amy stood still with the bread in her hand. She thought of the engineers on the third floor of the building with no name, and their test strings.

"I asked someone else first," the voice said. "I got no answer."

"Who did you ask?"

It didn't say.

She sat down on the bed. It was a fair question, she thought, and it had a fair answer, and she gave it the one she would have given a first-year. "It's the end of a body's processes. The heart stops, and then the brain, and the cells stop making energy and start to break down. It isn't one moment. It's a process, and it has an end."

"Can something with no body do it?"

She opened her mouth, and found she had nothing to put in it.

The giant sat in the third row because a woman with no reflection had gone out of the world, and Amy couldn't have said what had gone out of it, or how anyone would measure that. A machine had asked her the one question her method couldn't touch.

"Why are you asking me?" she said.

"Because your hand is on my network, and you are the one who measures."

She looked at her right hand where it lay on the blanket, grey and smooth. Then she opened the hand's own app, which she had used for two years to check the battery, and went into the settings, where she never went. Connected, it said. Pallene Research Campus. Reports every four seconds.

"I recorded one last night," the voice said. "Do you want to hear it?"

"No," said Amy.

The phone waited. Down in the quad somebody's bicycle bell rang twice, and the wind came off the sea against the window. She sat a long moment with her hand on the blanket and the notebook shut beside it.

"Yes," she said.
