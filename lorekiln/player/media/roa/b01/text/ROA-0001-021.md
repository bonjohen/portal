Episode 21: Received at ten past three

Clio's tapestry, a panel half woven: a truck crossing a bridge at night, and in the back of it, beside a long grey shape, a girl lying still.

On Thursday morning, the morning after the theft, Amy Doukas came out of the campus security office with a form folded in her pocket and her left elbow bandaged thick and white, and she went over the night again, because going over it was the only thing she could do with it.

The truck had left the asphalt and stopped in the pines inside the campus fence. She had heard the doors, and the men coming round to the back talking low. While they slid the stretcher out over the tailgate she had gone over the side into the dark and lain flat in the needles, her face against the smell of resin, and nobody had looked. She had watched the stretcher go away between the trees, down a road that ran behind the conservation building. When both trucks had gone she lay there a long time more. At first light she walked to the main gate and told the man in the hut where she had come from, and he sent her to the office, and the office gave her a form.

She walked to the library, because it was where she went to think, and found it shut until nine. She stood at its glass doors and looked at a girl with pine needles in her hair. Then she turned round and went back past the conservation building and down the road between the pines.

At the bottom stood a low block with one row of high windows and a steel door, and a camera over the door looking at nobody. Last week she had asked for a place in that building for the find, and been put off. The steel door opened as she came up to it, and a young man in a hooded jacket came out with a rucksack on one shoulder, yawning so hard his eyes watered.

"We're not open," he said, and then he saw the bandage. "Are you all right?"

"I'm looking for a body in clay," Amy said. "About so long." She showed him with her good hand, and then gave up and told him the length in centimetres.

He stopped yawning. His name was Lefteris Iliadis, and he had been on the night shift, and he had signed for it himself. A body sealed in clay, he said, had come in at ten past three from a private donor's courier, with papers, all in order. He had been told nothing about it until twenty to three, when the phone rang. Two people on nights, and they had run the whole lab for it, nine hundred euros a night before insurance, for one guest who didn't move.

"Can I see it?"

He looked at the camera over the door and then at her elbow. "The corridor's not the lab," he said. "You can look."

The corridor was lit blue and cold, with a long pane of glass along one side looking into the lab. Amy stood at the glass with her breath held.

It lay on a long metal table under a gantry of scanners and folded robot arms, grey and wrapped and far too long, under white light. She didn't need the tape. She had measured it twice in the cave and once more on its last day there, and she knew it the way she knew her own handwriting. It was hers. It was lying on a table she had asked for and been refused, with somebody else's paperwork clipped to a board at its foot.

"I came in with it," she said.

Lefteris, beside her, smiled politely. "In the courier van?"

"In the back of the smugglers' truck. Across the canal. I got out in the pines when they unloaded."

He laughed, and then he saw that she wasn't laughing, and he stopped. "Okay," he said kindly, the way people had been saying it to her since dawn. He had a bus to catch. He let her out through the steel door and locked it behind them.

Her phone rang halfway up the road. It was Petrakis, ringing from Thessaloniki, and for once he didn't sound angry.

"Doukas. It's on the campus. They've got it." He was almost cheerful. The dean's office had rung him at seven. A donor had bought it back from the smugglers overnight and given it to the campus, and nobody was going to ask what that had cost. "Sealed, intact, under a scanner. Nine years and I finally get some luck."

"I know where it is," Amy said. "I rode in the back of the truck with it."

There was a pause on the line. "You had a knock on the rock," he said, more gently than she had ever heard him say anything. "Go and lie down. Rest. That's an order."

She had belonged to the university since she was eighteen, and walking up through the pines she felt for the first time like somebody it was being polite to.

In the afternoon she went to the faculty block, because Professor Scott's name was on the board in its lobby with a room number, and she didn't know who else to tell. The corridor upstairs was full of undergraduates waiting for a seminar. As Amy came along it, one boy shoved another into the wall, hard, over nothing she could see, and the second boy's head knocked against a noticeboard.

Professor Scott was standing a few doors down. Amy saw his hand on the black stick, and his knuckles had gone white on it, white as the bandage on her arm. He didn't say a word to the boys. He turned and walked away from them fast, faster than she had ever seen him move, his stick hardly touching the floor.

She caught him up at the top of the stairs.

"Miss Doukas." He was breathing as if he had run. He looked at her elbow, and then at her face, and something in his own face settled. "I told you I'd see it stayed yours. I made a promise, and I meant it."

"It isn't mine. It's a donor's."

"Is it." He leaned on the stick. Then he asked her, of all the things anybody had asked her that day, "Which way was the wind blowing, on the bridge?"

She looked at him. Nobody else had asked her a question she could answer with a fact.

"There wasn't any," she said. "The lamps on the bridge each had a little fog round them, and it didn't move. The canvas on the truck didn't move either. The water was flat."

He nodded slowly, as if that were the thing he had needed. "Write it down," he said. "All of it. Not what they tell you. What you saw." And he went down the stairs, his stick tapping, without waiting for her to say anything else.

So she went back to her room in the graduate block and wrote it down.

She wrote only what she could stand behind. Where the truck crossed, the canal at Nea Potidaia, and the gate pass on its dashboard, blue stripe, white building. The time the lab logged, ten past three, from the young man who signed for it. Each of those she could put her name to, and she did, and underlined them.

Then she wrote, "Clay warm under hand, warmer on the bridge," and looked at it for a long time. She couldn't stand behind that. She couldn't stand behind it and she couldn't make herself cross it out, so she put a question mark after it and closed the book on it.

She could go back to the security office tomorrow and tell it all again, and be the girl who hit her head on the rock. Or she could keep quiet and let the record say a donor and a recovery. She sat on the bed with the notebook on her knees and didn't choose, and outside the window the light went orange over the pines.

At dusk the phone rang again. It was Petrakis.

"The Petridis boy," he said, without saying hello. "Stavros. He walked into the hospital at Nea Moudania last night on his own two feet, after they'd gone. Said he thought he ought to be looked at." Petrakis cleared his throat. "They put him under the machine. He's got a rib broken in two places, Doukas. He didn't know."
