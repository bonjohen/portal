Episode 74: The right to sell

On Thursday morning, in the saloon of the Anthousa at Nea Fokea, Nicholas Vardis opened the safe behind the chart cabinet and took out the campus deed.

It was a copy. The bursar, Haris Bakalis, had made it for him the week before, forty pages on the Foundation's account, and had handed it over with an apology for the length. Vardis had read it once on the night it came, quickly, the way a donor reads. Now he laid it on the saloon table under the lamp, with a cup of coffee he didn't drink, and read it again the way Sofia Andreou read clauses: slowly, aloud under his breath, one line at a time.

Outside the porthole the girl's family were going out. He heard the small blue boat's engine catch and cough and catch again, and go away across the harbour. He didn't look up.

Most of it was what he expected. The university's title to a hundred and forty hectares between Kalandra and Poseidi. The strip of protected coast it could never build on. The easements, and the old right of way down to the shore. Then, near the back, the conditions of the sale and leaseback in the nineteen-nineties, when the university had sold its own buildings to a bank to pay for new ones and rented them back.

Bakalis had called that part a history lesson, the day he signed the girl's pass. Nobody reads it, he had said. It's all thirty years ago.

Vardis read it twice.

Condition eleven was four lines long. When the leaseback ended, the buildings came back to the university, and the university kept for itself, for ever, the right to sell any of them again, with the ground under it, whenever the council so resolved.

He sat back.

He knew land. He had bought it and lost it under a dozen names, and he knew how the old law ran under the new one like water under a road. The waiver in clause 14.2 gave up the giant's claim to the parcel, and that was bad enough. But a waiver was one thing, and a sale was another. While no sale was open, the ground under the faculty hall would still be the university's and nobody else's, and something older than the university might yet keep its hold on him there. When the council opened a sale, the ground became a thing on a market, to be weighed and priced and handed over. On the parcel, then, the giant would be killable only now and then, whenever a sale was open.

He couldn't have proved that the land worked so. He knew it would, the way he knew the ring would tighten.

He thought of Sofia. She was decent, and exact, and she didn't know this was here. If he gave it to her she would read it aloud in the dean's office, slowly, and the professor would hear it, and the giant might listen to the professor this time. The deal would stop. It would stop for a month, or for ever.

The deed was a weapon against the thing he had spent a month making. And he had always kept what helped him. He had kept the ring since Constantinople.

He folded the copy along its staples and put it back in the safe, under the ring's old insurance papers, the ones from 1998 that said not to be valued. He shut the door and turned the dial. He told nobody.

It might never matter. He told himself that as he drank the cold coffee. Last night, from the top of the goat path, he had watched the giant sit down on the shingle with his face to the sea and stop wanting anything, and a man who wants nothing signs nothing. If Alcyoneus never stood up again, there would be no signing, and the deed could lie in the safe until the paper went brown. Vardis found that he didn't know which he hoped for. He had always known what he hoped for, before.

In the afternoon his driver took him down to the campus, and he sat at the window of the Foundation's room with Sofia's draft on the desk and didn't read it.

At half past three the night guards came on early. There were five or six of them in their blue shirts, and on the paths across the quad below the window they met a crowd of the dig students coming back from a lecture. Vardis couldn't hear what was said through the glass. It was about the giant, he thought, from the way a student kept pointing south toward the shore and a guard kept shaking his head.

Then one of the blue shirts pushed a student in the chest with the flat of his hand.

It went quickly after that. It went the way the car park had gone on Monday, and the library steps on Tuesday, every small thing catching off the last, and in two minutes it was more than shoving.

Stavros Petridis was at the front of it, and stayed there longest.

Vardis knew him by the lion's pelt. The boy wore it with the head pulled up over his own and the forepaws knotted on his chest, and when he spread his arms wide in front of the students the skin swung open, and Vardis saw its inner face, and the broad dark band along it, almost black, with one straight edge like the hem of something else. A baton came down on the boy's shoulder and stopped on the fur as if it had hit stone. Stavros laughed. The next one landed. Vardis saw the fur give under it like any skin, and the boy rock, and not look round. Another came, and another, and he stood there grinning under them with his arms wide, letting them come.

He stood there longer than anyone, as if how long he could bear it proved something.

Vardis watched him and not the fight. And he saw what nobody down there could have seen from the ground. The boy's shoes had split. Both of them, cheap canvas shoes, open along the side at the ball of the foot, and there was something dark on the paving where he had planted his feet. He hadn't looked down once. A boy who had lived twenty years by rules, who had been taught to check his own feet every night because he could never feel what they had done that day, had stopped checking.

Behind the front line something went wrong. A girl went down on the path with her arm under her, and the fight stopped.

Somebody ran for the surgery. The guards stepped back. Stavros went on laughing by himself until he saw the girl's arm. He stood quite still while two of the students lifted her and took her away toward the surgery, and the guards went off the other way, and the quad emptied around him.

Vardis turned the ring on his finger. It was loose and easy, the way it always was afterwards.

When the quad was empty Stavros sat down on the kerb at the edge of the paving, under the Foundation's window. He untied his shoes and took them off, one and then the other, and peeled off his socks.

They were soaked dark red, both of them, from the toes to the heel.

The boy held one up and looked at it. Then he laughed again, a short surprised laugh, as if it were someone else's.
