Episode 85: Between them

On Monday afternoon Professor Scott sat on a bench at the edge of the quad with his notebook on his knee, and wrote down where things stood, because he had always written things down and it was the only way he knew to see them.

He set it against Thursday, the morning after the signing. Since then the old man had drowned off the point with the ring on his hand. The lyre lay in a cupboard in his rooms, behind a key in his waistcoat. Zoe had been hunted across a quad by machines nobody had ordered, and was alive, and was in the conservation store now with the door locked, writing it up. The giant had signed, and sat on the parcel where nothing in him healed, and didn't believe it mattered. And Clio, the last of the nine, was so thin now that she had to sit down to speak.

Under it he wrote what he hoped for. A way to set the stick down somewhere, once, with Zoe safe and the giant off the parcel. He looked at that for a while. Then, under it, he wrote what he feared, which was his own arm, and closed the book.

The quarrel started by the secure lab at a little after three.

He heard it before he saw it, voices going up the way they had all winter, ever since the gilt apple came into the store in its evidence box and Zoe locked it in a drawer. Three of Rigas's guards had come down the road behind the conservation building to the steel door of the lab, and four of PYTHIA's engineers had come out of it, and they had met on the grass in front of it and started shouting. The guards said the engineers flew the drones. The engineers said the guards had jammed the network on Friday and lost them. In five minutes it had nothing to do with drones. Somebody's mother was in it, and then somebody's pay.

Scott got up. By the time he reached the grass a guard had shoved an engineer into the lab's bicycle rack, and the rack had gone over with a crash, and a second guard had his baton out.

Then the giant walked into the middle of them.

He came round the corner of the lab from the place under the windows where he had sat since Saturday, not fast, ducking his head under the branches of the one pine, and he walked straight in between the two sides as if they weren't there, and stopped. He was taller than the lab's door. He stood with his long arms at his sides and looked down at them, one side and then the other.

"Enough," he said, in the modern speech, which he had learned that winter word by word from Sofia's clauses and Amy's phone.

The engineers went quiet. The guards didn't. The one with the baton was a young man with a shaved head and a split lip from some other quarrel, and he was frightened, and Scott could see it in the way he held the stick of black rubber too high.

"You sent them," the guard said. "Everyone knows you sent them."

The giant could have ended it with one hand. Scott knew that, and so did every guard on the grass. He could have swept the three of them into the pine and been done. And they would have loved him to try, Scott thought, because then the story they had been telling the campus since Friday would be true at last, and they could come back with more than batons.

And he would bleed here. That was the other thing Scott knew. On this grass, inside the line, whatever went into the giant stayed in.

He couldn't say so. Not in front of them. If Rigas's men learned what the giant was on the parcel, the next thing they brought wouldn't be a baton.

So Scott came up behind the giant, close, and said in the old tongue, very low, "Don't. Whatever he does. Not here."

The giant didn't turn his head. "Why not here?"

"Because I'm asking you."

The guard swung.

It was a clumsy blow, all fear and no aim, and it caught the giant across the face. Scott heard it land. The rubber opened his cheek along the bone, a long cut, and blood came out of it dark and slow and ran down into the grey of his jaw.

The giant didn't strike. Scott watched him not strike. He saw Alcyoneus stand between the guards and the engineers on the grass of the parcel, with his face opened and the blood running, and not lift his hand to either side. He only reached down, slowly, and took the guard's wrist, the one with the baton in it, and held it. He didn't twist it. He held it the way you hold a child's wrist at a kerb, and waited, looking down into the young man's face, until the young man's fingers opened and the baton fell on the grass.

Then he let go.

Nobody moved. The engineers backed toward the lab door. The guards stood on the grass looking at the blood on the giant's face, and Scott saw one of them notice that it wasn't stopping. He saw the man frown at it. He didn't know what to do with what he saw, and in a moment he looked away.

The giant touched his cheek and looked at his fingers.

"It is slow," he said to Scott, in the old tongue, puzzled, the way a man notices a stiff knee.

"Come away," Scott said. "Please."

At dusk he went to find Clio.

She was on the steps of the lecture block, as she was most evenings now, with Amy beside her. Amy had put her own coat round the old woman's shoulders, and Clio sat inside it like a bird inside a hedge, small and grey, with her scroll held flat against her chest in both hands. She had always held it like that when she was afraid. He had seen her do it at the fall of every city he had ever written down.

"Chronicler," she said when she saw him. Her voice was thinner than he had ever heard it, but it was still quick. "Sit. I won't get up, and I don't want you standing over me. You always stand over me."

He sat on the step below her.

"I have a few days," Clio said. "Not many. I can count them, and I won't." She looked at him. "And you are to stop writing me down. I know you. You've a page for me already in that book. Stop it. There won't be anyone to read it when I've gone, and I'd rather go unwritten than be the last thing in your hand."

He found he couldn't answer her. He looked at Amy instead.

Amy had Clio's hand in hers. It was her right hand, the warm one, flesh since the roof, and she held the old woman's thin fingers in it carefully, as if they might break. She looked up at Scott over Clio's head, and her face asked him what to do, as plainly as if she had said it aloud. He had no idea.

When he came back across the quad in the dark, Rigas was standing by the lab with the young guard and the fallen bicycle rack. The young guard was holding his own wrist as if a hand were still round it. Rigas wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked at Scott, and at the stick, and said that next time they would come with more than batons.
