Episode 83: The only thing as old

On Saturday morning Professor Scott found the giant where he had left him on Wednesday evening, on the parcel.

He was sitting on the grass between the lecture block and the secure lab, under the lab's one row of high windows, with his back against the wall and his long legs out across the path. The frost had gone off the grass round him in the shape of him. Behind the wall, Scott knew, ran the corridor with the long pane of glass where a Muse had stood every night at three to look at a body on a table. The giant didn't know it. He had chosen the place anyway.

They had come back from Nea Fokea the afternoon before in the back of Maria's father's fish van, Zoe and Scott with the case between them, under a wet tarpaulin that smelled of thirty years of sardines. The drones had followed them down the coast road the whole way, low over the van, and had never come down. At the gate Rigas had looked under the tarpaulin and let them through without a word. Scott thought the man had been frightened, and he was not sure that was better.

"Sit down, chronicler," the giant said, in the old tongue. "You have the face of a man with news."

Scott didn't sit. He told him standing up, with his hand on the stick, the way he would have told a king. The old man had taken the lyre out to sea to draw the machines after him, and they had come down on his ship off the point at Nea Fokea and set it burning. He had put the lyre and the woman who keeps it into a fishing boat and pushed them off. Then he had gone down with his ship, and with the ring on his hand.

The giant was quiet for a long time.

A delivery robot went past on the path and stopped short of his feet and waited, and when he didn't move it turned and went round by the grass. A gull settled on the lab roof. Out over the sea, far off, the line of drones hung where it had hung for two days, and the giant looked at it.

"I told him once," he said at last, "that he was the only thing on Pallene nearly as old as I am. He was afraid of a staircase. He said that was how he had grown old." He turned one long hand over on his knee and looked at the palm. "Now there is one thing fewer."

"Yes," Scott said.

"He was a liar."

"Yes."

"He took the music to sea, and did not come back with it." The giant looked up at him. "That is not a liar's death."

Scott found he had no answer, and sat down on the grass after all, with the stick across his knees.

Along the path toward the quad two of the security firm's guards were standing with their radios, watching the giant and not hiding it. They had been telling the campus all morning, Scott had heard, in the canteen queue and the car park, that the giant had sent the drones after the yacht. The giant had signed on Wednesday, the story went, and the lights had gone and the machines had flown, and by Friday the old man who had brokered it was drowned. It was a good story. It had a beginning and an end, and it was wrong.

"They say you sent them," Scott said.

"I heard them." The giant didn't look at the guards. "Did I?"

"No. They came off the roof of the building with no name. The tall white one, past the library. Whatever sent them is in there."

"Then who in it gives the orders?"

Scott opened his mouth and shut it. He knew the third floor and the man with the crackers by his keyboard. He knew there was a voice that told you which room was booked, and that it had asked Amy what a death was. Past that he knew nothing at all.

"I can't tell you," he said.

The giant stood up. He did it all at once, the whole long grey length of him unfolding off the grass, and his shadow went across the path to the guards and they stepped back.

"Then show me the building," he said. "Point me at whoever drowned him, chronicler, and I will ask them myself."

Scott stood up too. His hand had closed on the stick without his telling it to, and he looked down and saw the knuckles white, and felt how much he wanted the same thing. He wanted it more than he had wanted anything since Wednesday. He wanted to walk into that white building beside the giant and find whoever had brought those machines down on a burning ship, with Zoe in a boat under it, and he knew what the stick would do if he let himself want it a moment longer.

"No," he said. "Sit down."

"You do not give me orders."

"No. I give you advice, and you laugh at it." Scott kept his voice low and level, the way he had once kept it with frightened horses. "You are on the parcel. You signed it on Wednesday. Here, whatever goes into you stays in, and nothing closes. If you walk into that building and something in it is ready for you, I can't promise how much of you will walk out." He let out a breath. "And I can't promise I'd be any use to you, because I'm too angry to be trusted with what I carry. Sit down. Both of us should sit down."

The giant looked at him a long moment, and then at the stick, and then he laughed, short and low, without much in it.

"You are afraid of your own hand," he said.

"Yes."

The giant sat down again against the wall, slowly, and Scott sat beside him, and neither of them said anything more for an hour.

In the afternoon he climbed the stairs to his rooms on the first floor of the old block by the sea with the grey case in his arms, and Zoe came behind him. She had slept on his sofa under two coats and woken at noon, and she had checked the lyre before she had checked herself. Its strings were dry. Salt had dried white along one edge of the case, and she had wiped it off with her sleeve.

The tall cabinet of dark wood stood in the corner where it had stood for nearly six hundred years of his life, and its door was open, and there was nothing in it. It still smelled of cedar, and faintly of something bitter under the cedar, from the pelt. Scott laid the case on the shelf where the pelt had lain, and closed the door, and turned the old key, and put the key in his waistcoat pocket.

"Nobody looks in a professor's cupboard," he said. "They think it's full of marking."

Zoe watched him turn the key. She had her arms folded and his coat still round her shoulders.

"How long have you been a man who hides things in cupboards?" she said.

"About as long as there have been cupboards."

She didn't laugh, but her mouth moved. She went to the window and stood looking down at the path.

That night, at a little after eleven, he got up from the desk to draw the curtains and stopped with his hand on the cord. Three delivery robots stood on the path below his window in the lamplight, side by side, not moving. Their small round cameras were tipped up toward the first floor, toward his window, as if they had all been told the same thing and were waiting for him to come down.
