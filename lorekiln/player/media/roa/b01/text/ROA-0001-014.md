Episode 14: Mourners

At dusk the three sisters came up the track from the coast road toward the camp, and Melpomene, walking last, called her grief up over all three of them like a shawl.

It came easily. It always had. It rose out of her the way warmth rises off a stone at evening, and it settled on Clio's shoulders and on Urania's and on her own, dark and heavy, and the three of them were dressed in it. To mortal eyes it would look like black cloth, like the clothes old women wear in the villages when somebody has died. That was what grief looked like to them now. Melpomene had chosen it for that.

"Will she see us?" Urania asked.

"She'll see three women in mourning," Melpomene said. "Nobody else will look. People don't look at mourners."

The floodlights weren't on yet. The camp lay grey in the last light, the tents pale and the trench a dark cut under its scaffold, and the crew were round the supper table under an awning, with a pot of beans and the radio on. The girl stood by the trench with a clipboard. As they came up the track she raised her head and looked straight at them.

Melpomene felt it. Being seen was like stepping into cold water, after so long.

They walked the edge of the trench in single file. Nobody at the supper table looked up. Clio kept her scroll rolled tight, and Urania kept lifting her face to the first stars as if she were checking them against a chart, and the girl watched them come with the clipboard held against her coat.

Urania stopped beside her. She looked down at the girl's right hand, the grey one.

"This is the one," she said, in the Greek they had spoken when Greek was new.

Melpomene stood at Urania's shoulder and looked at the girl's face. It was a polite face, puzzled and kind. It was the face of someone being patient with old women. There was no fear in it anywhere, and no wonder.

"She doesn't believe a word of anything," Melpomene said.

"Then she'll learn." Urania lifted her hand and laid two fingers on the back of the prosthetic, where nobody touched it. "Hear us, child. If you won't heed us, I'll give you this hand back myself, and it will be the last thing I ever do."

The girl looked down at the two fingers on her hand. Then she looked up and said she was sorry for their loss.

She meant it. That was the strange part. She thought they were old women who had buried someone, and she was sorry, and she said so in plain modern Greek, the way she would have said it to anyone. Urania thanked her as if she had said something kind, and Clio inclined her head, and the three of them went back down the track into the dark.

Halfway down, Urania began to count.

"I know how many days I have," she said softly. "I counted them by the fire. If she won't hear us before they run out, I'll have to do it. I'll put the warmth back into that hand with what's left of me, and it will take all of it. I've worked it out. I can feel where it will come from."

"Don't," Clio said.

"I only want it said aloud once."

"You've said it. Don't count it. If you count it you'll start living by the count."

Urania was quiet after that. Melpomene stopped on the track and looked back up at the camp.

The girl was still standing by the trench where they had left her. She had already turned back to her clipboard. She was writing something, a number perhaps, or a context. She had heard every word Urania said, and none of it had gone in. It had run off her like rain off a roof.

"I'm going back," Melpomene said.

"Sister," said Clio.

"Just to the table. Just for a moment."

She went up the track alone and let the shawl fall from her, and walked into the light of the awning unseen. The crew were eating. The red-faced boy was telling a story about his mother and a bucket, and laughing before he got to the end of it. The girl came and sat at the end of the bench with a bowl of beans and her clipboard beside it, and ate with her left hand.

Melpomene stood behind her.

She knew what the girl needed. She needed to feel it, not only to hear it. A woman had just stood in front of her and offered her life for a hand, and the girl had said she was sorry for her loss. If she could feel the weight of that for one moment, only the weight, it would be enough to start. Melpomene had done it to a thousand people in a thousand theatres. She had leaned over them in the dark and let her grief go into them, and they had wept for a king who had never lived, and gone home understanding something they hadn't understood when they came in.

She put her hands on the girl's shoulders, and reached for it.

Nothing came.

She reached again. It was like putting a bucket down a well and hearing it knock against dry stone. The grief was there in her, all of it, as it always was, but the part of it she could give away was gone. She had spent it on the track, on the shawl, and it hadn't come back. It was as if the gift had to fill again, like a cistern after a dry summer, before she could draw on it a second time.

The girl ate her beans. She looked up once, toward the dark track, and frowned a little, and then she shook her head and turned the page on her clipboard. By the time she had finished eating, Melpomene could see it in her face. The three women had become something she half remembered, the way you half remember a dream at noon.

Melpomene took her hands away.

She stayed. Her sisters went back to the fire, and she didn't. She sat on a crate by the finds tent through the evening, while the floodlights came on with a crack and a hum and the girl worked alone at the register inside, and she watched the dark beyond the lights and waited for the cistern to fill. It didn't. Not that night.

At two the girl came out with a torch and walked the trench edge. Melpomene followed her. The floodlights ended at the far end of the scaffold, and past them the scrub went dark and the ground rose into a shoulder of grey rock. The torch beam showed the girl's breath, and then the breath blew back into her face, though there was no wind.

The girl held out her good hand, and then she knelt.

Low in the rock face, half hidden by a root, there was a crack the width of three fingers. It was breathing. Melpomene knelt beside her on the cold ground and watched her take a peg from her pocket and push it into the earth, and tie a strip of orange tape to it with the grey hand. The knot slipped twice. The girl stopped, and breathed, and did it again slowly until it held.

Then she sat back on her heels and laughed, quietly, once, at herself.

Melpomene didn't laugh. She had put her face close to the crack, the way she would have put her face to a sleeper's mouth to see if he still breathed. The air that came out of the rock was cold and old and smelled of the inside of the hill.

And under that, faint as a word said in another room, it smelled of pine smoke.

She knew that smoke. She had last breathed it on a hill above the plain on a morning three thousand years ago, with the wind coming in off the gulf and the camp fires burning below. She knelt there with her cheek against the stone, and she didn't move, and she didn't let herself think the thing that the smoke wanted her to think.
