Episode 78: Watched from above

On Wednesday afternoon the dean's staff held the read-through of the giant's final agreement in the faculty hall, and Nicholas Vardis sat in the front row with his good coat buttoned and both hands on his stick.

They had cleared the giant's things out of the hall for it. His blankets were gone from the corner, and the towers of chairs he had slept behind had been unstacked into rows facing the low stage. On the stage, at a trestle table, sat Sofia Andreou with her papers and the dean's assistant with a laptop and a microphone he didn't need. The giant wasn't there. He had stayed down on the shingle all week.

Vardis didn't look round at the room. He didn't need to. He knew who had come by the sound of their chairs. Twenty or so of the faculty and the dean's staff, and at the end of the last row, by the sound of a page turning, the conservator, Zoe Lambrinou, with some report on her knee.

Sofia began to read. Name. Roof. The gold, held in trust. Protection. The lyre, in the conservator's keeping, whenever he liked.

Halfway through the name the doors opened at the back and somebody came in late and sat down at the other end of the conservator's row. Vardis turned his head far enough to see. It was Professor Scott, grey in the face, in the rumpled grey suit, and his trousers were wet to the knee and there was salt drying white on his shoes. He had come straight up the goat path from another night on the shingle. He sat with the black stick across his knees and looked at nobody.

Vardis turned back to the stage.

Fourteen point two, Sofia read, routine, and went on to the costs.

Sofia paused to drink, and the dean's assistant took the chance to lean into his microphone.

"Just to say, from the dean's side," he said, pleasantly. "We're all very grateful for the work. It's been a nuisance, frankly, the whole claim. This tidies it up."

Behind Vardis a chair scraped.

He didn't turn at once. He heard someone stand, at the back, slowly, and heard the room go quiet the way rooms do when they don't yet know why. Then the voice came, low, from the end of the last row, and it was aimed at the back of his head.

"You are buying him a coffin," said Scott, "and calling it a house."

Vardis turned in his chair.

The professor stood at the end of the last row, ten seats from the conservator. He was shaking. It wasn't the cold, though he must have been cold. His whole body shook, the hands and the wet knees, the way a man shakes when he is holding something down with all the strength he has. And he held the black stick in his right hand at arm's length, out to his side, away from his own body. It pointed at the empty wall by the doors. It pointed away from the rows of people, and away from the far end of the row, where the conservator sat with her report on her knee.

Vardis had seen sappers carry powder like that, in the old wars.

Vardis looked at him for a long moment. He looked at the stick, held out so carefully toward nothing, and at the woman it was being held away from. He thought he understood the stick better than anyone in the room but its bearer. He didn't answer.

He couldn't have. He was being accused of buying the giant. He had bought a good deal more than the giant, and the professor didn't know it, and the smaller accusation stood in the air of the hall with the larger one behind it, unspoken. There was nothing to do but sit in his good coat and be accused of the smaller thing.

"Professor," said Sofia, quite gently, "if you have a point about the drafting."

Scott sat down.

The reading went on. Vardis heard none of it. He watched Sofia's green pen move down the pages and thought of the deed in his safe, and of a fuel card statement folded in his coat, which he had not filed, and had not burned.

Afterwards he waited in his seat until the room had mostly gone. His driver came for him and gave him his arm, and they went out together slowly through the doors and onto the steps of the hall.

The professor had gone. Vardis saw his back far off across the quad, going toward the car park. The conservator had been standing at the top of the steps when he came out, and now she came down them behind him with her report under her arm. She passed him on the step below. She didn't stop. She didn't look at him.

"The professor isn't usually wrong about people," she said, as she passed.

She went on down the steps and away across the quad toward her store, small and upright, with her pencil through her hair.

Vardis stood on the step with his driver's hand on his elbow, and he had no answer. It was a long time since that had happened to him.

At dusk he had the driver take him south down the coast road, and stop the car on the verge above the theatre.

He got out alone and stood by the gap in the wall at the top of the goat path. The light was going off the sea. Below him the broken seats went down in their curve to the cracked stage, and below that the path went down the cliff to the shingle, where the long grey shape still sat with its face to the water.

A car came along the road behind him and stopped. Scott got out of it. The professor didn't see him, or chose not to. He went to the top of the path and down it with his stick, slowly, finding the stones. A minute behind him came the girl, Amy Doukas, with a torch, the gauze pale in her hair, picking her way down after him.

Vardis watched them go down. He watched them come out onto the shingle, the old man and the girl, and sit down on the stones on either side of the giant, small beside him. The torch lay between them. Nobody spoke, that he could see.

He had always felt it about those three. He had felt it in the lab, and on the terraces above the plain. The giant, the professor and the girl were watched from above in a way he was not. It was the feeling he had had as a boy in the Hippodrome, of the crowd's eyes on the chariots and never on the stands. Nobody had ever looked at him like that. He had spent his long life making sure of it.

He had always been glad of it. It was how he had lived so long.

Standing at the top of the path in the cold, with the torch burning small on the stones below, he found that he envied them.

The professor isn't usually wrong about people. He still had no answer to it. He stood there until the torch went out.
