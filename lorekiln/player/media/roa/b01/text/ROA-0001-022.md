Episode 22: Forty-one

On Friday afternoon Amy Doukas took the bus north off Pallene, over the canal at Nea Potidaia, and up the coast road to the hospital at Nea Moudania. She sat by the window on the bridge and looked down at the water. It was green in daylight, and ordinary, and there were boats on it.

The ward was on the second floor, at the end of a corridor that smelled of floor polish and oranges. Professor Scott was already there.

He sat on a plastic chair at the foot of the bed with his black stick laid across his knees, his grey suit creased at the elbows, and he was watching the boy in the bed. Amy knew the look. In the seminar in Thessaloniki, on the first afternoon, when Stavros had joked about his bottle of water, Scott had looked at him a moment longer than anyone expected. He was looking at him that way now, and he had been doing it for some time.

Stavros Petridis lay on top of the sheet in a hospital gown, under a fan clamped to the bedrail. There were ice packs in towels at his neck and in his groin, and his face was the dry dark red of a brick in the sun. His arms on the sheet were as dry as paper, and the cut across his right knuckles, which had been going bad for a week, was under a clean dressing at last. He was laughing at the nurse.

"She says I'm her worst patient," he told Amy, as if they'd been talking all morning. "I said I'm her coolest. She didn't laugh. Nobody laughs at ice jokes in here."

"Hello, Stavros," Amy said.

"Amy came! Professor, she came. I told you somebody would." He tried to sit up and the nurse put two fingers on his chest and he lay down again, grinning.

The chart hung on a clipboard at the foot of the bed, beside Scott's knee. Amy didn't ask. She lifted it off its hook with her left hand and read it, the way she would have read a site log.

There was a rib, the seventh on the left, broken in two places. Under it someone had written that the patient was unaware of the injury, and someone else had underlined unaware. There was the fever, forty-one, and below the number a line of times, every half hour since Thursday morning, and every one was forty-one or forty point eight. He couldn't sweat it out. The ice was doing it for him, slowly, and not well. On the second page was a note about the left knee, from a consultant in Thessaloniki, a short paragraph that ended "Previous recommendations stand." Below it, in other hands and other years, the same consultant had written the same three words, and nothing else.

She hung the chart back on its hook.

"Are they taking the knee off?" Stavros said cheerfully. "They keep looking at it as if they'd like to."

"Nobody's taking anything off."

"Good. I'm used to it."

She pulled up the other chair. She had come for something, and she wasn't going to pretend she hadn't. "The police took my statement this morning," she said. "Two of them, from Nea Moudania, in the security office. They wanted to know if anyone saw me get into the truck."

"Did anyone?"

"I hoped you did."

Stavros thought about it honestly. She watched him do it. He looked up at the fan going round and his lips moved, as if he were going back over the night step by step.

"No," he said. "Sorry. I was busy smiling at Yannis. Somebody had to, he was having a terrible evening." He turned his head on the pillow and looked at her, and his grin went soft. "But I'll say I did, if you like. I'll tell them I saw you climb in, cool as anything. I'm very believable. Look at me."

Amy looked at him, lying in the fan's wind with ice on his neck, offering. It was the only help anybody had offered her since dawn on Thursday. She wanted it so badly that she had to look at the floor.

"No," she said.

"Why not? It's true. You did."

"Because you didn't see it." She made herself look at him again. "A witness who lies is worse than none. They'd find out. Then they'd have a reason not to believe either of us, and I'd rather have the truth in the record and nobody believing it."

He studied her for a moment. Then he laughed, delighted, as if she'd told him a good joke. "You're very strange," he said. "I like it."

At the foot of the bed Scott hadn't moved.

"How's Irini's wrist?" Stavros said. "The first-year. I grabbed her too hard at the dig, last week, getting her out of the mud. Petrakis made me write it down."

"Healing, I think. She was on the crew till Wednesday."

"Good." He seemed to mean it more than anything he'd said. Then, "And your body? The big one in the clay? Is that healing too?"

"It's on the campus. On a table."

"Well, there you are. We're both in bed." He laughed at himself, alone, louder than the joke. "Both under machines. Both terrible patients."

The nurse came back with the thermometer. She put it in his ear and waited and read it and wrote it down, and Amy read it upside down. Forty-one. It hadn't moved. Stavros watched the nurse's face while she read it, and his grin stayed exactly where it was, as if it had been put there and he couldn't feel whether it had slipped.

They stayed an hour. Stavros talked about water polo, and about his mother, who was coming on the evening bus from Kalandra with a cool box, and about a cat on the hospital roof he could see from the window if he lay at an angle. He didn't talk about the rifles. When his eyes started to close in the middle of a sentence, Amy stood up, and Scott stood up after her.

In the corridor Scott stopped the nurse at her desk.

"How long can a body hold forty-one?" he said.

The nurse looked up at him, an old man with a stick, and decided to tell him the truth. "Not long," she said. "Days at the very most, if we're lucky. Hours, if the ice stops working. Most people sweat. He can't. So everything inside him is cooking a little, all the time, and he lies there joking about it."

Scott said nothing. He stood a long moment by the desk with his hand on the stick, and Amy saw it tighten until the knuckles stood out, and then let go.

They went down the stairs together and out into the low evening sun. The bus to Pallene stood at the stop across the road with its engine running.

On the hospital steps Scott stopped and looked back up at the second-floor windows.

"There's a thing I've kept a long time," he said. He said it quietly, more to the windows than to her. "It would help him. I think it would. It's been in a cupboard for longer than you'd believe, and I've never once known who I was keeping it for."

"What is it?"

He didn't answer. He went down the last step and across the road with his stick tapping, and climbed onto the Pallene bus, and didn't look round. Amy stood on the steps and let it go without her.
