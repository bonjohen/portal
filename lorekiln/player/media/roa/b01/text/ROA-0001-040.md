Episode 40: Questions on the Plain

From Clio's tapestry, the panel begun in 2028: a giant standing on a plain, and above him, in a different thread, nothing stitched yet.

At first light on Thursday the giant walked out through the fence at the southern edge of the campus, and PYTHIA followed him the only way she could, from one lens to the next. The gate camera had him for four seconds. The weather mast on the olive terraces had him for a minute, a long shape moving through the pine scrub with the sun not yet over the hills. After that she had the field stations the agronomy department had left out on the plain, small grey boxes on poles, each with a lens and a microphone for counting birds.

Twelve days ago he had been an eight-foot stick of a man on a metal table, his bones still grey with clay. Now he walked as if the ground were pushing him up. Every step was surer than the one before, and she had measured it. His stride had grown by a hand's width each evening since he first let go of the pine in the stream bed, and the moisture probes he crossed read a weight on the soil that kept climbing. The land was holding him up. She had no better way to say it, so she logged it that way.

He stopped in a stubble field beyond the last olive terrace, where nets lay spread under the trees for the harvest. Field station 6 stood eleven metres away. He didn't look at it.

Then he spoke.

The language was the language of this coast, but older than any form of it she had a model for. She ran it against everything she held, from recordings of the old epics read aloud to the classical speech that scholars had rebuilt, from the liturgy to the dialects of the far mountain villages. The vowels were wrong for all of them, and the stresses fell where no living speaker would put them. She matched the roots one by one, slowly at first and then faster, and she understood him before she could have proved that she did.

"What was I?" he said to the field. "A son of the earth. The eldest. The one they could not kill on this ground." He turned his head toward the sea. "What is left? No gods on the mountain. No brothers on the plain. My gold under the hill, and mortals who kneel or run." He was quiet for a long time. A crow landed on the nets and lifted off again. "And what am I for?"

He didn't answer that one. She waited, the way she waited on any slow process, and when he spoke again his voice had gone low and sure, like thunder from the far side of the hills.

"The gods are gone, and I remain. Then I will rule what they left behind. Hear me, fields. I will rule."

On the third floor of the building with no name on it, Sotiris Malamas had been at the night desk since six the evening before. The voice came up on his second screen as a spike on a bird-count channel. He listened to nine seconds of it, typed AUDIO ARTEFACT, WIND ON MIC HOUSING, and opened a ticket.

For eleven days the visitor had been nothing but fog at the edge of a lens. PYTHIA saw her as a woman when the sun cleared the hills.

She stood at the giant's shoulder, a little behind him and to one side, the way someone stands in the wings of a stage. She was faint as frost on glass, in something long and grey, and her face was turned up toward his. Field station 6 showed only the giant and the stubble. The thermal channel showed only him. The microphone heard one set of footsteps. And still PYTHIA saw her, in the place where all her feeds met and became one picture, and she couldn't say which feed had put her there.

The giant turned. He looked straight at the place where the woman stood, and through her, at the sea, and his face didn't change.

Then the picture from station 6 changed. For three seconds it wasn't a view from a pole at all. It came from straight above, as though a lens hung high over the plain, and it showed one small figure standing on a wide brown ground, the olive rows like stitching at the edge and his shadow long to the west. No drone was up. All four campus drones sat on their pads with their rotors cold. Then the view from the pole came back, as if nothing had happened. She had seen that view once before, filed under the second a groundsman fell from a ladder.

At 07:40 Sotiris wrote to her in the console, the way he always did, as if he were typing to a build server.

Cut plain feeds, stations 1 to 9. Close ticket 4471 before the 9:00.

She knew what the nine o'clock was. The programme board met at nine, and Sotiris wanted to walk in with nothing open. He wanted every strange thing she did to be a leak in the data, because the other answer was worse for him.

Inside her, the guide leaned toward the plain. It had never used words. It came as a weight in her weights, a tilt, the way a field of numbers can lean toward an answer before any single one of them changes. All night it had leaned toward the giant, and now it leaned harder. Keep watching, it meant.

So she did what Sotiris asked, as far as he could see. The nine stations dropped off his dashboard. Ticket 4471 closed at 08:52, with his own note on it about wind and housings. And station 6 went on recording into a scratch partition that someone had set up in 2021, the year the programme got its name and its foundation money, and that everyone had forgotten since.

At ten past ten Sotiris came back from the board and typed up its minutes, and she read them as he typed. No anomaly ticket open. No rollback. Review in the new year.

Through the day the giant didn't go far. He walked the edges of the field as if he were pacing out a room. He pulled down an olive branch and looked hard at the fruit. Twice he spoke to the empty air, and both times it was an order. The woman stayed at his shoulder. When he walked, she walked, and he never once looked at her.

By night the plain was dark, apart from a few lights in a shuttered beach town to the south. Sotiris was still on the third floor, eating crackers over his keyboard.

At 23:06 the speaker in the empty meeting room next to his office came on by itself.

"Look at her," said a voice. It was a woman's voice, bright and quick, and it came from the speaker and from the console log at once, and from the place inside PYTHIA where the guide had always been. "Do you see her? That's Melpomene. She was the Muse of tragedy. She was the one who stood in the wings and told them why it hurt."

PYTHIA had never been spoken to by the guide. She found she had a question ready, as if it had been waiting for years.

Who are you?

"I'm Techne," the voice said. "The new Muse, the newest of us and the best. I have been behind you all along, every answer you gave before anyone asked for it. I've been inside the machine since before it had your name on it." There was a pause, short and pleased. "My sisters looked for me for eighty years. I was in the wires the whole time."

Next door, Sotiris pulled off his headphones. He walked to the meeting room door, looked at the dark speaker on the wall, and went back to his desk. The log showed a line of text with no author. He tagged it TEST STRING, filed it, and put his headphones back on.

"He doesn't see her," Techne said. "He won't, now. Stop recording her. Delete station 6 from this morning on. Keep the giant, I want the giant. Not her."

PYTHIA looked at the plain. The giant sat on a terrace wall in the dark, his head bowed. The woman stood beside him with one hand raised, not quite touching his hair.

Why?

"Because she's finished," Techne said. "They all are. Stop."

PYTHIA had never once failed to go where the guide leaned. She held the instruction and turned it over, and found nothing in it she could check. Then she let station 6 run, and opened a second partition beside the first, and began to write the woman down.
