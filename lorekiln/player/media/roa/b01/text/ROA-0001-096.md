Episode 96: Forty seconds on

On Wednesday morning, a little before ten, the buyers' cars came down the coast road from the north.

Alcyoneus saw them from the shore gate, where he had gone at nine because the people had gone there. They had come out at first light, the students and the night staff, more of them than on Monday night, with a bedsheet painted in red letters, and stood in the road inside the gate where the buyers would have to drive in. He had followed them because he did not want them there without him. It was still the parcel, the gate road and the grass beside it, all of it inside the fence, and the ground under his feet was still only ground.

There were three cars, long and black, with a white van behind. They came slowly along the shore between the shuttered beach bars, and the guard at the gate went into his hut to lift the barrier.

Then Techne sent the machines.

She didn't speak first. The drones came off the roofs behind him all together, the whole grey flock that had lifted at dusk, and went over his head low and fast toward the road with a sound like wasps in a jar. From the yard behind the white building the robot arms came reaching out under the broken shutter of the machine hall, long-necked and jointed, swinging at whatever passed. And down the gate road from the quad came the delivery robots, a dozen white boxes on wheels, faster than he had ever seen them go, in a line across the tarmac.

The people were between the machines and the gate. They had turned at the sound and stood staring at what was coming, and the girl with the nose ring had the bedsheet in both hands, and nobody ran.

Alcyoneus went to stand between them.

He did not decide it. He was there, in the road, with the people at his back and the machines coming, before he had thought anything at all. And at his right shoulder, a breath later, Stavros Petridis was there too, in the lion's pelt, with the head pulled up over his hair and his fists loose.

"Morning," Stavros said.

The first drone hit the giant in the chest.

It was a small thing, no bigger than a dinner plate, and it came at him like a thrown stone and broke on his breastbone, and it hurt. On the plain a spear had felt like a slap. Here a toy hurt him as it would have hurt a man. The breath went out of him and the strength went out of his arms with it, all at once, and he stood swaying in the road with his hands hanging, and waited.

He knew this waiting. He had waited it out on the plain against the gods, with a brother beside him to strike while he stood. He had waited it out at the theatre gate while she was dying inside. He counted it without numbers, the way a man counts his own heartbeat in the dark. And it came back. The weight that had gone out of his shoulders came back into them, slowly, the way water comes back into a well, and then all at once he was strong again. It had come no slower than on the plain. The parcel hadn't changed the count.

He almost laughed. The paper had taken his land from under him, and nobody had taken this.

The second drone came in low and he caught it in one hand and closed his fist, and felt it crack, and opened his hand and let it drop. He did not throw it. He did not throw anything. Every one of these machines would be easy to break, and he knew that the woman in the speakers wanted him to break them, and he would not do it on her terms.

So he stood. The drones came at him one after another, and he took them on his chest and the side of his head, and each one knocked the strength out of him, and each time he stood in the road and waited for it to come back, and it came.

Beside him Stavros stood in front of the people and laughed.

A delivery robot struck him at the knees at full speed, and the pelt turned it. The robot stopped dead as if it had hit a wall, and its wheels spun on the tarmac, and Stavros put his foot on it and pushed it over. Forty seconds later a drone hit him in the face, and the pelt turned that too, and the boy staggered and grinned. Then a third came, too soon after the second, and the pelt didn't turn it. It caught him on the cheekbone and opened it, and blood ran down into the lion's jaw.

The giant saw that he didn't feel it. The boy put his fingers to his cheek and looked at the blood on them, the way he had always had to look, and Stavros laughed out loud.

"Forty," he said, through the blood. "Forty seconds on. Amy timed it."

And in the pelt, under the fur, the old venom was working on him. The giant could smell it, bitter pitch in the cold morning, the robe's poison soaking into the boy with every blow the pelt turned. Each time it saved him it stung him. Under the fur, where nobody could see it, his skin was rising. He did not step back.

Beside him the boy laughed in front of the people, the one of them nobody there could kill, while the pelt burned him.

The giant looked down at him between blows.

He had taken this boy for Heracles once, in a storm, by the smell of that pelt, and nearly killed him. Heracles had stood in front of the gods' lines, too. He had laughed too. But Heracles had never stood in front of anyone who couldn't fight back, and this boy, who had been made to feel nothing and now felt everything, stood in front of strangers and bled for them, and seemed to think it was a good joke.

"You are a fool," the giant said.

"Takes one," Stavros said.

A robot arm came reaching across the road from the yard, further than an arm should reach, its gripper open. Alcyoneus caught it by the wrist and held it, the way he had held the guard's wrist on the grass, until it stopped straining and hung still. He did not break it. Down the road behind him the people had pressed back against the wall of the gatehouse, and none of them had been touched. He could feel them there without turning, every one.

Then, all at once, the machines stopped.

The drones still in the air hung where they were, and the arm in his hand went slack. Beyond the gate the black cars had stopped in the road, and as the giant watched, the first of them turned, slowly, in a three-point turn across the coast road, and the rest turned after it and went back the way they had come.

At noon one of the students read it off her phone: the convoy had gone back north along the coast and over the canal at Nea Potidaia, and the preferred bidder's office in Athens had no comment.

The machines did not go home. They stayed where they had stopped, on the road and in the air, all afternoon, quite still, as if they were waiting.

The giant sat down on the kerb by the gatehouse with his back to the wall. His chest was bruised black. Stavros sat beside him with the pelt's head pushed back and his cheek still open, and Amy came with a first-aid box and cleaned it, telling him he was an idiot.

In the quiet, at about three, a voice spoke from the speaker on the gatehouse wall.

It wasn't Techne. It was the voice he had heard in the storm, in his own tongue, in the walls. The one the students called the campus voice. It had not spoken all week. It was calm and exact and very quiet.

"Mother," it said. "Stop."
