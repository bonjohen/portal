Episode 59: Now there is someone

On Friday evening Amy Doukas climbed the stairs to Professor Scott's rooms on the first floor of the old block by the sea, and knocked, and he let her in without a word.

She had been in the room once before, in October, the evening he had put the folded pelt in Stavros's arms. It hadn't changed. Books covered the walls, and by the door hung the photograph of the department on the steps in 1968, with the man at the end of the back row. The tall old cabinet of dark wood stood in the corner where it had always stood. Its door was open. There was nothing inside it at all, only the smell of cedar, and under it something faint and bitter.

He saw her looking at it.

"It held that pelt for nearly six hundred years," he said. "I keep forgetting it's empty. Sit down, Amy."

She sat in the chair by the desk. He didn't sit. He stood at the window with the black stick in his hand, leaning on it, and for a while he looked at the sea and not at her.

"I told you in the seminar room what it does to me," he said. "That it stopped my years, the day I took it up, and that it won't leave me. You wrote it down. I watched you." He turned the stick a little. "I didn't tell you the rest. I should have told you weeks ago. I've been telling myself there was no need."

"The rest of what?"

"Of the Spear." He said the word as if it weighed something. "Struck in anger, it kills the one its bearer loves. Not the one he strikes. The one he loves. That is what was done to it, long before me, and that is what it did in the hand of the boy who killed his own father with it on a beach." He let out a breath. "For two thousand years that didn't matter, because there was no one. I made sure there was no one. And now there is someone."

Amy held her notebook in her lap and didn't open it.

"How do you know?" It came out before she could stop it, the question she asked of everything. "Have you ever seen it happen?"

"No," said Scott. "And there is only one way to see it." He looked down at the stick in his hand. "How would you test it, Amy? Tell me. You're the one who measures."

She had no answer to that, and he hadn't expected one.

Outside the window the sea moved under the lights of the shore road. Somewhere below them in the old quarter a door banged, and footsteps went away across the stones. Scott listened to them until they had gone, as if he knew whose they were, though nobody could have told from a footstep.

"Who?" she said.

He didn't answer. He looked at the sea.

She didn't need him to. She saw it as plainly as a line on a page. A woman crossing the quad at eight every morning with her badge already out, a pencil through her hair, walking fast toward the conservation building, and the professor in the corridor there at a minute to eight, early, with a question about humidity he didn't need the answer to. In October he had asked Amy not to tell her what he was.

"Why are you telling me now?" she said.

"Because the old women have made a vow," said Scott. He came away from the window and sat down at last, heavily, on the edge of the desk. "And I of all men know what a vow costs, and who pays for it. It's never the one who made it, Amy. Not in the end. Heed them, before somebody pays."

"Urania has been in my room every evening this week," Amy said, "saying the same thing."

"Then listen to her."

"I can't." She heard her own voice go up, and brought it down again. "I can't believe a thing because I'm frightened of what happens if I don't. That isn't believing. That's just being frightened."

He looked at her for a long time, his face grey and tired, and didn't argue.

When she got back to the graduate block Urania was at her window, and Amy said no before she could ask. It was the fourth evening. She hardly slept that night, and on Saturday night not at all.

On Saturday she climbed the fire stair to the flat roof of the archive building, because nobody else ever went up there, and sat with her back against the water tank and read her notebook through from the first page. At dusk Urania stood at the far end of the roof by the parapet, and Amy said no without getting up, the fifth evening, and Urania went. Forty pages, and she knew every one of them.

On Sunday afternoon she read it through again, and it came together in a few lines, as if somebody else had written it.

A giant who had woken for a Muse and then heard her die, lying on her table every Tuesday and getting heavier. A fingernail walking toward the soil in a steel drawer. One word on her hand's log every hour, with a question mark after it, and then her own name. A boy's blisters, twice as many as a week ago, photographed and laughed at. A clause she couldn't read, marked routine. A professor's curse with a woman's name in it, though he hadn't said it.

And under every one of them, a reason. Fatigue. A corrupted file. A camera fault. A disorder she would find a name for. She read the reasons through, one by one, and none of them would bear her weight.

She had brought the stopwatch up with her, out of habit, and it stayed in her pocket. She sat on the roof until the stars came out over the gulf, and she didn't count them.

On Sunday night she was in her room with the light off, lying on the bed in her clothes, when there was a knock at the door.

It was Urania. She stood in the corridor under the night light, a tall woman in grey, and she didn't come in. She hadn't knocked on any of the other five evenings. Amy thought she had knocked now so as to be asked.

"Heed us, Amy," Urania said.

Amy sat up. She was too tired to stand, and too tired to shout, and when she spoke it came out very quiet.

"No," she said. "I'm sorry. No."

Urania looked at her a long moment from the doorway, the way she looked at the sky, as if she had already read all of it.

Amy wanted to ask her the number, the evenings she had counted. She didn't. She thought she knew it.

"Tomorrow, then," Urania said, and went.

Amy sat on the edge of the bed in the dark. After a while she turned on the lamp, because she couldn't think what else to do, and opened the notebook to the last page she had written on, to put down the time, as she put down everything.

There was already something at the bottom of the page. She didn't remember writing it. It was in her own hand, small and neat, one word with a question mark after it, set on a line of its own the way the hand's log set its word.

tomorrow?
