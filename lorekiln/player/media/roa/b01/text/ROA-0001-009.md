Episode 9: The proper frame

On Wednesday morning the sun was already hot on the canvas when Amy Doukas came out of her tent, and Yannis Petrakis was standing on the spoil heap at the top of the camp with his phone held up at the sky. It was the one place on the site where a call would hold. He listened, said "Yes" four times and "No" once, and came down the heap sideways, the way he came down everything.

"Thessaloniki," he said. "They're sending a van in the morning. Proper frame, proper straps, two people from conservation. The body goes to the department and the crates go with it."

"Good," Amy said. "Then I'll ride with it."

"You'll ride on the bus with the crew."

She looked at him. He had the clipboard against his chest like a shield, and he didn't look back at her.

"I found the fissure," she said. "I took the first measurements, and I'm the only one who has handled it."

"Which is why they want you writing it up, they say, and not in the back of a van holding its hand." He tapped the clipboard. "The dean's office has a senior team for it. A find like this has to have a name on the paper that people already know. That's what they told me."

"Is that what you think?"

Petrakis gave a short laugh with no pleasure in it. "Nine years I've walked this plain. Nine years of potsherds and field walls and one Roman drain, and nobody from the dean's office drove up to look at the drain. Now I have the best thing anyone ever pulled out of this ground, and I'm to sign it over and go home on the bus as well." He squinted at the cave mouth. "So, no. It isn't what I think. But it's what's happening."

He went off toward the trench, calling for somebody to bring the level.

Amy stood for a while with her coffee going cold. It was the find of the century. She had believed it since the first morning and written it down nowhere, because it wasn't the kind of sentence you wrote down. They aren't taking it from me, she thought, and then she thought, plainly, but they are.

She went down into the cave anyway.

It was cooler inside, the air still and mineral. The carved walls ran back into the dark, the giants and the gods locked against each other along the stone, a knee here, a shield there, a face with its mouth open. The body lay where it had lain since she found it, on its shelf of rock in the round chamber, wrapped and sealed in grey clay.

She set up the lamp and took her last measurements, though she had taken them all before. The length, and the depth of the clay at the crown of the head. She held the tape with her left hand and pinned its end with the prosthetic. The fingers closed slowly when she asked them to, and she watched them close, because if she looked away they would stop halfway. The length was the same as on the first day. It was far too long. She wrote the figure down in her notebook in small careful digits, and underlined it, and didn't put her hand on the clay.

She could have. Nobody was there to see. She knew from the first time what it would be like, and she knew there was nothing in her kit that would explain it, so she left her hand on the tape and told herself that a measurement she couldn't repeat was not a measurement. Then she photographed the body from each side, slowly, for a file that would have somebody else's name at the top of it.

By noon the trench was a white glare. Stavros Petridis was in it with a mattock, breaking out the last of the fill by the cave mouth so the van could back up close in the morning. Amy came up the ladder with her camera bag and stopped, because his face had gone a dark, even red, like a tile, and his arms were dry as paper.

"Out," she said.

"Ten more minutes."

"Stavros. Out."

He laughed, and climbed out, and sat on an upturned bucket in the shade of the finds tent. His left knee went stiff as he bent it and he straightened it with both hands, quite calmly, as if it belonged to someone else. He took the thermometer from his shirt pocket, put it under his tongue and read it with his eyes crossed.

"Thirty-eight point nine," he said. "I am a very small oven."

Amy took a water bottle from the crate and poured it slowly over his head and the back of his neck. He shut his eyes and let her. The water ran down his shirt and dried on his shoulders almost while she watched.

"My mother would do the whole bucket," he said. "She'd do the bucket and then she'd sit on me." He laughed again, a loud laugh in the hot quiet, and two of the crew looked up from the sieves and smiled, because he did it all day and they had stopped minding.

There were bruises down both his shins, purple going yellow, and a long graze on one forearm with grit still in it. Amy pointed at the graze.

"When did you do that?"

He turned his arm over and looked at it with interest. "No idea. Yesterday? It's on my list for tonight." He saw her face and his own face went patient. "I check. Every night, feet and hands, elbows and knees, and the temperature twice a day in summer. My mother wrote it all on the inside of my wardrobe door when I was fourteen, and I copied it out again last year." He took the thermometer out again and checked it, and put it back. "I don't feel it, Amy. It's not the same."

"It's the same skin," she said.

"You sound like her." He grinned and tipped his hat over his eyes. "Is it true they're taking the body off you?"

So the whole camp knew. She said it was true.

"That's rotten," Stavros said, from under the hat. "You found it. If it were me I'd lie down in front of the van." He thought about that. "I'd be good at it, too. You could run me over twice."

She didn't laugh, and he laughed for both of them.

In the afternoon the work turned into packing, and the camp got quiet and orderly the way it did at the end of any season. Crates came out of the stores, and the trays with their foam slots. Amy wrapped sherds at the trestle table and wrote numbers on the trays, and her right hand ached at the wrist where the socket sat, an ache she could feel, which was almost a comfort. She thought of the tall woman in black who had touched the back of it on her first evening.

At about five o'clock a car came along the road below the camp.

She heard it before she saw it, because cars on that road were rare, a few farmers and the water truck on Mondays. It was a white pickup with a canvas cover on the back, and at the bend below the track it slowed until it was barely rolling. Through the pines she saw a man in the passenger seat turn his head and look up at the camp. He looked at the tents for a long moment, and then at the dark mouth of the cave. Then the pickup picked up speed and went on round the hill, and the sound of it went with it.

"Who was that?" she said.

Petrakis had seen it too. He shrugged. "Somebody who heard there's treasure up here. Every village from here to the canal has heard by now." He looked at the guard by the floodlight, who was eating a sandwich. "It's one more night. They'll be gone in the morning, and so will we."

Amy went back to the trestle and picked up the next sherd. She wrapped it and fitted it in its slot. Everything she trusted said he was right, that a car was a car and the road was a public road. She wrote the time in the margin of the register anyway, five past five, a white pickup, and the shape of the bend where it had slowed.

The sun went down behind the hills. The air turned, quite suddenly, from warm to cold, and the floodlights came on with a hum. Down on the road, at the bend below the camp, an engine she couldn't see had stopped. She waited for the sound of it starting again, and it didn't come.
