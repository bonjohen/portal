Episode 52: Never free

On Monday evening, when Amy Doukas came back from the library with her notebook under her arm, an old woman was sitting on the bench outside the graduate block with a rolled paper in her lap.

Amy had listened to the recording twice in the night. A woman's voice, thin as a thread pulled out of cloth, asking who she was if nobody was there to share it with, and then calling someone her love and telling him to sit. Then nothing, and the wind on the drone's microphone. She hadn't written any of it down. It was the first thing since the dig she hadn't written down.

She knew the woman on the bench. She had seen her at the dig on her first evening, one of three in black at the edge of the camp, whom she had taken for mourners from the village. This one had held a rolled paper then too.

"My name is Clio," the woman said. Her voice was firm and quick, as if there wasn't time for it to be anything else. "My sister died on Saturday, in a theatre. You've heard her, I think. You had better come and sit with the ones who are left."

"I'm sorry about your sister," said Amy.

"Yes. Come."

She got up and went off along the pine walk without looking back to see if Amy followed. Amy stood a moment with her key in her hand. Then she put it in her pocket and went after her, because an old woman was walking into the dark between the pines alone, and because she wanted to see where she would go.

The pine walk ran from the graduate block to the edge of the campus, where the trees stopped above the protected strip of shore. At the end of it Clio didn't stop. She walked on into the last of the pines, and Amy, three steps behind her, walked through something.

Afterwards, in her notebook, she called it a change in the air pressure. Her ears filled the way they filled in a lift, and cleared. The sound of the sea stopped, and the smell of the pines stayed.

There was no campus. There was a wide dark floor of earth, and over it the sky, a vast canopy of stars, more stars than she had ever seen above Litochoro on the coldest night of the year. In the middle of the floor a fire had burned down to embers. Its red light went out a little way and stopped, and past it she could find no wall and no edge, nothing to set a tape against.

The tallest woman was sitting by the fire. Amy knew her too. At the dig she had glanced at the stars and then touched Amy's prosthetic hand, and made a promise over it that Amy had written down as grief talking.

"This is Urania," said Clio, and sat down on the far side of the embers. "Sit, Amy."

Amy sat. The earth was warm under her. She took out her notebook, because her hands needed something to do, and found her pencil, and began to count.

She counted their breaths first. Clio breathed fourteen times in a minute, which was ordinary for a woman her age who had walked fast. Urania breathed nine times, slowly and deeply. Then Amy looked up and began on the stars, the bright ones in the square of sky above the fire, and wrote the number down, and started again because she had lost her place.

"She is counting us," Clio said to her sister.

"She counts everything," said Urania. Her voice was soft and full of doubt. "She always has. It is how she keeps still."

They told her what they were. Clio did most of the telling. They were the Muses, she said, or what was left of the nine. Some of the others had faded as the world stopped needing them, one by one, over a long time, and on Saturday the Muse of Tragedy had faded in the theatre where she had always gone. Now there were two. Urania was the Muse of the stars, who could chart the whole of the heavens and say where every light in it would be on any night to come. Clio kept the history. She held up the rolled paper and didn't unroll it. It was a scroll, Amy saw, old and soft at the edges.

"We chose you," Clio said. "At the dig, on your first evening. Our sisters who faded left their gifts behind them, and somebody has to carry them. You carry them already. You don't know it."

"What gifts?"

"When the guard's torch went over you in the pines and didn't find you. When the smugglers looked straight at you in the dark and saw nobody. That was Polyhymnia's, and she has been asleep since 1945."

Amy wrote down the year. "Keeping still is a skill," she said. "People see what moves. You can learn it. I learned it at the dig, in the finds tent, at night, when there was nothing else to do." She looked at her page. "And everything here can be explained by fatigue. I slept four hours last night. I haven't eaten since lunch. My ears popped, and I'm sitting by a fire I can't see the edge of, and I'm counting stars because they don't make sense."

"Then count them," said Clio.

The fire ticked. Nobody added a stick to it, and it didn't go out.

"There is an old lyre in the conservation store," Clio said after a while, more slowly. "Under a card that says do not play. It was our sister's. Euterpe's. She sang her last song into it, and part of her is in the wood still, and you will be asked to play it before the end." She leaned forward over the embers, and her eyes in the red light were very hard. "Hear me, Amy. Music drawn through that instrument is never free. The Lyre will want something back for every song."

"Want what?"

Clio didn't say. Her hand had closed tight on the scroll in her lap.

Amy wrote the warning down word for word, and under it, out of habit, a line for the reason it couldn't be true, and left the line empty.

Urania said very little. All the while her sister talked she sat with her long hands in her lap and her face turned not to the stars but to Amy's right hand, the grey one, lying on the open notebook. She looked at it the way Amy had seen her grandmother look at the weather over the mountain, as if it was going to do something and she had worked out when.

"Why are you looking at my hand?" Amy said.

"I made you a promise over it."

"I remember. I don't want it."

"I know," said Urania. "I am waiting for you to want something else."

When Amy looked up again the fire was further off than it had been, and the pines were round her, and the sound of the sea came back all at once, as if a door had opened onto it. She was standing at the end of the pine walk with her notebook in her hand and nobody beside her. The lights of the graduate block were on behind her. It was much later than it should have been.

She walked back. She didn't run.

In her room she looked at the clock, which said three in the morning, and sat down on the bed to take off her shoes. There was grey on the knees of her jeans. She rubbed it with her thumb and it came off soft and fine, and smelled of pine smoke.

Amy took a sample bag out of the box on her desk, and scraped a pinch of the ash into it with the edge of a card, and sealed it. Then she wrote on the label in her smallest capitals, the date, the time, and under them, after a long time, the words source unknown.
