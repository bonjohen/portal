Episode 98: The biggest thing since Heracles

On Thursday morning, at a little after eight, a contractor's crew came in by the shore gate with an armoured excavator on a low-loader and a van full of crates.

Alcyoneus heard it before he saw it, from the grass under the lab windows: a heavy diesel grinding down the coast road, and then the barrier going up, and the guard at the gatehouse saying nothing at all. By the time he had walked round past the library to the yard behind the white building, the low-loader was backed up beside the machine hall and its ramps were down, and the excavator was coming off them.

It was yellow, and it was armoured. Somebody had bolted grey steel plates over its cab and its engine, and caged its windows with mesh, the way men armour a thing they expect to be hit. Its arm was folded under it like the foreleg of a crouching animal, with a toothed bucket at the end as wide as a door. Its tracks bit into the tarmac of the yard as it came off the ramps, and the ground shook a little under the giant's feet.

The crew were six men in orange jackets and hard hats, with a foreman in a white one. They did not look at the giant. They began to carry the crates out of the van, two men to a crate, and the crates had red labels on them, and the men carried them carefully, as if they were full of eggs.

He knew what they were for. He had heard the man on the open line.

The hall's far wall stood on the very edge of the parcel. The old fence came up to it from the pines at one corner and went on again from the other, and the wall itself was the line, with nothing beyond it but the ditch of the coast road and the scrub and the sea. That morning the crew cut a hole in it. They cut it from inside, by the cooling plant, with a saw that screamed for half an hour, a ragged gap as tall as a door, so that they could run their cables out to the road. Through it the giant could see grey daylight and the scrub, and the ditch beyond it full of rainwater.

They carried the crates in through the shutter and stacked them along the cooling plant, and began to open them.

The students came at ten.

Nobody had sent for them. Lefteris had been on the phone since dawn, and the word had gone round the canteen and the halls, and they came down the road in twos and threes, the ones who had slept against him and many he didn't know, some of the night staff still in their lanyards. They walked past the excavator and the crew without stopping, and went in under the broken shutter into the machine hall, and stood among the racks.

"So nobody will dare," the girl with the nose ring said to him as she went past. She didn't stop either.

By noon there were perhaps sixty of them inside, sitting on the floor between the racks with their backs to the warm machines, and the crew had stopped work and stood in the yard looking at the foreman, and the foreman was on his phone.

At one Rigas came down with his guards. He had his orders on a sheet of paper, as he always did. He read them out in the yard in a loud flat voice, so that the students inside would hear. The building was to be cleared by dark. Anyone still inside at dark was trespassing on property under offer, and would be removed.

Then he looked at the giant, and the giant looked back at him, and Rigas looked away first.

The giant stood in the yard all afternoon, between the hall and the excavator.

He did not go inside. He stood with his back to the shutter and his face to the machine, and at about three, when the low sun came round over the white building, he turned and looked back along the campus the way he had come.

In front of him, at the edge of the parcel, the biggest thing he had stood against since Heracles waited for him, yellow and armoured and patient, and everything behind him on the parcel had gone still.

He saw it as if from very high up. The library and the quad were empty, and the paths had no robots on them. On the roofs the drones sat dark in their row. Nothing moved anywhere on the hundred and forty hectares but the wind in the pines and the thin exhaust of the excavator ticking over. It was the stillness he remembered from the plain, from the last morning, when the gods' first lines had fallen back and the whole field had gone quiet, and he had known that whatever came next would be the last of them.

The chronicler came down the road at four, and Zoe Lambrinou came with him, carrying the grey case.

He came slowly, leaning on the black stick, and his face was grey. He stopped beside the giant and looked at the excavator, and at the students sitting inside among the green and amber lights.

"Let them do it," he said, in the old tongue. "Please. Let the humans blow up their own machine. It's theirs. Walk away. Walk out through that hole in the wall, there, onto the road, and stand on your own ground and watch."

"The people are inside it."

"The guards will get them out. That's what Rigas is for."

"Rigas has been trying since one."

Scott was quiet a moment. Then he said, very low, "I can't stop it. I have one throw, and I don't dare. And you can't stop it here without striking, and if you strike, they'll shoot you, and on this ground you'll die." He looked up. "There's nothing for you to do."

"There is something," said the giant. "I can stand."

Amy came out of the hall behind them. She had been inside with the students since noon. She looked at the giant once, and he saw her understand that he would not move, the way she always understood a thing a moment before it was said. She didn't argue. She went past them both, up the yard and out toward the shore gate, where people from the beach towns had begun to gather on the coast road to watch, and he heard her voice go up, clear and steady, telling them to go home and get off the road. She didn't look back at him once.

The foreman came across the yard with a loud-hailer. He stood ten paces off, which was as near as he would come, and lifted it.

"You," he said, and the word boomed off the wall of the hall. "The big one. You're on private property under offer. Get off the parcel now, or you'll be cleared with the rest of them."

The giant looked at the excavator.

He could have folded it like paper. He knew it in his arms. The strength had come back into them on its count after every blow on Wednesday, and it was there now, waiting, and the armour bolted to that cab was nothing to it. One blow, and the thing would lie on its back in the yard with its tracks in the air like a dead beetle.

And then the guards would fire, and the people inside would pay for it, one way or another. Whatever he broke here, they would pay for.

So he stood, and did not do it.

"I am cleared," he said to the foreman, "when they are."

The foreman lowered the loud-hailer and went back to his men.

At dusk the charges were set. The giant watched the crew come out of the hall in a line, unrolling a thin cable behind them across the yard, and the foreman knelt by a grey box at the end of it and checked his watch, and then checked it again. Inside the hall the students had not moved. He could hear them talking among the racks, low, the way people talk in a church.

The foreman's radio crackled. A voice came out of it from the third floor of the white building, crisp and certain, and the giant heard every word.

"The building's clear," it said. "We have it from security. It's empty."

It was not.
