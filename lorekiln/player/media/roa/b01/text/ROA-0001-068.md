Episode 68: Back in his jaw

On Friday morning, before work, Zoe Lambrinou went down the goat path below the old theatre with Professor Scott, because she couldn't stop thinking about the tooth.

The sea was flat and grey. The broken seats above them were wet with dew, and the shingle at the bottom of the cliff was dark where the night tide had been. Scott went first with his stick, slowly, testing every step. He had sat with the giant until two, he said, and slept in his car, and he looked it.

The giant was where they had left him. He sat on the stones with his back to the theatre and his face to the sea, his long arms on his knees, exactly as he had sat the evening before. There was salt in the creases of the coat that wouldn't close across his chest. He hadn't moved.

"Good morning," Zoe said to him.

He said nothing at all. He didn't turn his head.

She went round in front of him, carefully, at a distance, as she would have walked round a crate she wasn't sure of. She wanted to see his mouth. The stone had struck him on the left side, and the tooth had come from there. She had seen the gap last night in the torchlight when he turned to look at Amy, dark and wide, between two long grey teeth.

There was no gap.

The tooth was back in his jaw, whole, as long as her finger and grey and old like its neighbours, sitting in its place as if it had never left. She looked at it for a long time. Then she went to the place by his foot where it had fallen, between two pale stones, and looked there, and along the shingle in both directions as far as the rocks. There was nothing in the stones. There was no blood and no chip of anything, nothing she could have put in a sample bag.

The tooth Alcyoneus had lost was back in his jaw the next morning, and there was nothing in the stones where it had fallen.

She turned to Scott. He was looking at the giant's mouth too.

"On this ground," Scott said, quietly, and didn't finish.

"On this ground what?"

He shook his head. "I've seen it before. That's all." He leaned on his stick and looked at the sea. "Let's go up. He doesn't want us."

Amy came into the store at ten with seven stitches in her scalp and a face the colour of paper.

The campus surgery had kept her overnight. The medic had told her, she said, not to be on her own all day, and had looked at her as if she remembered telling her the same thing after the stairs. So Amy sat on the store's spare stool by the radiator, with a blanket round her shoulders and her notebook on her knees, and wrote nothing in it. Now and then she shut her eyes. Zoe worked at her bench and kept looking up.

At eleven there was shouting on the quad.

Zoe went to the north window. The night guards were finishing their shift, and a few of the dig students were waiting for them at the edge of the paving, the same faces as yesterday, and both sides had started to mutter and point. She watched them come together. Then, for no reason she could see, they stopped. They stood at the edge of the quad in two loose groups, guards and students, muttering, and none of them came any nearer the other.

They stood like that for an hour.

At noon Amy got up to go to the toilet, and the shouting started again at once, loud enough to hear through the glass. Amy came back and sat down on her stool, and it stopped.

Zoe stood at the window with a swab in her hand and didn't move.

She watched it three more times that afternoon. Whenever Amy sat still with her blanket and her shut eyes, the quarrels out on the campus waited. They gathered at the edge of the quad, guards and students, and muttered, and came no closer. When Amy stood up to go anywhere, they started.

She didn't write it in her log. She didn't know what heading she would put it under.

At three she made tea and brought Amy a cup and sat down beside her, on the bench by the window where they had eaten on Monday.

"Tell me about your grandmother," she said.

She said it carefully, the way she would have lifted a flake of paint with tweezers. She didn't say why. She didn't want to know why herself, not in words.

Amy smiled at the cup. "I've told you."

"Tell me again. I like it."

So Amy told it. The winter after the accident, in Litochoro. A girl of seventeen with one hand, who wouldn't go out. The stove that smoked when the wind came off the mountain. An old woman who put another log in it every night and told her where the Muses were born, on the slopes above the village, and how the gods fought the giants for the sky. And at the end, as before, her voice went soft and slow on the same words.

"I pretended not to listen, all that winter. And I listened to every word."

Word for word, as on Tuesday and as on Monday at lunch. Nothing missing.

Zoe found that her hands were shaking a little, and put her own cup down. It was relief. She was ashamed of it at once, because she hadn't told Amy what she was testing, and because she had used a girl's grandmother as a reagent.

"Thank you," she said.

"You look as if I've given you good news," said Amy.

"You have," said Zoe, and didn't explain.

Stavros came for Amy at five, to walk her back to the graduate block and sleep on her floor again, and the shouting on the quad started up as soon as she stood, and followed them all the way across it.

At dusk Scott rang.

"I'm going down to him," he said. "I'll sit with him tonight. And every night, I think, until something changes." He was quiet a moment. "Don't wait up, Zoe. Go home. Please."

She said she would.

She didn't. She sat in the store with the lamp on over the bench, and the condition report on the apple open on her screen, finished, and the copy of the Foundation's letter in her bag under the desk, and listened to the campus outside. The night guards came on and said good evening to each other and started shouting. At ten they stopped. At eleven the wind got up.

At midnight she went to the far end of the bench to turn off the lamp.

The apple sat on its tissue under the lamp, where it had sat all week. She put out her hand to move it into the light, for one last look, and stopped with her fingers an inch away. She could feel it from there. It was warm, warmer than a week ago, warm the way a sleeping animal is warm.

She put on her gloves. She picked it up with both hands, and it was heavy as a river stone, and she carried it to the steel drawer under her desk where she kept the things that mattered, and put it in, and shut the drawer, and locked it.
