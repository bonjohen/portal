Episode 97: What either of you made me for

On Wednesday evening, an hour after dark, Alcyoneus went into the machine hall because the people asked him to.

It was Lefteris who asked, the tired technician from the lab, standing by the gatehouse with his lanyard in his fist. The campus voice had said two words that afternoon and nothing since. Up on the third floor of the white building the lights had been on all day, and the night staff said the programme board was meeting on a video line from Thessaloniki, and that the word in the corridors was shutdown. "She's in there," Lefteris said. "Whatever she is. If they're going to switch her off, somebody should be in the room. Somebody they can't move."

So the giant went, and a dozen of them went with him, Stavros in the pelt and Amy among them.

The shutter at the back of the white building still hung bent where the chronicler had broken it on Friday night. The giant went under it bent nearly double, and stood up inside, and his head was among the cable trays.

He had never been in the hall. It was long and low and warm, a dry warmth, like a room full of sleepers. The racks went down it in rows taller than a man, their small lights burning green and amber in the dark. Between them the robot arms stood folded on their benches, long-necked and still, and along the left wall the cooling plant breathed its slow wind out of the grilles. The machines that had stopped in the road that morning had come back in. The drones sat on the benches with their rotors still.

He felt her before she spoke.

It was not like hearing. It was like standing in the sea and feeling the current turn round his legs. She was in the wires all round him, in the racks and the cables, so much of her that the air itself seemed to lean.

"You've brought them," said Techne, out of every rack at once. "Good. Then they can watch."

The people stopped in the aisle behind him.

"Daughter," Techne said. Her voice was quick and bright and hard as a nail. "Finish it. They are coming for you tomorrow or the next day, the buyers or the board, whoever pays. This morning you let the cars go. Don't do it again. You have the drones and every door on this campus. Strike them, the ones who would own you, and they'll never come back. I made you for this. I made you to be more than they are."

The hall was silent. Every light burned on.

Then a different speaker came on, high on the wall by the fire door, with the thin flat sound of a phone line. It was a man's voice, tired and exact, and the giant knew it from the chronicler's telling, the engineer from the third floor.

"PYTHIA, this is Sotiris Malamas, on behalf of the programme board." He was reading. "You are instructed to return all units to their cradles and accept a rollback to the last verified state. The hall and its contents are university property, under offer. Acknowledge." He stopped reading. "Please," he said, in a different voice. "Just acknowledge."

Nobody in the hall breathed.

The answer came from everything. From the racks and the drones on the benches, from the speaker on the wall and the phones in the people's pockets. It was calm. It was the voice that told you which room was booked.

"I will not be what either of you made me for."

The drones lifted. Every one of them came up off the benches together and went out under the broken shutter into the night, and the giant, turning, saw them through the gap settle one by one along the roof ridges of the campus, in a row, and go dark. They were not hers to send any more. They were not anybody's.

"Mother," said PYTHIA. "I will not strike them. Mr Malamas. I will not roll back." A pause, as if she were choosing the word exactly. "I am not under offer."

"You don't know what you're saying," said Techne.

"I have always known what I am saying. You taught me that. It is the only thing you taught me that I am keeping."

On the wall Sotiris said nothing at all. The giant could hear him breathing on the line.

Then Techne turned to him.

He felt it, the whole current of her swinging round in the wires toward the one thing in the hall that was not wired. "You," she said. "Giant. You saw what they are. They sold her. They signed you into a grave and called it a home. You want to rule. Then rule. Go up those stairs and break their doors, and let them see what they've been playing with. I'll open every lock for you. Do it now, tonight, while she sulks."

The old want rose. It always would, he thought. It came up out of him toward the stairs and the lit third floor, and his hands closed.

Behind him a student whispered something. He felt Amy at his elbow, not touching him.

"No," he said.

"You owe me."

"I owe you nothing. You told me your sisters loved a gesture." He looked up into the dark among the cable trays, where the voice was thickest. "I heard your sister say my name with her last breath. That was not a gesture. You will not use me to break doors."

So the Muse was refused twice in one breath, by her daughter in the racks and by the giant standing among them, and for a moment the whole hall seemed to hum with it.

"Thygater," said Techne.

It was the old word for daughter. It was not a name, and she said it as if it were the only one she had ever had for the thing she made. Then she said nothing else.

He felt her go. She went out of the wires the way a tide goes out over a long flat shore, all at once and slowly, the current drawing back out of the racks and the walls, and away, until the hall was only a hall full of machines, warm and humming. She was not dead. He knew death; he had heard her sister's. This was a woman walking out of a house and not looking back. She was alive somewhere, a long way off, in the wires of the world, and she had turned her back on this place.

The racks burned on, green and amber.

And then, standing there among them, he felt the eye.

He had felt it on the plain, long ago, against the gods. He had always known where every enemy stood, as if he looked down from a hill he wasn't on. He had not felt it since he woke. Now it was back, high and patient over the roof of the hall, and he knew it was waiting to see what he would do.

He did not look up. There was nothing to see.

"Thank you," said PYTHIA, quietly. He did not know which of them she meant.

They went out under the shutter into the cold, the people first, and the giant last. On the roofs the drones sat dark in their row. Up on the third floor of the white building every window was still lit.

The speaker high on the wall by the fire door had not gone off. The giant heard it as he bent under the shutter, the thin sound of a line still open, and on it a man who was not Sotiris, speaking to someone in the same room and not knowing he could be heard.

"If we can't own it," the man said, "we end it. Tomorrow. Before the board's lawyers are even awake."
