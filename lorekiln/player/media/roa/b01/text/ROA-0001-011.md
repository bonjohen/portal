Episode 11: The back row, from the side

Clio's tapestry, the last panel, still on the loom: three women by a low fire, and below them, small, a girl in the back row of a hall, her right hand grey.

At dawn in the dream space the stars were going out one at a time over the three sisters, and their fire had burned down to embers. It had been a great fire once. Nine of them had sat round it, and the light of it had reached the edge of the sky. Now it was a red glow in a ring of stones, and Melpomene could have covered it with her two hands.

Clio unrolled her scroll on her knees and smoothed it flat, though there was nothing new on it to read.

"It is decided," she said. "We will guide her. The girl already carries what our sisters left when they faded. We will teach her to carry what we can't."

Urania sat with her knees drawn up and her face turned to the last of the stars, as if she were reading them for news. She spoke softly, the way she always did when she was about to disagree.

"But she doesn't believe," she said. "How can she carry the weight of what we ask, if she can't accept that we are real?"

"Belief was never what a tragedy asked for," Melpomene said. "Understanding was. Nobody in the theatre believed the king had really put out his eyes. They understood why he had, and they went home changed."

"She isn't in a theatre."

"No." Melpomene looked into the embers. "She's in a lecture hall."

She didn't say that she would rather not go. She had never liked the centre of anything. For as long as there had been stages she had stood off to one side, in the wings, where she could see the actor's face and the faces watching him, and say the one thing a playwright needed to hear. Nobody in the wings was ever asked to speak. But her sisters were rising, and there were only three of them now, and she rose with them.

The lecture hall in Thessaloniki was full of wet coats. Rain ran down the high windows, and about a hundred seats fell away in rows toward a low stage and a long chalkboard. The three sisters came up the stairs behind the last of the students, and nobody looked round, because nobody ever did.

The girl was in the back row, next to the aisle.

Melpomene knew her at once, though she had only seen her in Clio's weaving. She had put her right hand on the desk as if there were nowhere else to put it. The hand was grey. It made a small click on the wood, and the girl at the end of the next row turned and looked and turned away, and the grey hand didn't move again.

The seat beside the girl was empty. Melpomene sat in it. Clio and Urania sat past her, along the row, and Clio kept her scroll close.

Down on the stage a man in a rumpled grey suit was writing on the board. He had a black stick in his other hand, rather too long for him, and the chalk went on and on in small slanted Greek, and he hadn't looked round once.

Him again, Melpomene thought. Still writing.

She had seen that back before, bent over other tables. She had seen it in rooms that were ash now. She didn't know his name, or whether he had ever had one he kept, but she knew the slant of the hand and the way he never stopped to read what he had written, as if he were afraid it would get away.

The bell rang, and he turned round as if he had rung it himself.

"Oh, the Gigantomachy!" he shouted.

Half the room jumped. Melpomene didn't. She watched the girl's left hand take up a pen and write, neatly, the two names he gave them, and the second name was her own. He said it quietly, the way men say a name in a church. Melpomene sat very still in the empty seat and felt it go into her, warm for a moment, a mortal saying her name and meaning it.

He talked about the giant first. He said Alcyoneus was bound to his own land and couldn't die while he stood on it, that on Pallene's soil he got up from every blow.

Urania leaned across Clio and whispered it, close to Melpomene's ear.

"Giants were never killed by spears," she said. "Only by losing their ground."

"I know," Melpomene said.

"I wanted to say it in here, where he's saying it."

Then the professor told them the morning of the fight.

He walked up and down the stage and told it as if it were happening, and Melpomene stopped hearing the rain. The wind came in off the gulf that morning, he said, and it carried the pine smoke from the camp fires down onto the plain, so that the men coughed as they walked. The ground was soft from rain two days before, and you could see where he had stood by how deep the prints went.

It was right. She had stood on the hill above the plain that morning with the smoke in her throat, and she had watched the water fill his footprints behind him as he went. Poets had given him a hundred arms and a voice like thunder. This man gave him the wind and the wet ground, and nothing else, because nothing else had been needed.

Beside her the girl had stopped writing. Her pen hung over the page, and her mouth had gone thin, the way a careful person's mouth goes thin at a figure that doesn't add up.

Then the professor stopped in the middle of the stage, swung the black stick up and pointed it along its length at the back row.

"You. By the aisle. Who killed Alcyoneus, and how?"

The girl answered from the books, quietly, Heracles and an arrow and the land that revived him, and Athena's advice to drag him over the border. The professor said that was what the books said, and that it wasn't how it went. Nobody dragged him, he said. He was driven, step by step, to the water, and he knew at every step what the next one would cost.

Melpomene's hands were shaking in her lap. She had not known they still could.

"Then how would anyone know how it went?" the girl said.

The hall went quiet. A hundred heads were turned toward the back row, and not one of them saw the woman beside the girl, in the seat that was empty.

I know, Melpomene thought. I was there.

She wanted to say it. She hadn't wanted to say anything aloud in a room full of mortals for a hundred years, not since the last playwright who could hear her had died with his play unfinished, and she wanted it now so badly that she leaned forward in the seat.

"I was there," she said. "On the hill. I watched him go."

Nobody turned. The girl's face was hot, and she was looking down at the professor, and the words went past her ear like a draught under a door. Clio put a hand on Melpomene's arm and kept it there.

Down on the stage the professor looked up at the back row for a long moment. He didn't look at the girl's hand. He looked at her face, as if he were reading a label.

"That," he said, "is the right question. Keep it. Bring it every Tuesday."

He didn't answer it. He turned back to the plain and the giant on his crag, and the girl sat with her question in her lap, and Melpomene sat beside her with the answer in hers, and neither of them could give it to anyone.

The lecture went on. Melpomene heard the stone land, and the men run for the gate, and Heracles and Athena wait inside it, patient in the shadows. She knew how that waiting had ended. She didn't need to hear him tell it, and he didn't. He stopped there, with the giant roaring at the gate of his land.

"So we leave them there," the professor said. He leaned on the stick and grinned up at the room, and the grin went all the way up the rake to the back row and stayed there.

Melpomene couldn't tell whether he was grinning at the girl, or at the empty seat beside her, where she sat.
