Episode 64: Three notes

On Monday morning Zoe Lambrinou set out the terms on the bench by the window before Amy Doukas arrived, the way she would have set out a treatment.

She had spent the weekend on them. She had read the lyre's file through again on Saturday, and on Sunday she had sat at her kitchen table at home with her own log open, scrolling back through the weeks since October to the four days when her log jumped an hour. Four times she had sat down to work on the strings, and four times she had looked up and found the clock an hour on. The store's camera said she had stood with her hands on the strings the whole time, quite still. She had put it down to tiredness, and then to the clock.

So the terms were these. The case would be open on the bench, where the light was good and she could see. Amy's warm hand would be bare and the other gloved, so that only the one hand touched the strings. And beside the case Zoe's log would be open on its laptop, with the clock written in at the top of a fresh page, and she would write down the time of everything, as it happened, whatever happened.

Amy read the terms off the page and nodded.

"You've done this before," she said.

"Never. That's why there are terms."

They didn't start at once. Zoe had a condition report to finish on the gilt apple, which still sat on its tissue at the far end of the bench, and Amy had samples of her own to log, and neither of them seemed in a hurry. At half past twelve Amy went out and came back with bread and white cheese and two pears from the canteen, and they ate at the bench by the window with the lyre's case shut between them.

"My grandmother used to make me eat," Amy said. "That winter. In Litochoro." She turned a pear in her warm hand and looked at it. "I'd sit by the stove and not eat, and she'd put the plate on my knee and sit down across from me and start a story in the middle. Where the Muses were born, up on the slopes. How the gods fought the giants for the sky. She'd stop at the best part and wait until I'd eaten something." She smiled. "Every night she put another log in the stove. And I pretended not to listen, all that winter."

Her voice went soft and slow, as it had on Tuesday, on the same words.

"And I listened to every word."

Zoe ate her bread and didn't say anything. She had heard that sentence three times now, each time the same, each time set down like a cup. She found that she liked hearing it.

At twenty past one she wiped the bench, and opened the case, and wrote the time.

The lyre lay on its foam in the window light. Its sound box was dark old shell, worn smooth at the edges by hands that weren't theirs. Its two arms of pale horn curved up to the crossbar that Amy had cleaned on Tuesday, and from the crossbar the strings came down, bright and cheap and modern, the only new thing about it.

Amy pulled the blue glove onto her left hand. She flexed the right, and looked at it, and held it over the strings without touching them.

"What do I play?" she said.

"I don't know. Whatever it wants. Three notes. No more than three. Then we stop and I write it down."

Amy nodded. She put the tip of her forefinger to the lowest string and plucked it.

The note came out of the lyre and filled the store. It was low and round and perfectly clear, and it went on longer than any nylon string had a right to, and Zoe felt it in the bench under her wrists. She had heard the thing hum before, with nobody's hand on it. She had never heard it played. She found she had stopped breathing.

Amy plucked a second string, higher. Then a third, between the two.

It was the clearest sound Zoe had ever heard from any instrument, and she had heard a good many, in all her years among other people's treasures. It was clear the way cold water is clear. Three notes, and they hung in the air over the bench together, and she felt something in her chest lift to meet them.

Then Amy put her finger to the first string again, to see, and nothing happened.

The string was dead. It moved under her finger and made no sound at all, flat as a wire on a fence. Amy plucked the second, harder, and it gave a dull small click like a fingernail on a table, and no note. She ran her warm hand across all of them, and they lay under it like strings on a broken toy.

"It's stopped," Amy said. Her voice was very small. "Zoe, it's stopped."

Zoe wrote the time. 13:24. She leaned over the bench and looked at the strings, and at the crossbar, and at the pegs, and nothing had moved. Nothing had slipped. She put out one gloved finger to touch the nearest string, and drew it back, because those weren't the terms.

"Don't touch it," she said. "Let it be."

They let it be. Amy sat with both hands in her lap. The clock over the desk went round. Zoe heard Amy's breathing and her own, and the fan in the climate cabinet behind them, and outside the window, a long way off, somebody laughing on the quad.

She bent over the lyre again to look at the crossbar, close, with her face a hand's width from the strings.

One of them hummed.

It was very faint, a single thread of sound under her breath, as if her breathing on it had been enough. She went still. The hum went on a moment, and then the string beside it took it up, quieter, and the lyre lay on its foam with two strings singing softly at nothing, the way it had sung on the day Amy's hand first passed over it in October.

She wrote the time. 13:25.

Then she closed the case. She closed it gently and did up the catches, one and two, and laid her flat hand on the lid, the way you lay a hand on the back of a frightened child.

"That's enough for today," she said.

Amy didn't argue. She was looking at her right hand as if she had never seen it before.

Through the window, out across the yard, there was shouting from the staff car park. Zoe looked up. Two men in lab coats were standing by a small red car and a white van, both talking at once, and she knew one of them, a technician from the soils lab who brought her mould samples every winter with a box of sweets. He was shouting about a parking space. The other one pushed him, hard, with both hands, and he went backwards into the van's wing mirror and it snapped off and hung by its wire.

They stood looking at the mirror. Then they started shouting again.

"Everyone's so angry," Amy said. "All week. Have you noticed?"

"Since Friday," said Zoe, and didn't know why she had said Friday.

Amy stood up and gathered the cups and plates from lunch to take to the sink. Halfway there she stopped, and flexed the warm hand, opening and closing it, and looked back at the closed grey case on the bench.

"I'd like to play the whole thing one day," she said. "All of it. Not three notes. Whatever it is."

Zoe was a woman who said nothing before she had read the file. She had said it to deans and to a giant. She opened her mouth to say it now.

"Perhaps," she heard herself say.
