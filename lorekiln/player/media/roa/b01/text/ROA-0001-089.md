Episode 89: One after another

At seven on Friday morning Amy rang Professor Scott and said only, "I've found her. The building with no name. Come."

He found them in the long ground-floor corridor, under the strip of cold light, halfway down, by the heavy grey fire door into the machine hall. Clio was lying on the floor against the wall with the tapestry spread over her knees, and Amy was sitting beside her on the concrete with her back to the wall and her coat over both of them. The clock above the fire door said ten past seven.

Clio was smaller than she had been on the steps. She was so light now that when Amy shifted, the old woman moved with her like a cloth.

"I tried to lift her," Amy said. Her voice was steady and very quiet. "To take her back to my room. I can't. It's not that she's heavy. It's that there's nothing to hold. My hands go through the edges of her." She looked up at him. "So I'm staying here. I'm not moving."

Scott knelt. "Why here?" he said to Clio. "Of all the places."

Clio opened her eyes. They were still quick.

"Because this is where the next of it is being made," she said. "In there." She moved her head a little toward the fire door and the hum behind it. "Somebody should see it, chronicler. I always went where it was made. I'm not going to stop for the last page."

Then she shut her eyes again and held the scroll against her chest.

Scott stayed an hour. Then the first of it started outside, and he went.

All that day the campus came apart in an order he had never once seen in all his notes.

At nine the guards and the engineers came to blows in front of the building over the night's lost drones, as they had on Monday, and he got between them and talked until his throat was raw, and they went off in two sullen knots and it was over. Nothing else started while it lasted. Nothing started for a quarter of an hour after. He sat on a bench and got his breath, and the moment he stood up, the students came.

They came out of the library and down the steps, forty of them, fifty, because a rumour had gone round the canteen that the guards had shot the giant and meant to finish him. They met Rigas's men on the quad, and it was the students against the guards now, harder than the first, louder, with a broken window in it and a girl's nose bleeding. Scott was in the middle of that one too. It went on longer. When it ended, it ended all at once, as if somebody had said enough.

And then the drones came over the quad, a whole flight of them, hanging low over the paving where the fight had been, only when the fight was done and he had caught his breath. Not before. They held off until he was ready for them.

He stood under them with the stick in his hand and looked up.

In two thousand years of watching other men's battles he had seen fights spread like fire and fights go out like lamps, and riots that came in waves, and wars that stopped for harvests. He had written all of it down. He had never seen fights arrive as these did, one after another, each harder than the last and each waiting politely for the one before it to be over. He didn't know what it meant, and he didn't write it down.

At four he sat on the step of the lecture block, where Clio had sat on Monday, and got out his notebook, and set it down in a few lines because he couldn't stop himself.

The Muses gone, all but one, and she on a corridor floor. The old man in the sea with his ring. The lyre in a cupboard behind a key in his waistcoat. A giant sitting on grass that could kill him, with a cut on his face four days old, and his blood lying on it where it fell. And the woman he loved walking into every room he was afraid of, and taking his arm on the stairs.

He looked at the page. Clio had told him to stop writing her down. He drew a line through her, gently, so that the words still showed.

By dusk the word was all over the campus, and it came to him in pieces. Rigas's guards meant to go into the machine hall that night and cut its power, to ground the drones for good. The engineers had heard, and meant to stop them, and some of the students meant to film both. Somebody said the guards had bolt cutters. Somebody said the engineers had locked themselves in.

At a quarter past six his phone lit with the campus assistant's calm white box, which he had never once opened. The badge of Z. Lambrinou, it said, had opened the yard door at the back of the building with no name at four minutes past six.

He had texted her at four where he would be. With Amy. In the building with no name. She had come to find him.

He went back to the corridor at a run.

Amy was where he had left her, on the floor, under the strip of light. It had gone dimmer. Half the tubes were out, and the green exit sign at the far end was brighter than the rest. Clio lay across her lap now. Behind the fire door the hall's hum had stopped, and through it, very faint, he could hear men shouting.

He tried the fire door. It was barred from inside and didn't give.

"Zoe's in there," he said.

Amy looked up at him. "She asked for you." She tipped her head toward Clio. "Just now. She said, where is the chronicler. Twice. Please. Stay with her. She's never asked anyone for anything."

He looked down at Clio. Her eyes were shut, and her lips were moving very slightly, as if she were reading something to herself.

He thought of the one woman who had stood at his shoulder through every death he had written down in two thousand years, reading what he wrote, and who was going now on a concrete floor, and had asked for him. And he thought of Zoe on the other side of the door, where the shouting was.

He could only be in one place. He had always been able to be in only one place.

"I'm sorry," he said to Amy. "Keep her. I'll come back."

Amy didn't answer. She bent her head over Clio and held her.

He went out through the end door into the cold yard with the stick in his hand, and the anger came up in him as he went, all the way up. He was angrier than he had been in two thousand years.

The shutter at the back of the machine hall was down. He put his hand on it and it was warm. The lock at its foot was fused, the steel run and set like candle wax, and from inside, in the dark, he could hear every one of them shouting.
