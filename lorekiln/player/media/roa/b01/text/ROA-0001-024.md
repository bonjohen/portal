Episode 24: Breath on the glass

Amy Doukas carried a folder of clay photographs across the campus on Wednesday afternoon to the conservation store, a long single-storey building with a loading bay at the back and a sign on the door that said staff only. She knocked, and waited, and knocked again.

The woman who opened it had a pencil pushed through her hair and blue gloves on her hands. Zoe Lambrinou looked at Amy, and at the folder, and then went back inside to the bench without asking her in, so Amy followed.

"The clay in the secure lab," Amy said. "It's mine, I found it. I wanted to ask how clay that old ought to be handled, if it starts to crack."

"Has it cracked?"

"Not yet."

"Then you're early. Good." Zoe peeled off one glove and pulled a brown file from a tray on the bench, and Amy saw her own name on the tab. Zoe sat down and read it. She read it all the way through, every page, while Amy stood holding the folder of photographs, and she didn't look at the photographs, or at Amy, until she had turned the last page.

"You're the one who says she rode in the truck," she said.

"I did ride in the truck."

"Mm." Zoe closed the file. "The dean's office says a donor, and the lab says a donor, and you say a truck. And Petrakis's crew dug it up and the university's permit covers the ground. So whose find is it, really?"

"Mine," Amy said. "I found it. I've measured it more times than anyone alive."

Zoe almost smiled. She held out her bare hand for the folder, and at last she looked.

Amy waited. The store was cool and dry and smelled faintly of glue, and steel shelves ran the length of it under flat white light. On the bench by Zoe's elbow lay a catalogue card in fresh ink, and on a shelf at chest height, alone, sat a small old lyre, its sound box dark with age and its two horn arms pale. Amy saw it and didn't go near it. She didn't know why not. It was only an old instrument on a shelf.

Zoe went through the photographs quickly and well. She asked about the cloth under the clay, and the damp in the cave, and how fast they had taken it out into dry air. Then she wrote on the back of a requisition form in a small hard hand the humidity to keep the room at, and under it the name of the only person on the campus she would let near the clay with a brush, which was her own.

"If it cracks, you come to me," she said. "Not the lab. Me."

"Thank you."

Zoe pushed the form across, and her other hand knocked the catalogue card, and she picked it up to straighten it. Then she stopped and read it, and frowned.

"Lyre, tortoiseshell and horn, provenance unknown. Strings modern. Do not play." She turned the card over and back. "That's my writing."

"Is something wrong with it?"

"Eleven words." Zoe counted them again with the tip of her pencil. "I copied it off the card that came with it, on Saturday, I'm sure I did. I always do. And I can't remember writing it." She set the card down very square on the bench. "Nineteen years," she said, mostly to herself. "I've never once not remembered writing something."

She looked up at the lyre on its shelf. Amy looked too, and then away.

That night Amy went to the lab after eleven. Lefteris had said on Monday that she could sit with him on his breaks if she brought coffee and didn't touch anything, and she had a paper cup in each hand.

The way from the graduate block ran across a gravel yard behind the faculty rooms, between two walls. It was dark apart from a lamp at the far end. Halfway across the yard she stopped.

She knew the guard was behind the wall to her left. She hadn't heard him. She hadn't seen a torch. She simply knew, the way she knew where her own feet were, that he was coming round the end of that wall, slowly, about twenty steps off, with his radio on his belt. It was as though she could see the whole yard from above, the two walls, the lamp, the little shape of the guard and the smaller shape of herself standing still with two cups of coffee. The picture was as clear as a map.

She waited, quite still, and nothing came round the wall. Then the cups were burning her fingers and she walked on, and the guard came round the end of the wall to her left, exactly there, and lifted his torch, and saw her and nodded and went on.

Amy stood a moment longer in the gravel, holding the cups. Her head had drawn her a map like that once before, in the back of the truck, and she had blamed the cold. She had walked this yard a dozen times, and knew where the guard went, and that was all it was. She walked on, and didn't write it down.

In the lab Lefteris was alone at his three screens, the woman from imaging gone to the canteen. He took his coffee with both hands like a present.

"You want to see something boring?" he said. He brought up two scans side by side, one from the morning it came in and one from tonight, the ribs laid out in pale grey bars. "One millimetre. Here, and here. The ribs have moved a millimetre in a week." He tapped the screen. "That's settling. Clay dries, it shrinks, things move. I'll be writing the word settling a lot."

Amy looked at the number. It was the only number she had, and she couldn't make it mean anything yet. She wrote it down anyway.

"These scanners," Lefteris said, leaning back. "You know how we got them? The Foundation. Vardis money. The grant came through in July, the same day my colleague's car went off the road above Kalandra. She's all right, she's walking. But that's how I got moved onto this team. Lucky, really." He said it cheerfully, and drank his coffee.

At three he played her the corridor camera from the night before, the way he had on Monday. He had turned the contrast up. The blue corridor, the long pane of glass, the lab bright beyond it. Nobody in the corridor at all.

"Watch the glass," he said. "Not the corridor. The glass."

At five past three, on the pane, at head height, a small patch of grey came up out of nothing. It was round at first and then not round. It was the shape a patch of glass goes when someone stands close to it and breathes. It stayed a long time. Then it faded, slowly, from the edges in.

"Condensation," Lefteris said. "The air handling cycles at three. I've put a ticket in."

"Every night?"

"Every night." He laughed, not quite comfortably. "It's a very regular fault."

Amy sat on the spare stool with her notebook on her knee and watched him play it twice more. Then he went off down the corridor to the canteen to find the woman from imaging, and she was alone with the screens and the long grey shape beyond the glass.

At ten past three, the hour the body had come in, she looked up from the screen at the real glass in the real corridor.

A patch of it, at head height, was fogging over, coming up round and spreading. There was nobody there.
