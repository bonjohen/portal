Episode 43: What is in the wood

On Monday at dusk Alcyoneus kept the small man's terms and went to the southern fence before he went onto the campus.

He had spent Sunday on the buses. He had ridden one to the canal and one back, with his knees against the seat in front and the coat open across his chest, and a woman with a basket of eggs had moved three rows away from him and then, at the canal, come back to ask if he was a basketball player. He hadn't understood her. The phone the girl gave him had understood her, and told him, and he still didn't know what it meant.

The professor was at the wire, as he had said he would be. He stood on the far side of it in his grey suit with the black stick planted in the pine needles, as if he had stood there every evening since Friday, and perhaps he had.

"You came," he said, in the old tongue.

"I keep my word. You said you would keep yours." Alcyoneus put his hand on the top of the fence, and the wire sagged under it. "Where do your people keep their oldest things? The oldest made thing on Pallene. Where the old things are kept, the old ones come."

The professor looked at him for a long time. The light went out of the sky behind the pines while he looked.

Then he walked along the fence to a gate with a chain on it, and took a key from his pocket, and opened it.

They went down a road between the pines in the dark, the professor's stick tapping and the giant's bare feet making no sound at all on the asphalt. A long low building stood at the bottom with a loading bay at the back and one window lit. The professor knocked on the door under the light and waited, and knocked again, and opened it.

The woman inside was working at a tall metal cabinet with a glass front and a little screen of numbers glowing on it. She had a pencil pushed through her hair and blue gloves on her hands. She heard the door and turned, and found a giant stooped under her lintel with his head bent sideways against the frame, and Professor Scott behind him with his hand raised, already apologising.

"Zoe, I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. He asked to see the oldest thing on the campus, and I thought it was better he asked me than walked in on his own."

She didn't scream. Alcyoneus liked her at once for that. She stood quite still with her gloved hands held up in front of her, the way a healer holds them before she touches a wound, and she looked at him.

She looked at his face the way the girl with the tape had looked at his shoulders. She read it from the brow down, slowly, as if there were writing on it, and she didn't hurry, and she didn't look away when he looked back.

"Touch nothing," she said. She said it to both of them. "Either of you. Nothing in this room. Do you understand me?"

The professor told him what she had said. Alcyoneus nodded, and came the rest of the way in, bent nearly double under the ceiling, and stood where she pointed. She didn't take her eyes off him. She peeled off one glove and laid it on the bench, and picked up a pencil instead, as if a pencil were the proper thing for a person to hold at a time like this.

The room was cool and dry and smelled of glue. Steel shelves ran the length of it under white light, with boxes on them and wrapped shapes. On the bench by the window, on a bed of grey foam, lay an old lyre, its sound box dark with age and its two arms pale as bone.

He knew it before he was near it. He knew it the way you know a voice in the next room before you can tell the words.

"May I?" he said, and the professor asked her, and she looked at the lyre and at him, and after a long moment she nodded once. "Don't pluck it. Don't lift it. One hand, flat."

He knelt by the bench, so that his head was level with hers, and laid his long hand across the strings without plucking them.

Under his palm the wood breathed.

It was very faint, in and out, slower than a sleeper's breath. He kept his hand there a long time. He remembered the feasts before the war, the long tables on the mountain, the torches, and a voice going up over all the other voices so that the gods stopped eating to listen.

"Euterpe is in there," he said. "She put part of herself into the wood before she faded."

The professor didn't translate it at once. When he did, he did it quietly, word for word, and didn't add anything.

The conservator took her eyes off the giant's hand for the first time and looked at the professor instead.

"How can he possibly know that?" she said. "How can anyone possibly know that?"

The professor asked him.

"The way you would know your mother's voice," Alcyoneus said, "through a wall."

When it was put into her speech the woman said nothing at all. She looked at the lyre on its foam. Then she looked at Scott, waiting for him to tell her that it was a story, an old man's game with a frightened student in a costume, anything. Scott stood leaning on his black stick and said nothing, and didn't deny it, and she saw that he didn't.

"There were nine of them," Alcyoneus said. His hand was still on the strings. "Nine sisters. One of them left herself in this. Did any other? In a cup or a stone, here or anywhere?" He looked from one of them to the other. "You keep the old things. You tell the old stories. One of you must know."

The professor put it to her. She shook her head slowly. "I don't know what he's asking me."

"I don't know," the professor said.

This time Alcyoneus could not hear whether it was a lie. The small man's face was only tired.

He had come for one sister's grave to tell him where the other was. It wouldn't. A grave tells you only who is in it.

He lifted his hand off the strings.

They went on breathing a moment after, in and out, though nobody's hand was on them. The rhythm wasn't Euterpe's. Euterpe had breathed like a singer, deep and quick. This was slower and lower, a breath taken before speaking and held, and he had heard it on the hills above the plain on the morning he died, and in the dark of the clay for longer than he could count.

He turned round on his knees.

There was only the conservator's lamp on the bench, and the black window, which held the lamp and the three of them and nobody else.

The strings were still.

"What is it?" the woman said. She had seen his face change. "What did you hear?"

He didn't answer her. He knelt by the bench and looked at the dark window for a long time, as if it might give her back.
