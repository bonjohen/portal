Episode 100: A ring in a fish

Harbour office, Nea Fokea, register of the drowned, 6 November 1978: "Vardis, fisherman, of this village, went in after a stranger. The stranger lived."

On the Thursday night after the strike, in the cold beyond the campus fence, the pieces began to move.

A hand lay in the ditch beside the coast road, palm up, filling slowly with rain. Its fingers closed, and it began to drag itself through the mud toward the hills. A rib lay among the olive roots on the terrace above, white in the dark, and it turned over like something waking. In the dune grass by the shore an eye lay open, looking at the winter stars, and blinked.

None of them knew yet what they were. They knew the ground under them, which was theirs, and the pull of one another, the way water knows the slope.

Behind the fence, on the parcel, nothing of him moved. What had fallen there was only ash and blood, and the ground let it lie.

By first light on Friday they had found each other on a strip of scrub between the road and the sea. The pieces of him that had fallen on Pallene soil beyond the parcel came back to him one by one, and Alcyoneus pulled himself back together with his own two hands, furious. He set his jaw into his skull with a crack. He pushed the last rib home and held it there until it took.

Then he lay on his back on the soil beyond the parcel, whole again with the light coming up over the water, and it felt as if he had only been sent back to the start.

He remembered all of it: the people under his arms, the white of it, and being in a great many places at once.

He was angry at the strike, and angrier at the paper he had signed so that he could stand where Melpomene had last looked for him. It had made him a man for the length of a field.

High over the scrub a small grey drone hung in the dawn. He threw a stone at it and missed, and that made him angrier still. Higher than any drone was the eye he had felt in the hall, that had watched him die and was watching him now, and there was no stone for that.

He walked back to the campus barefoot along the coast road with a tarpaulin off a shuttered beach bar tied round his waist.

The fence ran along the edge of the hundred and forty hectares as it always had, wire on concrete posts, a gate at the shore road. Beyond it the machine hall was a black shell. Smoke still came off it in a thin line.

The people were in the car park in front of it. Some sat on the kerb in foil blankets. Lefteris was one of them, with his lanyard still round his neck, and the girl with the nose ring sat against his shoulder, her hair singed short over one ear. A woman in a torn university fleece walked slowly along a row of them with a list, reading out names, and some answered and some did not. At the end of the row there were shapes under sheets. Stavros was there in the old pelt, working among the broken concrete, heaving one end of a beam off the ground for two men with a stretcher. He hadn't taken it off since Wednesday. The giant could smell the venom working in it, bitter pitch over the smoke, and the boy laughed and bent to the next beam. Whatever it was doing to him under the fur, he would find it some night in a mirror, and put the pelt back on in the morning.

Alcyoneus stood at the gate and counted the sheets, and then he counted the people standing up. There were more of the second.

Amy Doukas was at the wire. She stood on the inside of the gate with her warm right hand resting on the mesh, and she looked at him.

He had been looked at by Heracles across a plain full of his dead brothers. Nobody had looked at him like this since the old days, from the wings, with the whole of a story in it and no fear at all. She looked at him the way Melpomene had.

"You came back," Amy said.

"I was sent back," said Alcyoneus. "I did not ask."

Professor Scott came down the path from the lecture block, tapping the shiny black stick. His grey suit was filthy, and one sleeve was burned to the elbow. Zoe walked beside him, her arm through his. He stopped a few paces behind Amy and leaned on the stick.

"Don't," Scott said. "That's still the parcel behind this gate, and it's still for sale. You set one foot on it and it will kill you again. It doesn't care why you've come."

"I know why I have come." The giant put his hand on the gatepost, and the concrete creaked. "The men who sent that fire are in that building with no name on it. I can hear them. I could reach them in ten strides."

"You could," said Scott. "You'd be dead by the fifth."

Alcyoneus looked past him, at the third floor of the building beyond it where the lights were still on. Whoever had spoken to the foreman's radio was up there, and nobody in the car park could say who. He wanted to walk through the wire. He wanted every man who had pressed a button standing in front of him. He had wanted things like that for so long that wanting them felt like being alive.

Amy stepped into the gap between the gateposts. She stood there on the line, small, and she did not move.

"Then go through me," she said.

He looked down at her for a long time. Behind her the woman with the list read another name, and a man on the kerb put up his hand.

The anger did not go. It sat in his chest like a stone he had swallowed. But it was not worth her, and it was not worth the people on the kerb, who were alive because he had stood over them, and he found he did not want to undo that.

He took his hand off the post.

Above the gate a camera on its pole turned slowly toward him, and its little light went green. On the panel beside the lock a screen woke. It showed him one picture: himself, from inside the hall, with his arms out and his back to the fire, and under him the people, every face turned up. Then the screen went dark and the light on the camera went out.

So the campus voice was still somewhere, though her racks were ash behind him.

"Techne," he said to the camera. "Muse. Show yourself."

Nothing answered. He listened for her in the wires, the way he had learned to, and she was nowhere he could find.

"Is she still there?" he said to Amy. "Off to one side."

Amy put her warm hand flat on her coat, over her breastbone. "Yes," she said. "Standing."

He turned and walked back along the shore road, onto ground that was his.

On Friday evening, in Nea Fokea, the lamp was lit outside the harbour office, and Maria Vardis was gutting fish on the quay.

She was twelve, and she was fast at it. The catch from her family's boat lay in two blue crates, not enough to fill a third. Her brother Nikos was hosing down the deck. Through the office window she could hear her father doing sums out loud, the price of the fish against the price of the diesel, the same sums he had done all winter, and they did not come out any better for being said.

She took up the last fish, a big one, heavier than it should have been. She slit it from the vent to the gills and put her fingers in, and they closed on something hard.

It was a ring. It was heavy and gold, with a dark stone set in it, and the sea had not dulled it at all. She wiped it on her apron and held it up to the lamp, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever held.

She looked at the office window. She looked at Nikos, who had his back to her. Then she closed her hand around the ring, and it was hers.

Just to see if it fits, she thought.

Then Maria Vardis, of Nea Fokea, whose great-grandfather's cousin went into the water on the sixth of November 1978 pulling a stranger out of it, put the ring to the end of her finger.
