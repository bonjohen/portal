Episode 19: What is left of her

On Friday night the campus was dark apart from the lamps along the paths, and Melpomene went down the road between the pines to the low block of the secure lab, and through its steel door, and no further.

Inside there was a corridor, lit blue and cold, with a long pane of glass along one side of it. Through the glass was the lab. She had known there would be. She had told herself, coming down the road, that she was only going to look at the building from outside, and then she had told herself that a corridor wasn't a room, and here she was, with her face a hand's width from the glass.

He lay on a long metal table under a gantry of scanners. The clay was grey under the white lights, wrapped and sealed as the followers had left it, and two robot arms hung folded over it like the legs of a sleeping insect. There were two people on the night shift. One was a young man at a desk with three screens. The other was a woman from somewhere else in the building, who came and went with a mug. Neither of them looked at the table. They looked at the screens, where he was a column of numbers.

Melpomene stood at the glass and didn't go in.

It would have been nothing to go in. Glass was nothing to her. She could have walked through it the way she had walked through the steel door, and stood beside the table, and put her face down close to the clay, and nobody would have known but her. Clio would never have known. That was what made it a promise, she thought. A promise is only a promise when the door is open.

She kept it. She stood in the corridor with her hands at her sides.

Near midnight the young man at the desk got up, stretched, and went over to the table. He stood looking down at the clay with his hands in his pockets, and said something to it, too low for the glass to carry, and laughed at himself and went back to his screens. Melpomene envied him so much that she had to turn her face away.

And she didn't listen for her name. That was harder. The heat sat in her chest, stronger here than anywhere, and under the heat she could feel the place where the name had been on the bridge, the way you can feel the place where a tooth was. If she listened for it she would hear it. She knew she would. So she didn't listen. She stood there and made herself count instead, the way the girl counted when she was frightened.

She counted what had changed since the back row.

The girl had been chosen, and still didn't believe, and had climbed into the dark beside him anyway. He had come home onto his own soil, and he was warm. The boy who couldn't feel had stood in the floodlight and wouldn't fall, and she had wept for him, she who had never wept at a hero.

And her hands were thinner. She lifted one and laid it flat on the glass. She saw her palm against the pane, and the lab through her palm, and she felt nothing. It wasn't cold. It wasn't anything. She was too thin now to feel the glass, the one thing standing between her and him.

She took her hand away and went back up the road before morning.

On Saturday morning the sun was out, and the three sisters stood on the steps of the conservation building, waiting for Urania's news.

A car pulled up in the bay beside the steps. The woman with the pencil in her hair got out of it, the one who had crossed the quad on Monday reading a file, and opened the boot. There were crates in it, three of them, small and heavy, and she stacked two on the ground and lifted the third against her chest and came up the steps.

The professor was coming the other way. He had his black stick in his hand and a newspaper under his arm, and when he saw her he stopped, and then he went quickly back up the steps ahead of her and held the door open.

"Zoe," he said.

"Thank you." She didn't look up from the crate. She went through the door and he stood holding it, though she was already in, though the two other crates were still on the ground behind her.

Melpomene looked at his face.

She knew that look. She had seen it once on a giant. He had been standing on a crag above the plain at evening, with the dust of a whole day's fighting on him, and he had turned his face up toward the hill where she stood. He hadn't known what to do with his face, either. He had looked as if something had been put into his hands that he had never been taught to hold, and was afraid of dropping.

The professor let go of the door. He went down the steps, and picked up the other two crates himself, and carried them in, and came out again without them and walked off toward the faculty rooms with his stick tapping. He didn't look back. He didn't need to.

"Well," said Clio softly. She had seen it too.

Urania came across the gravel then, quickly, with her face drawn.

"The boy is in the hospital at Nea Moudania," she said. "The one who wouldn't fall. They took him in on Thursday. He has a broken rib, and he didn't know it until they put him under a machine, and now he has a fever. It won't come down. He can't sweat it out. He lies there laughing at the nurses, dry as paper."

Melpomene closed her eyes.

"And the girl," Urania said. "I sat by her last night while she wrote her notes. She has written the truck down, and the gate pass on the dashboard. She hasn't written us. When she thinks of the dig at dusk, she tells herself it was a dream. I watched her tell herself." Her voice didn't rise. It never did. "And I counted again this morning. Don't look at me like that, Clio. I counted, and there are fewer."

Melpomene thought of the lab in the night, and the glass under her hand that she couldn't feel. She wanted one hour in there. Only one. She thought of the boy laughing in a hospital bed, burning, and of the girl telling herself a dream, and of Urania's count, which was Urania's life.

"What do we do?" she said.

Clio turned her scroll in her hands. She looked at Melpomene for a long moment, and Melpomene let her look.

"This afternoon," Clio said, "the three of us will walk the campus together, and find the girl. All three. And you'll lead, sister. Wherever that warmth of yours goes, we'll go there first, so that I can see what it does to you."

"And then the girl," said Urania.

"And then the girl."

"I'll come," Melpomene said.

After noon the three of them set out across the campus. Melpomene went in front. She said nothing, and her sisters said nothing to her. She followed the warmth in her chest from path to path, and it came and went, strong by one building and faint by the next, and she went wherever it was strongest without telling them why.
