Episode 8: The old escape hatch

The bursar's office was on the second floor of the administration building on the Pallene campus, and at nine in the morning it already smelled of burnt coffee. Amy had crossed the canal at dawn on the Monday water truck from the dig, and she still had trench dust in the creases of her trousers.

Haris Bakalis did not look up when she came in. He was a soft, tidy man with reading glasses pushed up into grey hair, and his desk was covered by a single document, thick as a phone book, bound at the spine with brown tape.

"Doukas," he said. "The survey girl. You wrote to me."

"I wrote to the secure lab. They sent me to you."

"Everyone gets sent to me." He took off the glasses, found them on his head, and put the first pair back on. "You want the long table. Under the scanners."

"For the find from the carved cave. It's a body sealed in clay, wrapped in cloth under the clay, and it should be examined here, properly, before anyone tries to open it." She kept her voice level. "I'd like the booking in my name."

He made a small sound, not quite a laugh. "The lab costs nine hundred euros a night to run, and that's before insurance looks at it. An object from off the campus, of unknown age, unknown contents, no export papers. The committee meets in November."

"The season ends in two days. They'll crate it for the stores in Thessaloniki."

"Then it will be safe in Thessaloniki."

He turned a page of the bound document, and she saw it was a deed, a photocopy of a photocopy, with the university's old seal gone grey and fuzzy. Some clauses were marked with yellow tabs. His finger rested on one of them, a long paragraph about the buildings standing on the land, and he tapped it twice without thinking.

"What's that?" Amy asked.

"That?" He glanced down. "That's the old escape hatch." He closed the deed, gently, as if it could bruise. "Every campus has one. Ours is older than you are. Nothing to do with your cave."

"Escape from what?"

"November," he said. "Write to the committee."

Amy went down the stairs with her right hand in her jacket pocket. The prosthetic took concentration even for that, the fingers closing in their own order, and today she let it close as hard as it liked. She was not going to lose it to a committee. She had found the fissure, and she was the only one who knew the clay was warm.

The quad lay in hard October sunlight, all gravel paths and dry lawn between low white buildings. At the far end stood the building with no name on it, its windows tinted dark, where the campus kept its servers. Students sat on the steps of the library with their coffees, and the sea showed blue between two of the roofs.

She heard the stick before she saw him. Professor Scott came along the path from the faculty rooms with his black walking stick tapping on the stones, his grey suit rumpled as if he'd slept in it, his hair wild in the wind off the sea.

"Miss Doukas," he said. "You didn't ring me. Petrakis tells me you've found something he didn't expect. That must have hurt him."

"It's a burial," Amy said. "Sealed in clay. The bursar says the committee meets in November."

"The bursar would say the sea meets in November." Scott leaned on the stick, which was rather too long for him. "I'll write to them. They read my letters, mostly so they can complain about them. But tell them this, if they ask you. Clay like that was never meant to keep the world out. It was meant to keep—"

He stopped.

A woman was crossing the quad from the car park toward the conservation building, a file open in one hand, reading as she walked. She was perhaps fifty, in a dark cardigan, her hair pinned up with a pencil. Amy knew her by sight: Zoe Lambrinou, who had run conservation on the campus for nineteen years and was said to read an object's file before she would look at the object, or at you. She didn't look up from the page. She didn't see them at all.

Scott watched her all the way to the conservation door. The stick stood still in his hand. When the door closed behind her he turned back to Amy, and for a moment his face was blank, the face of a man who has walked into a room and can't remember why.

"Where was I?" he said.

"Clay meant to keep something," Amy said.

"Yes." He looked at the stick, as if it might tell him. "Keep something in. Yes. I'll write to the committee." And he went off along the path, tapping, without saying goodbye.

Amy stood on the gravel a little longer. She had watched him talk for nearly two hours without a note and never once lose his place.

The water truck took her back across the canal in the afternoon. By nine that night the camp north of Nea Potidaia was dark apart from the floodlit trench, and the cold had come down off the hills, so the crew sat in their tents in their coats. When the clay body was found the university had paid for a guard from the town, a heavy man in a fleece who stood under the floodlight with his radio and every few minutes walked a round of the camp with a long torch. Petrakis had told the crew that nobody went near the cave after dark. Not even, he had said, looking at Amy, the person who found it.

She waited until the guard had passed her tent. Then she went out with the infrared thermometer in her pocket and her notebook in her good hand, and she kept off the gravel, on the rock.

The cave mouth lay fifty metres up the slope, beside a heap of spoil and stacked crates. She was halfway there when the guard came back round the end of the trench, and the torch swung up the slope toward her.

There was nowhere to go. She stopped beside the spoil heap and kept still, with her breath held, and the beam ran over her like water over a stone. It went on up to the crates, and down again, and away.

She let out her breath and climbed on. Two minutes later the guard came round again, and again she stopped where she stood, and the light crossed her shoulder and her face and didn't stop on either.

She was ten steps from the cave when the torch came round a third time, far too soon, as if he had heard something and turned back. Amy froze as before. The beam came up the slope and stopped on her boots.

It stayed there.

"Who's that?" the guard said.

She didn't move. Moving would be worse, and she didn't trust her legs. So she did what she always did when she was frightened, and counted. One breath, slow, through the nose. Two. The light lay on her boots and the guard took a step closer on the gravel. Eleven. Twelve. She could hear his radio hiss. Twenty. The beam crept up to her knees and trembled there. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. She thought about Petrakis and what he would say, and kept counting.

At forty the beam slid up over her hip and her chest and her face, and on up to the crates, and the guard saw nothing.

"Goats," he said to himself, and went back down toward the trench.

Amy stood a while longer before she trusted it. Then she climbed down into the cave.

Inside, the air was cold and smelled of chalk. The clay body lay on its shelf where they had left it, under a sheet, its shape like a long seed. She folded the sheet back, pointed the thermometer at the clay over the chest and read it by the light of her phone, held low. Thirty-two point four. The cave air was fourteen.

She wrote both numbers down, and the time. Under them she wrote the number forty, and stared at it, and put a question mark beside it. A man's eyes adjusting to the dark, she told herself. That could be measured too, if she had the right kit. But the first two times he hadn't needed to adjust. She left the question mark where it was.

An hour later she took the reading again. The cave had fallen to twelve degrees around her, and her fingers had gone stiff.

The clay was still at thirty-two point four.
