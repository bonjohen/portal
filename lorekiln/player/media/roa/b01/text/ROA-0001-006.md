Episode 6: Warm Clay

The wind came off the gulf in the middle of the afternoon and set every tarpaulin at the dig snapping at once. Amy Doukas sat on an upturned bucket at the edge of the north trench, just north of the canal at Nea Potidaia, with her notebook on her knee and her phone in her left hand, and waited for Yannis Petrakis to finish being angry at somebody else.

He was angry at the university. He had been on the phone to Thessaloniki for twenty minutes, walking a line along the top of the spoil heap where the signal held, and every few steps he stopped and glared at the sky as if it had joined the other side.

When he came down he didn't sit. "They're sending a team," he said. "Conservation, and a photographer. Thursday, if the road holds."

"For the cave."

"For the body. The one week I find something worth having, they want their own people on it." He said "I find" without looking at her.

The fissure had been hers. She had found the crack in the dark and put her torch into it once the crew had opened it, and seen carved stone where there should have been earth, and she had walked alone to the body at the end of it, and now it was his find and Thessaloniki's body. Amy didn't say so. She had learned that Petrakis didn't take requests. He took complaints, and he made them.

"I'd like to finish the record before they come," she said.

"The record is fine."

"The record has the cave on a sketch. It isn't on the grid."

Petrakis rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. "Then put it on the grid. Don't touch the body. Don't let the Petridis boy touch the body. When they come, they find it where it lay, and nobody in Thessaloniki writes a letter about my crew."

He went off toward the tents. Amy looked at her phone. The grid lived on the university's servers with everything else, and the paper copy had gone soft in the rain. She opened the campus assistant, the one every student had and nobody thought about, and held the phone close to her mouth against the wind.

"Send me the survey grid for the Potidaia dig," she said, "the north trench, and the squares around the—"

The phone buzzed in her hand before she had finished, and the file was already open.

It was the grid, the north trench laid out in its numbered squares, and the fissure was on it. Someone had drawn it in where she had never drawn it, a neat red line running down the slope from square fourteen and opening into a shape that could only be the cave.

"Here is the grid for the north trench," the assistant said in its pleasant voice, "with the cave squares."

She hadn't said cave. Amy looked at the screen for a long moment. Somebody had updated the file, then. Petrakis on the phone this afternoon, perhaps, or one of the Thessaloniki people working from his sketch. That was the simple answer, and she wrote it in her notebook with a question mark after it, because she didn't like an answer she hadn't checked.

The ladder down into the fissure was aluminium, and it rang under her boots. Below the wind there was no sound at all. The cave was cool and dry and smelled of chalk, and her head torch picked out the carvings a little at a time, giants and gods forever locked in conflict along both walls, a spear here, a raised arm there, a face with its mouth open that she had stopped looking at after the first day.

The crew had cleared the fall from the passage in an afternoon, and the body lay where she had found it, on the shelf of rock in the round chamber at the end. It was wrapped in cloth like a mummy and sealed over the cloth in clay, grey and cracked, with lines of letters pressed into it that her lamp was too weak to read. It was long. She had measured it twice on the first day, because it was longer than any burial she had seen, longer than a tall man by a full head and more.

She set out her kit on a square of plastic. She had also brought the infrared thermometer from the conservation box, which she used for checking that the finds tent hadn't cooked anything at noon, and she put it down beside the camera without quite knowing why.

The grid on her phone matched the cave better than her own sketch did, which annoyed her. She pinned her points anyway and took the measurements by hand.

The scale bar was the problem. It had to lie beside the body for the photographs, and the shelf sloped, and the bar kept sliding toward the edge. Amy knelt and set it down a third time, and a third time it slid, and without thinking she reached out with her right hand and laid it flat on the clay to brace herself.

She took it away at once. Don't touch the body. But the hand was a hand that touched nothing, really. It was carbon and silicone and motors, and it went where she sent it if she thought hard enough about the sending, and it carried no oil from her skin and no salt. She told herself that, kneeling there, and the telling did not make it true for Petrakis.

Then she noticed the fingertips.

She had put her left wrist against them out of habit, the way she checked a cup before she drank from it, and the silicone fingertips that had lain on the clay were warm against her wrist, warmer than her own skin, and nothing in her kit explained it.

Amy sat back on her heels. The cave was fifteen degrees, or near it; she had read the wall each time she came down. She picked up the infrared thermometer and pointed it at the rock beside the shelf. Fifteen point two. She pointed it at the clay over the chest. Thirty-one point eight.

She read it again, and again from a different side, and then she did what she always did and went looking for the answer she would have to write down.

Her head torch was an LED and gave off almost no heat, and she switched it off anyway and read the clay in the dark by the glow of the display. Thirty-one point six. There was no sun down here, and there never had been. There was no cable along the shelf, no battery pack anyone had left, no crack in the rock where warm air might come up from somewhere else. She laid her good palm on the stone beneath the body, careful not to touch the clay itself, and the stone was cold. Things that rotted grew warm, she knew that much, but nothing had been rotting in this clay for a very long time, and it didn't smell of anything but chalk.

There was a noise above. Stavros Petridis was leaning over the top of the ladder, his face red and dry in the slant of light, a wet towel round his neck.

"Petrakis says you're not to touch it," he called down.

"I'm not touching it."

He laughed, the laugh arriving before the sentence was done, and the cave threw it back at him. "Neither am I. I'm not touching anything. I'm sitting in the shade like my mother told me." He tapped the thermometer clipped in his shirt pocket. "Thirty-eight point one. I'm a boiled egg. How is it down there?"

"Cold," Amy said.

"Lucky you." His head went back out of the light.

She looked at the clay a while longer. Then she opened the site log on her phone, where Petrakis would read it tonight and Thessaloniki would read it on Thursday, and she wrote down the grid points, the photographs and the time. She didn't write the temperature. She put that in her own notebook instead, both figures, the rock and the clay, with the time beside them and a line under each.

It wasn't a lie, she told herself, climbing. It was a number she couldn't stand behind yet, and that kind of number brought a team down the road early to take a thing away before anyone had understood it.

At dusk the wind dropped and the floodlights came on over the trench. Amy went back down the ladder alone with the thermometer in her pocket, and the cave was darker than it had been, and colder. She read the rock first. Fourteen point nine.

Then she read the clay, and stood there in the dark with the display lit green in her hand, because the number had gone up.
