Episode 86: What he can't put down

On Tuesday evening, a little after eight, Stavros Petridis climbed the stairs to Professor Scott's rooms in the old block by the sea with the lion's pelt folded over his arm.

Scott heard him on the stair. Nobody else on the campus came up two steps at a time and laughed on the landing for no reason. He opened the door before the boy could knock, and Stavros stood there grinning in a T-shirt in the middle of winter, with the pelt held out in front of him like a waiter's cloth.

"I've brought it back," he said. "Can I come in? I want to show you something, and it's a bit undressed for the stairs."

Scott let him in. Stavros laid the pelt on the desk, carefully, the way he had once been taught to lay down a find. It lay there tawny and heavy, the lion's head folded under, the forepaws crossed. On its inner face the broad band ran along one edge, stiff and almost black, as straight as a hem. Scott had kept it nearly six hundred years and knew that band better than his own hand. The robe's venom had gone into it there on the pyre, in the dark, and stayed.

Stavros pulled his T-shirt off over his head and turned round.

The blisters had spread. In October they had been red marks on his shoulders where the pelt lay heaviest, and by the week of the roof, Amy had told him, they were in rows along his forearms. Now they went right across his back from shoulder to shoulder and down over the ribs on both sides, clear ones and old ones gone brown and hard, like a field after frost. On the left side the skin had broken and wept and dried again.

"I only know because I looked," Stavros said, over his shoulder. "In the bathroom mirror, with a hand mirror, the way my mother taught me when I was fourteen. You can't feel it, so you look at it." He laughed. "Six years of checking my feet every night and finding nothing, and now my mother's rules finally have something to look at."

Scott didn't laugh. He looked at the boy's back, and then at the pelt on the desk, and he thought of a man on a mountain tearing at his own skin while it came away in his hands.

"Put your shirt on," he said gently. "Sit down."

Stavros put it on and sat in the chair by the desk, with his elbows on his knees, and for a moment he didn't joke. It was the first time Scott had ever seen him not reach for one.

"I'm giving it back," he said. "Not because of that. I don't care about that, honestly. They heal, mostly." He looked at the pelt. "Because of last week. You asked me on Monday to keep near the giant when the guards came. And that night a girl from my year asked me to stand between her and her boyfriend in the bar, because I can't get hurt." He smiled, a little. "Everybody wants me in front of something now. I'm the boy you put in front of things."

"I asked you to keep near him because you're his friend."

"You asked me because I don't fall down." Stavros said it without any edge. "That's all right. That's true. But I don't want it back like that. If you give it back to me, Professor, give it back because you think I should have it. Not because I'm useful. I've been useful my whole life. I was useful in the pool for a week on a broken foot."

He got up. At the door he stopped and looked back at the pelt on the desk, and Scott saw how much he wanted to pick it up again, and how hard he was not doing it.

"It's the only time I feel anything," he said. "You know that. I'm not pretending I don't want it. I'm just asking you to think about it first."

Then he went down the stairs, one at a time.

Scott sat a long time after that.

He took the pelt off the desk and held it on his knees, and leaned the black stick against the side of his chair, where it stayed, because he had laid it down himself and wanted nothing from it. The lamp was on. Behind him the tall cabinet held the lyre in its grey case, and a little of the old bitter smell still came out of the cedar when the heating ticked. Below the window the sea went on.

He had carried two things all his life that he could not put down for long. One was in his lap, and one was against his chair, and he had given the one in his lap away in October because he could not watch a fevered boy die of heat, and he had told himself it was mercy. It had been mercy. It had also been a way to put one of them down at last, and give the weight to somebody younger.

He thought of keeping it. Locking it back in the cabinet beside the lyre, and turning the key, and telling the boy no. It would be the kind thing. It would also be one more thing an old man took away from a young one because he knew better, and the boy had been told no his whole life, by every rule on the inside of his wardrobe door.

He didn't decide. At two in the morning he was still sitting there with the pelt on his knees and his hand on the fur.

On Wednesday morning he went down to the quad at nine with the pelt over his arm.

Stavros was there. He was carrying crates of bottled water from the canteen's van to the secure lab for the giant, two at a time, in the thin winter sun, because somebody had to, and the giant drank more than the taps in the lab could give him. He had done six trips. His face was dark red and dry, with no sweat on it anywhere, and he was laughing at something one of the drivers had said, and he put the crates down on the lab step and stood there, swaying a little, with his hand flat on the wall.

Scott walked up to him and held the pelt out.

"Take it," he said.

Stavros looked at it and didn't move. "Why?"

"Because I can't watch you overheat on a quad." Scott kept his hand out. "That's the whole reason, and it isn't a good one, and it isn't because you're useful. I gave it to you once because I couldn't watch you burn up with fever in a ward. I'm giving it back for the same thing. You'll have to forgive me." He looked down at the black stick in his other hand. "And because I can't put my own down either. I've tried for two thousand years. I'd be a hypocrite to make you."

Stavros took the pelt.

He shook it out in the doorway of the lab and swung it round his shoulders, and pulled the lion's head up over his hair, and knotted the forepaws on his chest. Scott watched the red go out of his face as he stood there. And then, a breath later, he saw the boy's mouth open, and his eyes go bright.

Stavros laughed, the sting already starting.

"There it is," he said.
