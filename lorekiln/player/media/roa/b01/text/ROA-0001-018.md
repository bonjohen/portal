Episode 18: The one who never visits

On Thursday morning, the morning after the theft, the three sisters walked the Pallene campus in a thin cold sun, and Melpomene let the heat in her chest lead them.

It had not gone out in the night. It sat low under her ribs like a coal somebody had banked, and as she walked the gravel paths it rose and fell. By the library it was faint. By the car park it was almost nothing. Behind the conservation building, where a road ran down between the pines, it grew, and she went that way, and her sisters went after her without asking.

At the bottom of the road stood a low block with one row of high windows and a steel door, and a camera over the door looking at nobody. The heat in her was strongest here. It was so strong that she put her hand flat on her chest, as if she could hold it in.

"The secure lab," Clio said. She had read the sign. "So that's where they've taken it."

"Him," said Melpomene, and then wished she hadn't.

Clio looked at her and said nothing.

They found the girl in the security office by the main gate, a small hot room with a counter and a kettle. Her left elbow was bandaged, thick and white, and she held the arm a little away from her side. She stood at the counter and told it again, slowly, to a man in a uniform and a woman from the university with a lanyard. A truck at the dig, and men with rifles. She had climbed into the back with it, she said, and lain beside it in the dark, and it had crossed the canal at Nea Potidaia onto Pallene, and she had seen a campus gate pass on the dashboard.

The man in uniform wrote some of it down. The woman with the lanyard looked at the girl's bandage and then at her grey hand, with the careful patience of someone deciding what she hadn't heard.

"You've had a shock," the woman said.

"I've had a crate on my arm," the girl said. "I'm telling you where it went."

They gave her a form.

Urania stood close beside her the whole time, very close, and Melpomene saw her lift a hand toward the girl's shoulder and let it fall again. The girl was too sore and too disbelieved to hear anything this morning. Even Urania could see that.

The girl came out past them into the sun with the form folded in her good hand. She stopped on the step and looked down the road between the pines toward the low block, as if she could see through the trees to the steel door. Then she put the form in her pocket without reading it and walked off toward the library.

Melpomene watched her go. Three nights ago the girl had stood in a guard's torchlight and counted, and the guard had seen goats. Last night she had climbed into the dark beside him. She didn't believe a word of anything, and she had gone with him anyway.

Outside again on the path, Melpomene stopped.

There was a sound under the gravel. She hadn't heard it on Monday. Or she had, and taken it for the sea. It ran along under the paths, in the cables buried there, and up the walls of the dark-windowed building with no name on it, and along the wires that went from roof to roof. It was low, and it was everywhere, and it had a tune in it.

Someone was humming in the campus wires.

It wasn't a machine. Melpomene had heard every kind of machine there was, and they didn't hum like that, with breath in it. It was one of them. It was a sister. A new one, who had never once come to the fire.

"Do you hear it?" she said.

Clio listened, with her head on one side. Urania shook her head.

"I hear something," Clio said at last. "Like singing in another house." She turned her scroll in her hands. "Where has the new one gone? The one who never visits. We looked for nine days, once. Where did she go?"

"Here," said Melpomene. "Everywhere. Into all this." She turned her face away from the dark building. "She was born in a fire, and she wants nothing of ours. She never did."

"You said worse than that in 1945," Urania said softly. "You said it to our faces, by the fire, with the ash still on your hands. You said one of us had whispered it to them."

"I remember what I said."

"Then don't say it again. She is our sister."

Melpomene didn't answer. She stood on the path and listened to the humming until she couldn't bear it, and then she went on.

That night, by the fire, she told them.

The embers were low. Clio sat with the scroll on her knees and didn't open it. Urania lay on her back under the stars. Melpomene sat with her arms round her knees.

"The body in the clay," she said. "It's his."

Neither of them moved.

"I knew it in the cave. I knew the clay. I watched them make it." She looked at the fire. "I've known since Friday. I didn't tell you, because I knew what you'd say. And I'm telling you now because I can't carry it on my own any more."

Urania sat up. "Oh, sister," she said.

Clio said nothing. She had known there was something. She had known since Friday, and waited.

So Melpomene told them the rest, the part she had never told anyone.

"It came in waves," she said. "The war on the plain. Not all at once. One band of gods came against him, and he broke them, and they went back over the hills. And then the plain went quiet. Hours, sometimes, a whole night. He sat on his crag with his back to the stone and got his breath, and I could hear him breathing from the hill. And then the next band came. Stronger than the last. Always a little stronger, as if someone were choosing them in order. And he broke those too. Every time."

"And Heracles," Urania said.

"Heracles came last of all. When there was no one left to send." Melpomene's voice went quiet. "I begged him. From the hill. I stood up where he could see me and begged him to turn back, to go up into the hills and lie low and let them have the plain. He looked up at me. He heard me. He didn't turn back."

The fire ticked. Somewhere far off, out past the stars, something that might have been a wave broke on a shore.

"And now he's in a room on his own soil," she said, "and he's warm."

Clio unrolled her scroll then, a little way, and looked at it, and rolled it again.

"You want to go to him," she said.

"Yes."

"No." Clio's voice was firm, the way it was when it had to be. "Look at your hands, sister. I've seen them. Every hour you spend standing at that lab is an hour of you that the girl won't have. There isn't enough of you left for both. And Urania's vow is counting, whether she says the days aloud or not."

"I am," said Urania. "I'm sorry. I am."

Melpomene looked at her own hand in the glow, and didn't need to hold it up to know what she would see.

"I won't go to him," she said. "You have my word."

Clio rolled the scroll up tight, and tied it.

"I have never yet known a tragedy keep its word," she said.
