Episode 23: The mason's name

At a quarter to eight on Monday morning Amy Doukas went down the road between the pines with a letter in her pocket, and this time the steel door let her in.

The letter was on Professor Scott's paper, in his slanted hand. It said that Miss Doukas had found the object now held in the secure lab, that she was the only person to have recorded it in place, and that the record could not be made without her. Under his signature he had written his phone number, as if anyone who doubted it should ring him and see what happened. The woman at the desk read it twice and rang nobody. She gave Amy a visitor's badge and the lab's papers for the body, in a grey folder, to sign that she had seen them.

Amy read them standing at the desk.

Received 03:10, from a private courier, on behalf of a private donor. The courier's firm was a name she didn't know, with an address in Thessaloniki. Condition on arrival, sealed and intact. Provenance, recovered. Then there was a line that said Donor, and a space after it to the end of the page, and on the line there was nothing at all.

She looked at it for so long that the woman at the desk asked if something was wrong.

"Who's the donor?"

"It says."

"It doesn't say anything."

The woman leaned over and looked, and frowned, and said the office would have it. Amy signed that she had seen the papers. Under her name she wrote the date and, small, "donor line blank," and the woman watched her write it and said nothing.

Inside, the lab was white and cold and bright, and smelled of nothing. Lefteris Iliadis was at the desk with three screens, at the end of his night, his hood up and a carton of juice in his hand.

"The finder!" he said. "You were telling the truth about that part, then."

"I was telling the truth about all of it."

"Okay," he said, and grinned, so that she couldn't tell whether he meant it this time.

She set up her camera on its arm beside the long table and began at the head.

In the cave her lamp had been too weak for the letters. She had seen that they were there, lines of them pressed into the clay over the chest and down the long body, and she had seen nothing more. Under these lights she could see every stroke. They were Greek, or the grandparents of Greek letters, some of them turned the wrong way round, and they ran in lines that went one way and then came back the other, like a man ploughing a field. She photographed them a hand's width at a time, with a scale bar laid along each strip, and logged every frame. It was slow work with the prosthetic holding the bar. She didn't hurry it.

Behind her Lefteris pulled on his jacket. On his way out he stopped beside the table and bent down to the clay.

"Wait till she's done," he told it. "Then you can do what you like." He laughed at himself and went out, and the steel door boomed shut after him.

Scott came at noon.

He stood behind her chair with his stick planted on the white floor and read off her screen without bending closer. He didn't stop to think. He went along the first line, and back along the second, the way the letters went, and read them to her in plain Greek as if from a letter he had been sent that morning.

It was a list of battles. Men's names, places on the plain, a ford, a hill. Then a line about cunning, a trick played on the gods with a fire left burning in an empty camp. Then, low on the chest, in deeper strokes, a line about love. Love for the Muse of Tragedy. He read the name and didn't change his voice for it.

"Melpomene," he said.

Amy had a grammar of the early dialects open on the bench beside her, and her own notes. She went through every word he had read, one at a time, while he waited. It took her an hour and ten minutes. She couldn't fault him once.

"Where did you learn to read it like that?" she said.

"I've had practice."

She opened her cave photographs next, because he asked to see them. He went through them quickly until he came to one of the round chamber's door, taken from inside, looking out. Over the door ran a carved lintel, a single long stone with a pattern of waves along its face, and he stopped there and leaned on his stick and looked at it for a long time.

"Dexios cut that," he said.

"Who?"

"Dexios. Look at the hooked chisel." He pointed with one finger, not quite touching the screen. "There, at the end of every stroke. He couldn't help it. He did it on every stone he ever cut."

Amy had read every publication on the masons of the north Aegean, which took less than a week. No record she knew named a single one of them before the classical period. "What's your source?"

"It's in the cut," he said, and that was all.

She wrote it down, and then she sat with her pen over the page. She had wanted the record so much. She had it now, on a screen, in a folder, with her name on the visitor's badge. And the man reading it to her had given her a mason's name and a goddess's, and there was nothing behind either of them but the way he said them. She could write down only what she could check, and leave the clay half read. Or she could write what he read, and put his name beside it as the source, and let it be his to answer for. In the end she did the second, and wrote "read by Prof. Scott, unchecked" in the margin beside every line she couldn't verify.

There were a great many of those.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"You can ask."

"Why would anyone write a love letter on a coffin?"

He looked at the long grey shape on the table, under the folded arms of the gantry. "It was never meant to be a coffin," he said.

"Then what was it meant to be?"

He didn't answer. He nodded to her, and went out, and she heard his stick go away up the road.

She worked on through the afternoon until the light in the high windows changed. She was packing the camera into its case when the steel door opened and Lefteris came back in, hours early, in a different jacket.

"Charger," he said, and went to the desk and dug it out from behind a screen. Then he stood there with the cable in his hand, and didn't go.

"Can I show you something? It's probably nothing." He sat down and opened a window on the left screen. "The corridor camera. It keeps a log of motion, just a list of times. I only looked because the alarm company asked." He turned the screen so she could see it.

There was a line for every night since Friday. At three in the morning, every night, the camera had logged movement at the glass, and every night it had lasted most of an hour.

He played her the footage from the night before. The blue corridor, the long pane of glass, the lab bright beyond it. The time in the corner ran on through three o'clock and past it.

There was nobody on the footage at all.
