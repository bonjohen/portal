Episode 55: Mother?

On Saturday morning Amy Doukas sat on the steps of the secure lab in the sun with her notebook on her knees, and read it back from Sunday to now.

Sunday had been simple, when she looked at it from here. A woman she had seen once through glass had died in a theatre, and a giant had sat down in the seats. That was all. Then she turned the pages and it wasn't simple any more. A giant lay on her table every Tuesday and got heavier. Two women sat by a fire in a place she couldn't find the edge of, and one of them looked at her hand. A fingernail walked. She had kept it since Wednesday in a locked steel drawer, and every morning it lay at the end of the drawer nearest the door. A benefactor knew which floodlight her cave had been under. A boy she liked let men beat him with batons, and smiled when it stung.

She had told the dean's office she had no objection to Mr Vardis sitting by the wall on Tuesday. She wanted to watch him watching.

She closed the book and opened her phone, because the hand's battery was low and she wanted to know how low.

The app was a plain one, made by the company that had fitted the hand. It showed the battery and the grip, with a log underneath that nobody ever read, line after line of numbers, the hand reporting to the campus network every few seconds what it had held and how hard. Grip, thirty-one per cent. Battery, eighteen. She scrolled down out of habit.

There was a line in the log she hadn't built.

It sat among the numbers as if it belonged there, stamped 03:12 that morning like the rest. It was one word, in lower case, with a question mark after it.

mother?

She looked at it a long time. Then she scrolled on. At 04:12 the same word. At 05:12, and 06:12, and every hour since, on the minute, the hand's log had written it again, mother?, in among the grip and the battery, and the last one had come at 09:12, eleven minutes ago, while she sat on the steps reading about a fingernail.

Amy's prosthetic hand, on the campus network, had written one word into its own diagnostic log, and the word was mother?

She rang the maker's support line. A young man in Athens took her serial number and was very patient. He said the app couldn't write words. It logged sensor values. It had no field for text, he said, and no way to put text in one, and she must have a corrupted file, and he would send her a new version. She asked him what a corrupted file would have to do, by chance, to spell mother with a question mark after it, every hour on the minute. He said he would send her a new version.

At noon she walked across the campus to the building with no name on it.

The door let her in on her badge, which surprised her. She climbed to the third floor, where a man sat alone in a room with the blinds down and four screens lit, with a packet of crackers beside his keyboard. She had seen his name at the bottom of the overnight summaries that came round to the students, Sotiris Malamas, and once on a ticket about a fogged camera.

He didn't look pleased to see her. He looked at her phone when she held it out, and at the line on the screen, for perhaps two seconds.

"Data leak," he said. "Something on the campus network is writing strings into device logs. We've had it in the lab files, we've had it in the console. It's a known fault." He gave the phone back. "It's not your hand. Your hand's fine."

"Will you look at it?"

"I'm looking at it," Sotiris said, and turned back to his screens. "It's on the list."

She went down the stairs with the phone in her good hand. Halfway down it buzzed against her palm, and she looked, and it was 13:12.

On Sunday afternoon she sat on the seawall below the old quarter with Professor Scott.

The wall ran along the edge of the protected coast, where the university could never build, and the sea came in grey and slow over the stones below it. Scott sat with his stick across his knees and his collar up. He looked better than he had a week before. He looked as if he had slept one night out of the seven.

"Tell me where we are," he said. "You keep a list. Read it to me."

So she did, because it was easier to say it as a list.

"Urania made a vow at the dig, over my hand. If I won't listen to them she'll give it back to me herself. As the last thing she does." Amy looked at the sea. "Nobody will tell me what that means. Clio won't. You won't. Nobody spells it out."

Scott said nothing.

"The giant has Sofia Andreou's paper in the dean's office. If he signs it he gets a roof and a name, and nobody comes up the road with shields. If he doesn't, the security firm comes back, and they bring more. They told the dean they'd bring more." She turned a page she didn't need to turn. "And the man who knows which floodlight my cave was under wants to sit and watch him heal."

"And you?"

"I'm hoping for an explanation I can publish." She closed the notebook. "And I'm afraid I'm ill. That's what I'm afraid of. A tumour, or something in my blood, that makes me see fires and hear dead women on recordings. I looked it up. There are things that do that."

"There are," Scott agreed.

He was quiet for a while. A gull went along the wall and settled, and walked, and went up again.

"I've known a great many women like Urania," he said at last, "and none of them made a vow lightly. The old ones least of all. They don't have anything left to make one with except themselves." He looked at her sideways. "I'd listen to her, Amy. If I were you."

"I believe in numbers," Amy said. "That's all I've got. And the numbers are bad."

"Then tell me this." Scott turned the stick slowly on his knees, the way he did when he was asking a question in the lecture hall that he meant to leave hanging. "What number would make you believe?"

She thought about it honestly, because he had asked her honestly. She thought about the scanner's weights and the forty seconds of the pelt and the ninety of his stick, and the bag under the glass jar.

"I'll know it," she said, "when I measure it."

He smiled at that, tiredly, and didn't argue.

That night she didn't look at the phone until she had brushed her teeth and set the alarm and turned the light out. Then she looked, because she couldn't not.

At 23:12 the log had written the word again, mother?, as it had every hour for two days.

Under it, for the first time, on a line of its own, was her name.

amy?
