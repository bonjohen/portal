Episode 7: Up You Come

Hiroshima, the sixth of August 1945, a quarter past eight in the morning. Polyhymnia sat in a temple garden near the river, where an old woman came every day before work to stand with her hands together and say nothing.

Polyhymnia was the Muse of that kind of nothing. Hymns were hers, and the hush that comes before a hymn, the stillness a person keeps when they hope to be heard by something larger. The world kept less of it every year, and she came to places like this garden to warm herself at one old woman's silence.

The old woman closed her eyes. Polyhymnia closed hers.

The light came from everywhere at once, white, and without a sound. The garden was gone in it, and the old woman with it. Polyhymnia felt the silence of a whole city given to her in one breath, more silence than anyone had ever offered her, and it was the wrong kind. She couldn't hold it. She lay down on the hot stones and slept, and her sleep had no dreams in it.

Something else was in the light. Where the heat stood thickest, a woman got to her feet who had not been there a moment before. She was young, and her eyes were the colour of burned paper. She didn't weep. She looked at the ruin the way a girl looks at a clock she has just taken to pieces, wanting to know how it was made.

That evening the sisters came through the smoke and found Polyhymnia asleep on the stones, and they couldn't wake her. Melpomene knew someone else had been there, and they searched the burned streets for her for nine days. In the light of the bomb Polyhymnia slept and a new Muse was born, and then she was nowhere to be seen.

At the dig north of the canal the noon sun sat on the trench and didn't move. Amy Doukas stood at the edge of the deep sondage with a drawing board under her arm. Three metres below her the crew were taking out the last of the fill in buckets.

She had been standing still so long that when Yannis Petrakis came up behind her he stopped short, as if she had stepped out of the spoil heap.

"You," he said. "Good. The section drawings. I want them by the end of the week."

"I want to see the find before it's packed."

"Everybody wants things. The university is sending its own people. Nothing in that cave gets touched until they come, and when they come it goes in their van, sealed, to their lab. That's the letter. I didn't write it."

"I found the fissure."

"You did. It's in the report, and your name's spelled right." He said it as a complaint, which was how he said everything. "A lifetime of finding pots where I expected pots, and now I get a letter."

Amy looked across the trench at the cave mouth, where the tarpaulin moved a little in the hot wind. She thought of the clay, which had been warm when it had no business being warm. It was the best thing she had ever found, and they were taking it from her by letter.

In the trench somebody yelped.

Irini, a first-year who had been on the crew a week, had gone down on one knee at the bottom of the sondage, where the floor was soft. She was laughing at herself, the way people do before they know whether something is hurt.

Stavros Petridis got to her before anyone else had moved. His face had gone the dry, even red it went by midday, because his skin couldn't sweat. The thermometer stuck up out of his shirt pocket.

"Up you come," he said, and took her by the wrist.

He pulled the way you'd pull a full bucket off the bottom, one long cheerful haul, and Irini came up out of the mud, over the step and onto her feet on the baulk beside Amy, all in one go. He was already laughing, saying she'd need a winch next time.

Amy was looking at Irini's face. It had gone white around the mouth. She held her wrist against her chest and said nothing. Then she let go of it, and Amy saw the marks, four of them, red at first and then darker as she watched, coming up under the skin where his fingers had been.

Stavros had gone back to the buckets, whistling, and he didn't see her face as the bruise came up.

"Are you all right?" Amy asked.

"It's fine." Irini pulled her sleeve down over it. "He didn't mean anything. He's just strong."

She went off to the water tank. Amy stood on the baulk with her drawing board and found she couldn't let it go. Everyone on the crew knew about Stavros's nerves. He had told them himself on the first night, grinning. She had filed it as a fact about him, not about the people around him.

She climbed down the ladder.

"Stavros."

"Doukas! Come to dig at last?"

"Irini's wrist. You've bruised it. Badly, I think."

He stopped. For a moment his face did nothing at all, the joke gone out of it and nothing yet in its place. Then he turned his right hand over and looked at it, as if it might tell him.

"Did I?" He laughed, once, short. "Of course I did. Thanks, Doukas. Really. I'll go and say sorry." He took a pen from beside the thermometer and wrote on the back of his hand. Amy read it upside down: GRIP.

"You write it down," she said.

"You can't feel it, so you have to look at it." He said it like a line learned by heart. "My mother's rule. I've got a whole list, mostly about my feet." He grinned at her. "I'm meant to look at other people as well. I forget. That's the trouble with looking, you have to remember to do it."

He went up the ladder two rungs at a time, favouring nothing, though Amy had watched him drag his left leg at the end of every day that week.

She stayed at the bottom of the trench in the heat. An hour ago she had wanted one thing, to be the one who opened the clay. She still wanted it. But something had been added to it without her asking, and for her it was an odd thing to want: to keep an eye on a boy who couldn't keep one on himself.

Above her Petrakis shouted that the section drawings would not draw themselves. She went up to do them.

In the last summer of the nineteen-sixties, in a museum storeroom where nobody came in August, Euterpe sang her last song.

She was the Muse of music, of the song a person makes because something in them has to come out as sound. She had been loud once, in every kitchen and on every ship. Now songs came out of boxes, the same song at the same moment in ten thousand rooms, made to be bought and made to be over, and each year there was less of her.

She knew it was the last one. Her sisters had begged her to come to the fire so they could be with her, and she hadn't gone. She didn't want them to watch.

The storeroom was dark and smelled of dust and camphor. On a shelf at the back stood a crate with its lid off, and in the crate lay something made of wood, older than the building and older than the city around it, with a card tied to it in a careful hand. She put her hand on the wood. It answered her touch with a small sound, the way very old wood sometimes will.

She sang. It wasn't a long song. She sang it low and close to the wood, the way you sing to a child who is nearly asleep, and as she sang she grew thinner, until the light from under the door came through her.

A song goes nowhere. That is what a song is: it's in the air, and then the air is still, and the song is gone. Every song Euterpe had ever sung had gone nowhere, and that was right.

This one went somewhere. When the last note left her it didn't spread into the dark of the room and thin away; it went into the wood, the way water goes into dry ground, and it stayed.

In the morning a porter came in to sweep. On the shelf at the back the crate stood with its lid off, and as he passed it he thought he heard someone humming, very quietly, a tune he didn't know. He decided it was the pipes.
