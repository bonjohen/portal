Episode 42: What the girl measured

On Saturday morning a young woman came out across the stubble toward the terrace wall with a canvas bag on her shoulder, and stopped a spear's length from him.

Alcyoneus had watched her come all the way from the road. She walked the way a man walks a field he means to plough, looking at the ground before she trusted a foot to it. She was small and dark, with her hair tied back hard, and her right hand was grey and smooth and didn't move the way her left one did. He had seen her before. She had been in the white room when he opened his eyes on the table, one of three small shapes under the lights, and the only one of them that hadn't backed away.

She said something in the speech the mortals used now, slowly and carefully. He shook his head. She took a breath and tried again, and this time it was the old tongue, or near it, stiff and correct, the way a child recites a lesson to a schoolmaster.

"My name is Amy Doukas. I found you, in the cave, in the clay. A woman on the bus said there was a giant asleep on the terraces." She swallowed. "They say I lost you. May I measure you?"

He laughed so loudly that birds went up off the olive nets.

"Measure me," he said. "Your kind always wanted to."

She took a yellow tape out of the bag, the kind that rolls back into its own case, and a thermometer, and a notebook with a pencil tied to it by a string. She asked him to stand against the wall, and he stood. She asked him to lay his hand flat on the stones and spread the fingers, and she ran the tape from his thumb to his smallest finger and wrote the number down. She held the thermometer under his jaw, and frowned at it, and wrote that down too.

He watched her face while she worked. He had seen the same look on his followers once, the men who came out on the plain at night after the dogs had finished with him and gathered him up. They must have measured him too, for the clay. They had wanted to get it right.

"It can't be," she said under her breath.

"What can't?"

"Your shoulders." She showed him the page as if he could read it, rows of small neat figures. "On the table you were four centimetres narrower across here. That was two weeks ago. Grown men don't get wider in two weeks."

"I am on my own ground," he said. "It feeds me."

She wrote that down as well, word for word, and drew a line under it that he took for doubt.

Then she asked him to walk across the field at his ordinary pace, and he walked, and she went along beside him half running, counting under her breath. At the far side she wrote a number and stood looking at it, and said again that it couldn't be.

They were coming back along the edge of the stubble when a dog came out of the farmyard below the terraces. It was big and yellow and it came up the slope barking, straight at them, low to the ground.

She stopped dead.

She didn't run, and she didn't lift her arms or call out. She stood exactly where she was with the notebook held against her chest, and the dog went past her as if she were a fence post and came on at him. He caught it by the scruff and set it down again facing the other way, and it ran off yelping, looking back once over its shoulder at the empty air beside him.

He turned to the girl and found he had to look twice.

While she stood still his eyes slid off her the way rain slides off oiled skin. He knew where she was, a spear's length away, and he couldn't keep his gaze on her. He had felt that only once before, long ago, at the feasts before the war, when the Muses walked among the guests and nobody saw them who wasn't meant to.

Then she moved, and she was only a girl with a notebook again.

"That is a gift," he said. "A gift of their kind. Who gave it to you?"

"Nobody gave it to me." She picked a yellow hair off her sleeve. "It's called keeping still. Dogs chase what runs."

"Dogs see what is there."

"Then it didn't see me," she said, as if that settled it, and opened the notebook again.

He looked at her bent over her figures, and something in it made him angry and something else made him want to laugh. She had a tape. She thought the tape would tell her what he was.

"Come," he said. "I will give you something to write."

It was past noon when they came to the edge of the plain, where the stubble gave out. A grey rock stood up out of the ground there like the crown of a buried hill, taller than two men. He walked to it and stood with his feet apart, and reached down into his shoulders for the blow that had split hills against the gods.

It came. He felt it gather, a weight that had nothing to do with his thin arms, the way thunder gathers behind a mountain before you hear it. He struck the crown of the rock with the side of his fist.

The outcrop split from crown to foot. The crack of it went out across the plain and came back off the hills, and the two halves leaned apart and settled, and dust came up between them and blew away toward the sea.

Behind him he heard a small click.

He struck again at once, at one of the halves, to show her it was no trick. His arm was only an arm. His fist hit the stone and stopped, and the stone didn't notice.

So he waited, as he had waited on the plain against the gods. He stood with his hands at his sides while the weight that had gone out of his shoulders came back into them, slowly, the way water comes back into a well after a bucket. He had never counted it. He had never needed to. On the plain there had always been a brother beside him to strike while he stood and waited.

When it was there again he said, "Now."

Click.

She wrote the number down. He asked her what it was.

"It's a number," she said. "I'll need to see it again before I tell anyone what it is."

"You have watched a hill split," he said, "and you write down a number."

"I watched a rock split." She didn't look up. "Anybody can say they watched a rock split. Nobody believes anybody. They'll believe this, if it's the same every time." She closed the notebook on the pencil. "You don't understand that."

"No," he said. "It is you who don't."

They walked back toward the road together. At the wall she took a small flat thing of black glass out of her pocket and held it out to him, a phone, she called it, an old one of hers. It answered questions, she said, if you asked it aloud. He turned it over in his long fingers. It was warm from her pocket.

At the road she stopped.

"I won't tell them where you are," she said. "On the campus. Nobody there believes me anyway." She looked up at him, squinting into the low sun. "What did you wake up for?"

"Melpomene," he said.

She opened the notebook and wrote it down. She didn't stop to ask him how it went. She wrote it in the old letters, fast, without a pause between them, as if she had written that name before.
