Episode 84: Ninety

On Sunday afternoon the drones came down off the roofs.

They had sat up there since dawn, along the ridge of the library and the gutter of the conservation building, a dozen grey shapes no bigger than dinner plates, with their small lenses turned toward the old block by the sea. Professor Scott had watched them from his window most of the day. Every hour or so one lifted, hung a moment and settled again a little nearer. The three delivery robots from the night before had gone at first light. The drones had not.

At half past three he saw Zoe come out of the conservation building with her bag on her shoulder and start across the quad toward him.

She had come that afternoon, as she meant to every afternoon now, to read the hygrometer in his cabinet, because the lyre was in it and she trusted no one else's numbers. He had told her on the phone at noon to wait until dark, and she had said that she had read the forecast, and that the forecast said drones. Then she had rung off.

He was down the stairs and through the long corridor of the old block before he had decided to go, and out under the arched gate onto the path with the stick in his hand.

She was halfway across. The first drone dropped off the library gutter behind her, and then the second, and the rest came down after them one by one off the roofs, not fast, falling into a loose line above the paving as if each had waited for the last to find its place. They came down low over her head. Zoe didn't run. She walked faster, with her bag held against her side, and looked straight ahead at Scott under the arch.

The stick moved.

He felt it pull in his hand toward the machines that were hunting her, the old warmth coming up the shaft into his palm the way it came when the thing wanted to go. It had wanted to go on Friday, on the harbour wall, and he had held it. It wanted to go now. And he couldn't tell any more, standing under the arch with his heart going, how much of the wanting was the stick's and how much was his.

That was the danger. If even a part of it was his, the throw would be wrong.

He made himself empty.

He had learned it long before there were locks or guns, alone, in the first years, when he still thought he could make the thing behave. You took the anger out of your arm first. You set it down beside you like a cup, still full, and left it there. You took the love out last, because it was the heavier, and set it down beside the anger. Then there was nothing in the arm at all but where the thing had to go.

The nearest drone had come down to the height of Zoe's head, a stride in front of her. Its lens turned to her face.

He threw.

It went out of his hand like a breath going out of him. He didn't see it fly. He saw the drone come apart in the air in front of Zoe, all at once, in a burst of grey plastic and small bright pieces, and the pieces rained down on the paving at her feet. The black stick lay among them, a long way off, quite still.

Then his hand was empty.

He knew how long it would stay empty. He had known it to the second for two thousand years. Ninety. Once the stick had gone it would not come back to him for ninety, however hard he reached, and he would not insult it by reaching. The girl with the stopwatch had timed it for him in November and been pleased to find him exact.

One.

The other drones had stopped. They hung where they were above the quad, a loose grey line, and none of them came lower. Zoe stepped round the pieces and came on, fast now, and he took her by the arm under the arch and turned her toward the door.

Nine. Ten.

"Inside," he said. "Up the stairs. Don't stop."

"You've dropped your..."

"I know. Go."

She went in ahead of him, and he followed her into the long corridor of the old block with nothing in his hands at all.

He had walked it a thousand times. It ran the whole length of the building on the ground floor, stone-flagged and cold, with tall windows down the sea side and the doors of empty offices down the other. He had never noticed how long it was. He walked it counting, at the pace of a man who would not run in front of a woman he loved, and every one of the tall windows had a drone outside it.

Twenty-two. Twenty-three.

They hung at the glass as he passed, one at each window, keeping pace with him on the far side of it. Not touching it. Their lenses turned to follow him from pane to pane. Zoe was ahead of him at the turn of the stair, and she stopped and looked back, and he shook his head, and she went on up.

Forty.

He thought about Odysseus, whom he had never met, on a beach in the morning. He thought about nothing, as hard as he could, because the anger was waiting where he had set it down, and he could feel it there, full to the brim, at his elbow.

Sixty-one. Sixty-two.

At the foot of the stair the last window of the corridor looked out on the path where it began. Outside it the drone hung lower than the others had, at the height of his face, and he stopped and looked at it, and it looked at him.

Seventy.

He climbed. His knees were old today, whatever the rest of him was. On the landing he heard his own door shut above him, and the key turn in it from inside, and something in his chest let go a little.

Eighty-eight. Eighty-nine.

At ninety it was in his palm again, heavy and dead, as if it had never left. He stood on the landing with his hand closed on it and breathed. Below him, along the whole length of the corridor, the drones hung at the windows and watched the place where he had been.

Zoe opened the door a crack, saw him, and opened it the rest of the way.

They didn't speak much that evening. She read the hygrometer in the cabinet and wrote the number in her log with the time, and then she sat at his desk and wrote the rest of it, the throw and the pieces and the corridor, in her plain report words, because she couldn't think what else to do with it. He made tea and didn't drink it.

At ten his phone rang. It was Sotiris Malamas, from the third floor of the building with no name, and he didn't say good evening.

"I've been through the logs on every drone we lost today," he said. His voice was flat and tired, the voice of a man who had been through them twice. "One unit, destroyed on the quad at fifteen thirty-six. I've checked the whole fleet back to Wednesday." He stopped. "Professor, nobody on the programme gave those drones an order. Nobody. Not once."
