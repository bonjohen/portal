Episode 58: Each on its own clock

On Thursday morning in the secure lab, with the long table empty and Lefteris eating his breakfast at the console, Amy Doukas made a list of the things she could do that she couldn't explain.

She made it the way she made every list, on a clean page, with a dash in front of each line and the date at the top.

The stillness came first. In the dark at the dig, on the last night, she had stood among the smugglers while they loaded the truck, and their torches had gone over her and their eyes with them. In the pines on the first of the month the guard's torch had passed over her twice from eight metres away. On the plain the yellow dog had run past her as if she were a fence post. And a week ago, in the car park, a guard had looked straight at her under a lamp and away again.

Then the knowing, which she had never written down before, because it was small. Sometimes, when somebody dropped something, a trowel in a trench or a pen in a lecture, she knew where it would land a breath before it landed. Not where it would fall. Where it would stop.

She looked at the page for a long time. It was a short list. Clio would have called it the gifts of her sisters, and Amy called it a list.

"Lefteris," she said. "Will you help me with something stupid?"

"Always," said Lefteris, with his mouth full.

She went to the far end of the lab, by the steel bench, and stood with her back to the wall, and asked him to look at her. He looked. She went still, the way she went still at the dig, letting her breath go shallow and her hands go quiet, and his eyes went off her to the door and came back puzzled, and went off again.

"Where are you?" he said. "You were just there."

"I'm still here."

He laughed nervously. "That's horrible. Do it again."

She stepped away from the wall and back, and stood, and reached for it at once.

It didn't come. He looked straight at her, a young woman in a grey jumper against a white wall, and said, "Well, yes, there you are."

She tried again a minute later, and he saw her. She tried every half minute, reaching for it as hard as she could, and he saw her every time. Then, after a wait, it came back, as easily as it had come at first, and his eyes slid off her again. She had the stopwatch on its cord round her neck. She clicked it.

They did it for an hour. However hard she reached for the stillness, it wouldn't come twice inside the same few minutes, and when the minutes were up it came without her reaching for it at all. She wrote the minutes down. They were the same every time, to the second.

Then she had Lefteris drop pens. He dropped them off the console from all heights, laughing, and the first time she said, before it hit the floor, "By the chair leg," and it stopped against the chair leg. The second time she didn't know at all. Nor the third. And then, after a different wait, longer than the stillness's, she knew again, and said, "Under the bench," and it rolled under the bench.

Amy sat down on her stool and looked at the two numbers.

The pelt turned a blow and then wouldn't for forty seconds. The professor's stick came to his hand and then wouldn't for ninety. And her own two small strange things, the stillness and the knowing, each came back to her only on its own clock.

She had always written the pelt's forty seconds down as a property of the material. She had no material to blame for herself.

She turned back through the notebook to October, to the page where she had first written forty point nought under the pelt, and underlined it. Then she turned forward again to today's page and didn't underline anything. A number was only a number until there was a reason under it. She had a column of numbers now, and no column of reasons.

"What is it?" Lefteris said, watching her.

"I don't know," said Amy, and wrote that down too.

In the afternoon she walked back toward the graduate block the long way, past the conservation building, because her hip was stiff and the long way was flat.

It was halfway along the building that her hand began to ache.

Not the grey hand. The other one, the one that wasn't there. She hadn't felt it in years. For the first months after the accident the hand she had lost at seventeen had ached every night inside the socket, the fingers clenched on nothing, and the doctors had called it phantom pain and given her exercises with a mirror, and slowly it had gone away. Now it was back, all at once, an ache in a palm that didn't exist.

And it pointed.

She stopped on the path. The ache was strongest on one side of the missing hand, the side toward the building, and when she turned to face the building it filled the whole palm. It pulled toward the long single-storey block with its loading bay, toward one end of it, where she knew the store was, and the old lyre on its foam on Zoe Lambrinou's bench.

Amy turned her back on the door and walked away. The ache pulled the other way, back over her shoulder, steady as a compass needle that swings round to north however you turn the box.

There was an old woman on the bench outside the building's door with a rolled paper in her lap.

"Sit down, Amy," said Clio.

"I'm going home."

"It's the lyre," Clio said. She didn't raise her voice. "Wanting the hand that can play it. It has wanted it since the day you came onto this campus, and it has been waiting for you to have the hand." She looked at the grey fingers and the crooked wrist. "My sister has vowed you'll have it, if you won't heed us."

"I have phantom limb pain," said Amy. "It's very common. It comes back with stress, and I've had a concussion and no sleep."

"Yes," said Clio. "Very common."

"What does it want back?" Amy said. "For every song. You said it would want something."

Clio didn't answer that, any more than she had by the fire. Her hands had closed on the scroll.

Amy walked on. She felt the ache all the way across the quad, pulling back toward the building, and it didn't let go until she was inside the graduate block with the door shut.

At dusk Urania came again. She was standing by the window when Amy came out of the shower, as if she had been there some time.

"No," said Amy, before she could ask. It was the third evening. "I'm going to prove it, you know. All of it. That it's a disorder. Something in my head, from the accident, or from the fall. There'll be a name for it, and a scan that shows it."

Urania looked at her with her soft, doubtful eyes, and something in them was very sad.

"Then you had better prove it quickly," she said.

When she had gone Amy sat on the bed and opened her notebook to the list, and the two numbers under it, and her phone buzzed on the blanket. It was a message from Professor Scott, one line, with no greeting.

Come to my rooms tomorrow evening. There is something I should have told you weeks ago.
