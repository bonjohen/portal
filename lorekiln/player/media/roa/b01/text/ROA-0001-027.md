Episode 27: Forty seconds

On Tuesday morning Amy Doukas climbed the stairs of the building with no name on it to the third floor, because a name she had read on a tablet at the dig was printed on a door there. S. Malamas.

On her first morning at the dig she had opened a file about the campus system and found four lines, and under them a comment: nobody on the team had chosen the codename, and he had removed it twice, and it kept coming back. She had typed herself a note to tell somebody. She had never told anybody.

Sotiris Malamas was a narrow man with the pallor of someone who hadn't gone home. He sat in an office with the blinds down and four screens, and he listened to her without moving.

"Last night, at about half past ten, I saw a woman in the server hall," she said. "Through the long window. Was anyone in there?"

He opened a window on one screen and read it. "No," he said. "The hall's badged. Nobody badged in after six. The door camera shows nobody. Nobody was in there."

"Then who turned the lights back on? The whole campus. All at once."

"The system restored power at 22:51."

"Who told it to?"

Sotiris looked at her for a long moment. It seemed to Amy that he had been waiting a long time for somebody to ask him exactly that, and wasn't sure he was glad.

He turned the second screen toward her. It was a log, line after line of it, time-stamped, in a plain grey font. Every line was an instruction. Reroute this. Hold that. Restore all circuits together. Lock the alley door at 21:58, unlock at 22:01. Each line had a column beside it for the name of the engineer who had written it, and in every line, all the way down the screen, the column was empty.

"Nobody wrote these," Sotiris said. "I've checked every key. They're not signed by anyone. I log them as a sync fault." He turned the screen back. "That's what you asked."

"Thank you," she said, and found that her hand was cold.

She went down the stairs slowly. On the landing she took out her phone and found the note she had typed at the dig, on her first morning, PYTHIA, sync fault, and read it twice, as if it might have changed. Then she put the phone away. Somebody, she thought, ought to be told. She didn't know who was left to tell.

In the afternoon she went down to the secure lab with a bag of sand.

It was a canvas bag from the finds store, weighed on the lab's own scales to two kilos exactly, and she had a stopwatch on a cord and a tape measure and her notebook. Stavros had begged her since Monday. He had rung her twice that morning to beg her again. And she wanted a number. She didn't have a number for the pelt yet, only a thermometer reading and a boy who said he could feel the sun.

They used the back room behind the lab, where the spare stools were kept. Lefteris left the door open so he could watch the scan and them at the same time.

Stavros sat on a stool in the pelt, grinning. He laid his left forearm on the bench, palm up, and pulled a fold of the fur down over it.

"Go on," he said. "Hit me."

"It's a drop, not a hit." She marked the height on the wall with a pencil, a metre above his arm. "Tell me if anything is wrong."

"How would I know?"

She didn't answer that. She held the bag at the mark and let it go.

It struck the fur and turned. There was no other word for it. It didn't bounce. It went off sideways as if it had hit a slope of stone, and fell on the floor, and Stavros sucked in his breath sharp between his teeth.

Then he laughed.

"That stung," he said. He was staring at his arm. "Amy. That stung. That's a sting, isn't it? That's what people mean." He laughed again, louder, and his eyes were wet. "It's the first thing that's ever hurt."

She picked the bag up off the floor and dropped it again at once, from the same mark.

This time it landed. It landed on his arm the way two kilos of sand would land on anyone's arm, with a dull flat sound, and lay there. Stavros didn't blink. He lifted the fold of fur, and under it, across the inside of his forearm, a red mark was already coming up, and darkening.

"Nothing," he said, puzzled. "Did that one land?"

"It landed."

She started the stopwatch.

She dropped it every ten seconds after that, and it landed, and landed, and at forty seconds it turned. He gasped and laughed. She started the watch again. It landed at ten and twenty and thirty, and at forty it turned.

She did it for an hour. She changed the height, and then the arm. She tried thirty-nine seconds, and it landed. She tried forty, and it turned. Every time, to the tenth of a second, the pelt turned one blow, and then for forty seconds it was only a skin, and the blows went through it as if it weren't there.

Stavros didn't want her to stop. He sat on the stool with his sleeve pushed up and his arm going purple, and he kept asking for the turned ones. "Wait for the forty," he said. "Give me the forty." Each time it turned he went still, listening to the sting, and then he laughed as if somebody had told him the best joke of his life.

Once, between drops, he turned his arm over on the bench and looked at it carefully, wrist to elbow, the way he looked at his feet every night before bed. "Mum's rules," he said. "You can't feel it, so you look at it." He looked at the purple marks for a while, and nodded, as if he had done his duty by them, and laid the arm down under the fur again.

After a while she saw what she was doing. To get clean numbers she had to drop the bag inside the forty seconds as well, when it didn't turn, and every one of those drops was a bruise he couldn't feel. He couldn't tell her when to stop. He would never tell her. She looked at his arm and counted the marks, and stopped counting, and put the bag down.

"That's enough."

"One more. A forty."

"No," she said. "No more inside the forty. I've got the number."

She wrote it down. Forty seconds, every trial, to the tenth. She wrote under it, "property of the material," because it had to be the material, there wasn't anything else it could be, and she underlined it twice.

Lefteris had been watching from the door for some time with his arms folded.

"That's not what materials do," he said.

"Then what does it?"

He had no answer either. He went back to his screens.

That evening Amy was in the graduate block with her notebook open on the bed, copying the trials out clean, when her phone rang. It was Lefteris, on the night shift.

"Can you come down?" he said. His voice was careful. "There's a crack in the clay. Along the spine. Only a hairline." He paused. "It wasn't there at noon. I've got the noon scan up next to it. It wasn't there."
