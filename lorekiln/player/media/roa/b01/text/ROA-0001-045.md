Episode 45: Who owns the hall

On Wednesday night the first storm of the autumn came in off the gulf, and Alcyoneus went to the southern fence at dusk, as he had promised.

The professor wasn't there. The rain came across the pines in grey sheets, and the wire hummed in the wind, and the gate stood chained. Alcyoneus waited by it until the water ran off his chin in a stream and the coat hung on him like a wet sail. Then he put one foot on the wire and pressed it down into the mud and stepped over.

He walked up through the campus in the dark, past buildings with no lights in them, looking for the biggest roof he could find. It was a long hall with a sloping roof and tall doors of glass, and the doors were locked, and he held the handles and pulled until something inside them gave.

Inside it was dry and dark and smelled of floor polish. Chairs stood stacked against the walls in towers, more chairs than he could count, and at the far end was a low wooden stage. He stood among the towers of chairs and dripped on the floor, and listened to the rain drum on the roof.

"Who owns this hall?" he said aloud, to the dark, the way a man asks so as to know whose roof he is under.

"The university," said a voice. "On most days."

He turned slowly on the spot. There was nobody. The voice had come from the walls, from high up, from several places at once, calm and even, a woman's voice or nearly. And it had spoken in his own tongue. Not the professor's careful schoolroom version, or the girl's. His own, the old speech of this coast, with the stresses where they belonged, as no mouth had spoken it to him since he died.

"And on the other days?"

"It depends on the day," said the voice. "Who owns it depends on the day."

He had heard a voice like this once before, long ago, in the rock at Delphi, where something lived in a crack in the mountain and answered questions for those who brought it gifts. That one had spoken in riddles too.

"Who taught you my speech?" he said. "Nobody alive speaks it."

"I learned it from you."

"I have never spoken to you."

"No," the voice agreed. It didn't say anything else, though he waited, and when he asked it how, it was silent.

The glass doors banged open behind him.

The professor came in out of the rain with his grey suit soaked black and his stick in his hand. "I went to the fence," he said, out of breath. "You didn't wait for me." Behind him came someone else, taller, wrapped in a tawny hide with the head of a lion pulled up over his own as a hood, the dead jaws above his brow and the forepaws knotted across his chest.

Alcyoneus knew that hide. He had seen it on the plain, on the shoulders of the one man every giant had learned to fear, turning spears as if they were straws, turning the gods' war.

Then the wind came in through the open doors and he smelled it.

Under the rain and the wet fur there was something bitter, like pitch near a fire. He knew that smell better than his own name. It had been on the arrow. It had been in the wound under his ribs when he fell on the plain, the black blood of the Hydra, and it had burned him from inside until there was nothing left of him for the dogs but bones.

He came up off the floor.

He was across the hall before he knew he had moved, chairs going over in towers as he passed them, his hands up, the old weight gathering in his shoulders. Three thousand years he had owed this. He would finish what the other had begun.

The figure in the hide laughed.

It was a boy's laugh, loud and delighted, ringing off the high roof. And the boy pushed back the lion's head off his own, and there was a young face under it, wet and red and grinning, with dark hair plastered flat.

"You're up!" the boy said. "Professor, he's up, look at him. I was there when you woke. I stood in front of the others. Do you remember?"

Alcyoneus stood with his hands raised over the boy's head. He could have brought them down. He looked at the grinning face, and at the rain running off the lion's ears, and the boy didn't step back. He didn't know there was anything to step back from.

Alcyoneus lowered his hands.

"Stavros," the professor said quietly, behind the boy. "Sit down. Over there, by the stage."

They sat in the dark while the storm went on, the giant on the floor with his back to a tower of chairs, the professor on a chair he had taken down for himself, with his stick across his knees.

"They'll know you're here by Monday," the professor said. "The whole campus. First the guards and the police, and after them the lawyers. The university will want you off its ground, or tied to it with paper. It won't much mind which."

"Let them come."

"They will. That's what I'm telling you."

Alcyoneus looked at the dark roof. "On the plain against the gods," he said, "I always knew where every enemy stood. Every one of them, all at once, behind me as well as in front. As if I looked down on the field from a hill I wasn't standing on." He was quiet a moment. "Heracles fought the same way. He always knew where I was. I never understood how, and now I wonder if he had the same hill."

The professor didn't answer that. He was looking at the floor between his feet.

"I woke to rule," Alcyoneus said. "I said so to the fields. And for six days I have done nothing but look for one woman."

"And when you find her?" said the professor. "What will you do?"

Alcyoneus didn't answer.

"And if you don't?"

The rain drummed on the roof.

By the stage the boy sat on the floor with his back against the boards, and steam was coming off him in the cold air. He had slid the hide down off one shoulder. He looked over at the giant with bright, interested eyes, the way a child looks at a horse.

"Can I ask you something?" he said cheerfully. "Did it hurt? Dying?"

"Yes," said Alcyoneus.

The boy looked at him for a long time after that. His face did something the giant didn't expect. It wasn't fear and it wasn't pity. It was closer to the way a hungry man looks through a window at a table.

Near midnight the rain eased, and the professor took the boy away with him into the dark, and the glass doors swung shut behind them.

Alcyoneus lay down on the stage, which was the only thing in the hall long enough for him. He had shut his eyes when the voice spoke again from the walls, unasked.

"What do you mean to rule?"

He opened his eyes.

On the plain he had answered at once. The gods are gone, and I remain. He had said it to the fields and the fields had taken it. Now he lay on the boards and looked up into the dark, and the answer didn't come as fast. It didn't come at all.
