Episode 67: Filed a year before

On Thursday morning Zoe Lambrinou sat down to finish the gilt apple's condition report, and went to the store's lending files for the museum's last loan agreement, to copy its insurance wording.

The lending files lived in a grey steel cabinet by the loading bay, four drawers of folders going back thirty years. Anything that came onto the campus on loan was in there, and anything the campus had lent out, and a good deal of paper that had nowhere else to go. Everything the Vardis Foundation had ever paid for went into the bottom drawer, because the bursar's office had once decided that a gift was a kind of loan.

She found the museum's agreement. Behind it, filed in the wrong year, was a folder with the Foundation's letterhead on the front.

It was a letter to the university, two pages long, signed by the Foundation's secretary. It asked, very politely, that the university apply for a permit to excavate on the slope north of the canal at Nea Potidaia, on a strip of scrub above the shore within sight of the bridge, and offered to pay for the season. Clipped behind it was a survey map. On the map someone had drawn a trench in red ink, long and narrow, running along the slope toward a grey outcrop of rock.

The letter was dated a year before the dig.

Zoe knew the dig. Everyone on the campus knew it now. She had seen Petrakis's site plan pinned up in the dean's corridor in October, after the find was stolen, with the long trench and its two floodlights. She laid the red line on the old map against it in her head. It ran in the same place, to the metre.

She read it twice, the way she read every file, slowly, from the letterhead to the signature. Then she took it to the copier by the door and made one copy of each page, and put the folder back in the bottom drawer, in the wrong year, where it had been. She folded the copies into her bag.

She didn't tell anybody. She couldn't think who she would tell. Scott, perhaps, but Scott was on the shore with a giant who wouldn't eat. Sofia Andreou worked for the university, and the university took the Foundation's money. And the Foundation was a polite old man with a gold ring, who had asked her on Tuesday how long the lyre had been on the campus and said yes as if he had known.

In the afternoon she watched the quad from the store's north window.

It began, as everything did that week, with words. The night guards had come on early, five or six of them in their blue shirts, and on the paths across the quad they had met a crowd of the dig students coming back from a lecture. Zoe couldn't hear what was said. She could see it was about the giant, because one of the students kept pointing south, toward the shore, and one of the guards kept shaking his head. Then a guard put his hand flat on a student's chest and pushed.

In a minute it was shoving, and in two it was more than shoving.

At the front of it, longest, was Stavros Petridis.

She knew him by the lion's pelt. He wore it with the head pulled up over his own and the forepaws knotted on his chest, and he stood at the front of the students with his arms held wide, and the guards hit him. Zoe saw a baton come down on his shoulder and stop on the fur as if it had struck stone. She saw him laugh. The next one didn't stop. It went into the fur like a stick into a sack of grain, and he rocked under it and didn't seem to notice. Another blow came, and another, and he stood there grinning under them, as if how long he could stand it were the whole point of being there.

Then something went wrong further back. A student went down on the path with her arm under her, and the sound she made came through the window glass.

The fight stopped. Somebody ran for the surgery. Stavros was still laughing, alone, until he saw the girl's arm, and stopped.

At dusk Scott rang.

"I'm going down to him," he said. He sounded as if he hadn't slept. "Amy's coming. Will you come? And bring bread, if you have any. He won't eat it. But I'd like him to see that someone brought it."

She had half a loaf in her bag. She took it.

They drove south along the coast road in Scott's old car, past the shuttered beach towns, and left it on the verge above the theatre. Zoe had never been there. Through a gap in the wall the broken seats went down toward the sea in a curve, grey and overgrown with weeds, and below them a cracked stone stage, and below that a goat path ran down the cliff to a strip of shingle.

The giant was on the shingle. He sat with his back to the theatre and his face to the sea, a long dark shape with his knees drawn up, as if he had been there for a year.

At the top of the goat path the night guards were already shouting.

There were three of them, sent down from the campus to move him on, and four or five of the dig students who had followed them in a car, and they had stopped on the path above the shingle and turned on each other. The guards said it was their job. The students said it was his shore. Scott went down past them without a word, and Amy after him with the torch, and Zoe came last with the bread under her arm.

Someone threw a stone.

She didn't see who. It was meant for a guard, and it went wide of him, and it went on down the slope in the dusk and struck the giant in the mouth where he sat.

He didn't move. He didn't lift a hand to his face. Something fell from his lips onto the shingle with a small clatter, and Zoe, who had come down onto the stones by then, saw what it was. It was a tooth, as long as her finger, grey and old, lying on the stones in the last light.

The giant didn't stand. He didn't even turn.

On the path above, the shouting got worse. A guard had a student by the collar, and Amy went up two steps and put herself between them with her arms out, and the guard shoved her.

She went backwards off the path onto the rocks.

Zoe heard her head strike. She was across the shingle before she knew she had moved, and on her knees beside her, and there was blood in Amy's hair, a great deal of it, black in the torchlight. Zoe pulled the scarf off her own neck and pressed it to the cut and held it there with both hands.

Then the shouting stopped.

It stopped all at once, as if somebody had shut a door on it. Zoe looked up. On the shingle below them the giant had turned his head, for the first time since he came down to the shore, and was looking at Amy, and everyone on the path had gone still under his look like rabbits under a hawk.

He didn't say anything. He only looked at her, at the blood and the scarf, for a long moment. Then he turned his face back to the sea.

Scott helped Amy up. She was dizzy and swearing and she would need stitches, and the guards and the students went up the path together without a word to each other and drove away.

Zoe went back for the tooth. She had a sample bag in her coat pocket, as she always did, and she thought someone should keep it for him, since he wouldn't.

She went to the place where it had fallen, between two pale stones by the giant's foot. She looked, and looked again with the torch, and turned the stones over with her fingers.

The tooth wasn't there.
