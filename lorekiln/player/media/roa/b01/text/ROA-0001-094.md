Episode 94: The people near him

On Monday evening, an hour after dark, the people began to come and sit on the grass round the giant.

Alcyoneus did not ask them to. He was sitting where he had sat all day, under the secure lab's high windows, with the ground gone ordinary under him, when the first of them came down the road behind the conservation building in twos and threes, carrying blankets and bags of oranges. Students, most of them, in coats over pyjamas. A few of the night staff from the lab and the white building, still wearing their lanyards. A young technician with tired eyes, whose name the giant knew because he had sat by the long table on the nights the giant lay on it, Lefteris, who had pressed the buttons on the scanners. They sat down on the wet grass in a half ring in front of him, close but not too close, the way people sit round a fire they aren't sure of.

"What are you doing?" he said, in the modern speech.

A girl with a shaved head and a nose ring answered him. "They're selling the hall," she said. "With PYTHIA in it. With contents. You're the only thing on this campus they can't sell." She shrugged. "So we're sitting with you."

He did not know what to say to that, so he said nothing, and more of them came.

By eight there were perhaps forty. Somebody had brought a camping lamp, and its white light lay across the grass and the faces. Stavros Petridis came down the road at half past, in the lion's pelt, with the head up over his hair and the forepaws knotted on his chest and two crates of water stacked in his arms. He set them down by the giant's feet and grinned at him. His face was its ordinary colour under the lion's jaw, and the giant could smell the old bitter pitch of the venom on him even through the cold.

"Room service," Stavros said.

Amy Doukas came last, with a notebook, and sat down at the giant's left hand, so close her shoulder touched his arm. She opened the notebook on her knee and wrote the time at the top of a clean page and the number of people under it, and sat with the pen in her warm hand, watching.

The guards came at ten.

There were a dozen of them, in the security firm's black fleeces, with batons and two big torches, and Rigas walked at the front with a bruise gone yellow and purple along his jaw where the chronicler had put him down on Friday night. They lined up at the edge of the grass, on the path, a long dark row between the people and the road.

"This is university property," Rigas said. "There's a sale on it. Nobody sits here overnight. Pick up your rubbish and go back to your rooms, and nobody's name goes on a list."

Nobody moved. The girl with the nose ring laughed at him.

The giant felt it come up through both lines then, the heat that had been on the campus all winter. He had smelled it on the quad and on the shore and in the hall where he signed, a heat like a fever coming, that turned every quarrel ugly in a breath. It ran along the row of guards like a spark along a fuse, and across the grass into the people sitting round him, and he watched it go into their faces. A guard on the end slapped his baton against his palm. A boy on the grass stood up and shouted something about Rigas's mother. Somebody behind the guards' line laughed, and somebody on the grass called him a dog.

Then somebody threw a bottle.

He didn't see who. It came out of the dark on the people's side and turned over once in the lamp's light and smashed on the path at Rigas's feet, and the guards' line came forward a step all together, batons up.

Alcyoneus stood.

He came up off the grass all at once, the whole grey length of him, and the lamp threw his shadow across both lines and up the wall of the conservation building. Every face turned up to him, the students' and the guards'. And he felt the old want rise up in him, as it had risen on Sunday when the woman in the speakers spoke of kingdoms. To break the line, to walk into those dark coats and scatter them like chaff and make them kneel. To rule the land he stood on, as he had come out of the clay meaning to. He could do it. Even here, even mortal, he could do it, and nobody on that path could stop him.

And under it, where the old want had always been alone, Alcyoneus felt something new, that wanted the people near him safe.

He did not know what to call it. It was smaller than the want, and quieter, and it would not move.

He looked down. The girl with the nose ring was staring up at him with her mouth open. Lefteris had put his arm round a younger student who was shaking. Stavros had stepped in front of the crates, grinning, his fists loose, ready to be put in front of something. And at his feet Amy sat quite still with the notebook on her knee, and did not look frightened of him at all.

He walked to the guards' line.

He did it slowly, so that they would see every step coming. He stopped a pace from them. He did not lift his hands. He only stood there, with his arms at his sides and his head among the lower branches of the pine, and looked down at the row of batons.

"Strike, then," said a guard near Rigas, a big man with a shaking voice. "Go on. Swing at us. Everyone's waiting for you to."

The giant knew it. He could feel them all waiting, on both sides. The guards wanted him to swing, so that they could say afterwards that he had, and the students wanted him to swing for them. Whoever won tonight, he would be the one who had struck.

So he didn't. He stood in front of the line and let them look at him.

It took a long time. A minute, perhaps more. The guard who had spoken lowered his baton first, without seeming to know he was doing it, and looked at it in his hand as if someone had given it to him, and the man beside him lowered his. Rigas was the last, with his bruised face set hard, who said a word under his breath and turned his back on the giant and told his men to go.

They went up the road toward the quad in a loose dark knot. Nobody had been hit. The heat went out of the air behind them, as quickly as it had come.

When the giant turned round, the people on the grass were looking at him as if they had never seen him before.

He went back and sat down against the wall. After a while they came closer. Through the night, one by one, the students fell asleep against him, against his arms and his long legs, the way children fall asleep against a warm wall in the sun. The girl with the nose ring slept with her head on his knee. Lefteris slept sitting up with his back to the giant's ribs. Stavros sat awake at the edge of the lamp's light in the pelt, and once the giant saw him take the forepaws off his chest and look at his own forearms and laugh softly at what he saw there.

The giant did not sleep. He sat as still as he had once lain in the clay, so as not to wake them.

Before dawn, when the lamp had burned down to a glow and the sky over the sea was just going grey, Amy spoke beside him. He hadn't known she was awake.

"Why did you sign?" she said. "Tell me the true reason. Not the house."

He opened his mouth to tell her. He had the words ready, the glass and the window at three in the morning. He found that they were not the reason, or not the whole of it, and that he did not know what the rest of it was.

He closed his mouth again, and could not answer her.
