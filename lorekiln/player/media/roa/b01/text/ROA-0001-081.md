Episode 81: Among the boxes

Clio's tapestry, a panel left loose: a white boat on a black sea, and over it a line of small grey birds that are not birds.

On Thursday morning, the day after the signing, the campus was shut. Professor Scott came down from his rooms in the old block by the sea at seven and found the security firm's guards on every gate, two to a post, with their white van parked across the main road in. Stelios Rigas stood at the main gate in a black fleece with a clipboard, writing down the name of everyone who came near it. He was a big man with a heavy, patient face, and he looked at Scott's stick before he looked at Scott.

"Nobody who stood near the giant goes out," Rigas said. "Dean's orders. Till we know what he's done."

"He signed a paper," Scott said.

"And the lights went." Rigas wrote something down. "And the old man's out there."

Scott looked where he looked. Out over the sea, beyond the protected strip of shore, the morning was flat and grey, and far out on it lay a white speck that was the Anthousa, off Cape Poseidi. Above the speck hung a line of small dark shapes, very still, like beads on a wire. Nobody on the campus had sent them, and nobody had called them back.

He walked to the conservation building with the stick tapping, and set it out for himself on the way, the way he had set out the state of things for two thousand years before he wrote them down. The giant had signed yesterday afternoon, and he would not leave the parcel, and he was mortal on it now and laughed when anyone said so. The old man had taken the lyre to sea, and the machines had gone after it in a line. And Zoe, whom he had told on the path below the theatre that his stick would kill her, had not slept.

He could see it as soon as she opened the store. She had her coat on over yesterday's jumper, and a pencil through her hair, and the log on the bench was open to a page with nothing written on it.

"It's empty," she said, before he could speak.

The climate cabinet stood open against the far wall. The foam bed inside it still held the shape of the lyre's sound box, a hollow like a sleeper's dent in a pillow. Its catalogue card lay on the shelf beside it, eleven words long. Zoe had put the dial back to the right humidity out of habit, and the cabinet hummed to itself, keeping nothing at the right humidity.

"He gave me a receipt," she said. "I read it. I let go."

"He meant you to."

"I know what he meant." She sat down on a crate of old pottery. "Tell me something true, Professor. You've been telling me halves since October. Tell me the whole of it, because I'm sitting in a room full of boxes with nothing to do with my hands."

So he did. He stood among the boxes beside the empty climate cabinet, with the stick in his hand, and told her.

He told her the spring he took it up, the year Caesar died, and the house by the sea where he had been a young man with ink on his fingers and no one to love. He told her whose it had been before. Odysseus had come home from the war and lived, and then a son he had never seen came looking for him with a spear tipped with something from the sea, and killed him on a beach without knowing who he was. Scott told her of the two thousand years after that, other people's stories written down at other people's shoulders, wars and libraries burning, and never once his own. And last he told her the rule.

"Thrown in anger," he said, "it finds the one its bearer loves. Not the one he throws it at. Wherever she stands. Behind a wall, or across the sea, it doesn't matter. It finds her."

Zoe listened the way she read a file. She didn't interrupt. Once she held up a finger, and he stopped, and she asked how he knew which spring, and he said he had written it down, and she nodded and let him go on. When he finished she sat with her hands together between her knees and looked at the hollow in the foam.

"I want the lyre back," she said.

That was all. Scott found he had expected her to say a great deal more, and was grateful she didn't.

They spent the day in the store, because neither of them could leave the campus and neither wanted to be anywhere else. She worked through a crate of loan pottery she had been putting off since the summer, wrapping each sherd on the bench under the lamp, and he sat on a stool by the door and handed her the labels. Twice the lights stuttered and came back. At noon a delivery robot rolled up to the store's glass door and stopped there, its camera turned on the empty cabinet, and after a minute it rolled away.

At dusk Zoe turned on the old radio on the shelf above the sink, the one she kept for the shipping forecast, and found the harbour channel out of Nea Fokea.

The harbour office came in through the hiss, a tired man's voice, flat with repeating itself. The motor yacht Anthousa had drifted north on the current through the day and was lying off the point at Nea Fokea, below the old tower, with her engines stopped. There were drones above her. The owner wasn't answering. Any vessel in the area should keep clear.

Zoe turned it off.

"First light," she said. "There are fishing boats in Nea Fokea. Somebody will take me out for diesel money."

"You will not."

He heard it come out of him in the voice he used in the lecture hall, the one that filled a room, and he hated it at once. Zoe looked up at him from the bench with a wrapped sherd in her hands.

"I beg your pardon?"

"There are machines over that boat that nobody can call off. You'd be under them in a fishing boat with an old man who won't answer his radio." He made his voice smaller, and it didn't help. "Zoe. Please. I can't go out there with you. If anything happened to you on that water I would be angry, and you know now what that does."

"Then don't come." She set the sherd down square in its tray. "I've kept old things for nineteen years, and that lyre since October. I don't stop keeping it because a man tells me to, and I won't be kept, either, like an object in a cabinet."

"I'm not keeping you."

"You're standing in my door."

He was. He looked down and saw his own hand on the stick, the knuckles gone white as bone, and he took the hand away and held it against his side, and stepped out of the doorway into the corridor.

She didn't say anything else. She finished the tray and switched off the lamp, and they walked out into the dark together and parted at the end of the conservation building without saying goodnight.

He didn't sleep either. At two in the morning his phone lit on the desk in his rooms by the sea. It was the campus assistant, which he had never once opened, telling him in its calm, exact voice that the badge of Z. Lambrinou had logged out at the side gate by the agronomy plots at one fifty-two.

He went down the stairs in his coat. The note was pushed under the door of the old block, in her small upright hand, on the back of a condition report.

You know I always read the file first. The file is out there.
