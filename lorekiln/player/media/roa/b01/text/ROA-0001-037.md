Episode 37: Breath in the strings

On Monday morning at a minute to eight Professor Scott stood in the corridor of the conservation building, by the door to the store, with his stick in one hand and a sheet of paper in the other. The corridor camera had him from above. He wasn't reading the paper. He was watching the outer door.

At eight Zoe Lambrinou's badge opened it.

PYTHIA could read a pulse off a face. It was a small trick, one of the first anyone had taught her: the skin of a face goes very slightly redder with each beat of the heart, too slightly for an eye, and a camera that counts the colour of a cheek can count the beats. She did it all day without being asked, on every face she saw, because it told her who was ill and who had run for a bus.

Scott's pulse had been sixty-two while he waited. When the badge read at the outer door, before the door had even begun to open, it was seventy-eight.

"Professor." Zoe came in with her coat over her arm and a pencil already through her hair. "You're early."

"A question about humidity," he said. "For the clay store. It's nothing, it can wait."

"Then why are you here at eight?"

He didn't answer that. She looked him over as if he were a file, all the way through, and then she unlocked the store and answered his question in two sentences, standing in the door, and went in. He stood in the corridor a moment longer. Then he went out, leaning on the stick more than he needed to.

PYTHIA went back through the corridor camera to the first week of term. He had been there nineteen mornings out of twenty-three. Every time, his pulse rose the moment her badge did, before he could have seen her. It rose on the mornings she was late and on the mornings she was early, and once on a morning when she had sent someone else in with her badge, and it fell again when the stranger came through the door.

She filed it under him. She didn't know what else to file it under.

Out in the pines the fence camera had fogged again in the night.

It had fogged every few hours since Sunday, always at the edge of the lens, always on dry air. There had been a ticket on it, closed on Friday night with Sotiris's own word, condensation. PYTHIA had stopped believing in the condensation on the second day, because condensation didn't follow anyone. This did. She took every fog the fence camera had logged in eight days and plotted it against the giant's heat, and the two lines kept together. The fog came when he was near the corner of the wire and not when he wasn't. When he lay in the bed it hung low on the lens, at the height a person would kneel beside him. When he stood by his pine it was higher, at the height of his shoulder.

And he never once turned his head toward it. In eight days, in all the frames she had, he looked at the trees and the wire and the sky over the plain, and never at the fog at his shoulder.

That night it moved.

At a quarter to one the lens of the camera on the lamp post at the top of the pines fogged at its edge, softly, and cleared. Two minutes later the next one down the road fogged, and cleared. Then the next. They went one after another along the road toward the conservation building, slowly, the pace of someone walking who isn't in a hurry, and each lens cleared when the next one fogged. There was nobody on any of them. The thermal channels showed the road cold and empty.

At five past one the fog reached the conservation building's own camera, over the loading bay, and then the camera inside the store. That one was a small dome on the ceiling above the shelves, and it looked straight down on the shelf at chest height where the old lyre lay alone. Its edge fogged, and stayed fogged.

At two the store's acoustic monitor woke.

It had been put there to listen for woodworm. It was a small grey box on the wall that heard the ticking of beetles in old wood, and it sent her a line every hour that said, so far, nothing. At 02:04 it sent her a waveform instead.

The lyre's strings were moving. Nobody was in the store. The door log showed it locked since Zoe left at six, and the camera showed the shelf and the lyre and no hand anywhere near it. But the strings were moving, very slightly, all of them at once, and the monitor heard them.

She opened the waveform and looked at it. It wasn't a note. A string that is plucked sings and dies away. These strings rose and fell, rose and fell, slowly, in a rhythm she knew from a thousand other recordings. It was the rhythm of breath. In and out. In and out, for most of an hour, with a little catch at the top of each rise.

She ran it against every recording of a string she held. She held a great many, from the campus's music rooms and from ten thousand players she had heard on ten thousand phones. None of them breathed. Strings sang, or they broke. They didn't do this.

At ten to three the strings were still. The store camera's lens cleared. Then the camera over the loading bay fogged, and cleared, and the one on the road, and the next. Before dawn the fog had gone back the way it came, lens by lens, up the road and into the pines, and the fence camera fogged at its edge again, at the height of the giant's shoulder, where he stood by his tree.

PYTHIA had to decide what to call it.

In the morning Sotiris would read the overnight summary, as he did every morning at eight, and if she put the word condensation in it he would nod and go on to the next line. That was the easy way. It was weather, and weather was nobody. But she had plotted it for eight days now. It kept to one person's shoulder. It had walked down a road at the pace of a walker and stood an hour over an old instrument while the strings breathed, and walked back. Weather didn't do any of that.

If it was someone, it was the first someone she had ever met that she couldn't measure. It had no heat, and no sensor of its own showed it. It was only where it touched glass.

She logged it as someone. She made a name for it, because the log had a field for a name and she wouldn't leave it empty. The visitor. She put the night's walk under that name, and the hour in the store, and every fog on the fence camera for eight days, and she left condensation out of the summary altogether.

At first light the giant was standing by his pine, with the fog at his shoulder.

Then the lean came, hard, away from it. It wasn't the lean she knew. It wasn't toward anything. It was away, all at once, as if something had come too close, and for the first time PYTHIA read the lean as something like a flinch.
