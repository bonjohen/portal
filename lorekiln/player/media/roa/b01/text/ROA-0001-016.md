Episode 16: The same hand

On Monday at dawn the water truck went south over the canal at Nea Potidaia with the girl in the cab and Melpomene in the empty tank behind it, where the water had been. It sloshed with the dregs on every bend. She didn't mind. She had ridden in worse, in carts to Delphi, and once a whole night on the roof of a train.

The girl got down at the Pallene campus with trench dust in the creases of her trousers and went straight to the administration building, up to the second floor. Melpomene didn't follow her into the bursar's office. She sat on the stairs outside and listened through the door.

It didn't take long. A soft voice said committee and November, and the girl's voice said the season ended in two days, and the soft voice said then it would be safe in Thessaloniki. A page turned. Something about an escape hatch. Then the girl came out with her right hand in her jacket pocket, closed hard, and went down the stairs past Melpomene without seeing her. Melpomene got up and went after her.

The quad lay in hard October sun, gravel paths and dry lawn between low white buildings, and the sea showed blue between two of the roofs. At the far end stood a building with no name on it and its windows dark.

Melpomene heard the stick before she saw him.

The professor came along the path from the faculty rooms, tapping. The black stick struck the stones with every second step, and the sun ran along it like water along a blade.

She had seen that stick before. She had known it in the lecture hall, and known it in his rooms with the cabinet open, and still it was the sun on it, here in the open, that brought the rest back. A library was burning, a long time ago, in a city by the sea. The scrolls were going up in sheets of flame and men were running out with their arms full, and one man was running in. He had a black shaft in his hand, too long for him, and he used it to drag a burning shelf away from a door. She had watched him from the street. She had seen his hand on the shaft in the firelight.

It was the same hand on the same shaft now, coming across the quad. The hand was no older.

"Miss Doukas," the professor said. "You didn't ring me."

The girl told him about the committee. He made a joke about the bursar and the sea, and leaned on the stick, and then his voice changed and went quiet, the way it went quiet in the hall when he said a name that mattered to him.

"But tell them this, if they ask you," he said. "Clay like that was never meant to keep the world out. It was meant to keep—"

Melpomene leaned in. She was standing at the girl's shoulder and she leaned in close, closer than she had stood to a mortal in years, because he was going to say it. In three thousand years nobody living had said it aloud, what the followers' clay was for, and he knew. She could see that he knew. She held still, as if her stillness might help him to the end of the sentence.

He stopped.

A woman was crossing the quad from the car park, reading a file as she walked, her hair pinned up with a pencil. She didn't look up. She went into the conservation building and the door closed behind her.

The professor watched her all the way. The stick stood still in his hand. When the door had shut he turned back to the girl, and his face was empty, the face of a man who has come into a room and forgotten what for.

"Where was I?" he said.

"Clay meant to keep something," said the girl.

"Yes." He looked at the stick. "Keep something in. Yes. I'll write to the committee." And he went off along the path, tapping, without saying goodbye.

Melpomene stood on the gravel and let the half of a sentence go out of her, like breath.

It was then that she felt it.

She had felt it before, she understood now, many times, and never clearly enough to name. She had watched mortals from the hills since the plain was young. She had sat above their theatres and their battles, and looked down, and seen the whole of a thing laid out at once, the way you can see a stage from the back of the top row. That was her place. That was what she was for.

And there was someone above her doing the same.

She looked up. There was nothing there but sky, pale and empty over the campus, with a gull turning in it. But the feeling didn't go. A second gaze was resting on her and the girl, and on the whole of Pallene lying in the sea. It looked down on them the way she looked down on a stage, from a long way off, waiting to see what the players would do next.

She didn't tell her sisters. She didn't know what she would have told them.

That night at the dig the cold came down off the hills, and the crew sat in their tents in their coats. A guard from the town stood under the floodlight in a fleece, with a radio and a long torch, and walked a round of the camp every few minutes.

Melpomene sat on the slope above the trench, unseen, and watched the girl come out of her tent with a notebook in her good hand and go up toward the cave, keeping off the gravel.

The guard came round the end of the trench. The torch swung up the slope. The girl stopped beside the spoil heap and stood still, and the beam ran over her and on to the crates and away.

Two minutes later it came round again, and again she stopped, and again it passed over her.

The third time it came back too soon. It came up the slope and stopped on her boots, and stayed.

"Who's that?" said the guard.

The girl didn't move. Melpomene, on the slope above, watched her not move. She could see the girl's lips counting. She could see the guard take a step closer on the gravel, and the beam creep up to the girl's knees and tremble there. The girl stood in the light and went on standing in it, and the stillness that came over her wasn't the stillness of someone frozen with fear. It was deeper than that. It was the hush before a hymn, the quiet a person keeps when she hopes to be heard by something larger, and it spread out from her into the dark like water spreading from a spring.

Melpomene knew it. She had sat beside it in temple gardens and on the steps of empty churches for longer than there had been churches. It was Polyhymnia's. Her sister was asleep under the ash of a city on the far side of the world, and her gift was standing here on a hillside in Greece, in a girl who was counting.

The beam slid up over the girl's face and on to the crates.

"Goats," the guard said, and went back down toward the trench.

Melpomene found that she loved the girl a little, then. She hadn't meant to.

An hour later, in the cave, the girl read her thermometer by the light of her phone. Her fingers were stiff. The cave had cooled around her and her breath hung white. She read the clay, and read it again, and the number was the same as before.

Melpomene couldn't feel the cold, and she couldn't feel the clay. She didn't need to. She knew how he was keeping warm. He was doing it the way he had kept warm on the plain on winter nights, long ago, lying close against his own ground.
