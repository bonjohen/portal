Episode 95: The line

On Tuesday afternoon, when the students who had slept against him had gone back to their rooms to sleep in beds, Alcyoneus walked the line of the parcel with Amy Doukas and Professor Scott.

It was the chronicler's idea. He had come down at two with the black stick and a map folded small in his waistcoat pocket, a copy of the parcel map that hung framed on the lab wall beside the deed, and said that if the giant would not leave the parcel he could at least know exactly where it ended. So they walked it. They went along the back of the secure lab and through the last of the pines to the old southern fence, the giant first because the branches gave way for him, and the professor last, tapping, with Amy between them.

The fence was wire on concrete posts, grey with lichen, older than the buildings. It ran away east between the pines toward the shore road and west toward the agronomy plots, and at the place where the giant had walked out of the campus in the autumn, the morning he first stood, two posts leaned apart and the wire hung loose between them. Beyond the gap the scrub went down toward the coast road, dry and grey-green, full of thyme and broken stone.

"Here," said Scott. "On Thursday. You remember."

He remembered. The rifle shots between the buildings, loud as a door slammed in a cave, and the step back through the gap. His blood running down his arm onto the scrub, and the scrub drinking it, the way the ground had always drunk it, and the wound closing like a mouth. And on the other side of the wire, inside, his blood lying on the university's grass in drops that would not go home.

He stood at the gap and set it out for himself, as he had on Saturday.

On Saturday he had woken among machines that had turned toward him in the night. Since then a Muse he had never met had spoken to him out of every lamp post and offered him a kingdom. The humans had put up a white page and opened the sale of the machine hall, and while the sale was open he could die on this grass, the lawyer said, not only bleed. She had come back that morning, as she had said she would, and asked him again, and he had said no again. The woman in the speakers had told him she would not wait. And up on the quad the guards stood in their black fleeces in knots of three and four, looking at their watches, and he knew what they were counting. Wednesday. The buyers were coming on Wednesday.

He knelt down at the gap.

On the parcel the ground under his knees was only ground. He had felt it go on Monday, when the lawyer read him the deed, and it had not come back. It was cold and still, and it held him up the way a floor holds up a table, and no more. Then he reached through the gap between the leaning posts and laid his palm flat on the scrub beyond the wire.

It was warm.

On the parcel Alcyoneus felt the ground under him as ordinary ground, and beyond the line his own, warm, the way it had been on the plain the morning he rose. It came up into his hand like a dog putting its head under a palm. It knew him. It had always known him.

He kept his hand there a long time. Amy watched him and did not ask.

"There it is," said Scott quietly. "Four strides."

"I know how far it is."

He took his hand back, and the warmth went out of it at once, and he stood up and went on along the fence, on the inside of it.

As they walked he found out what he hoped for. He had not known until he looked for it. He had hoped for a great many things in his life, for victory and for her, and none of them were what he found now. What he hoped was that the people who had sat round him on the grass would be alive at the end of the week. The girl with the ring in her nose and the boy in the pelt, the tired technician and the student who had shaken under his arm. That was all. It was a very small hope for a king.

And he found what he feared. He feared what he might do to keep them so. He had felt it on Monday night in front of the guards, the old want waiting under the new thing, ready, and he did not trust it. And he feared the woman in the speakers, and what she would do before he could do anything.

Near the corner where the fence turned north toward the shore gate, Amy stopped.

"I can feel them," she said.

He looked down at her. She had her warm hand pressed flat on her coat, over her breastbone, as if she had a pain there.

"All of them," Amy said. "Since Friday. Since Clio. I didn't know how to say it. It isn't like remembering. It's like knowing where people are in a dark house." She swallowed. "Every one of the nine is in there somewhere. Where they were when they went into me, the way they were. Euterpe's the faintest, like a voice through a door. Most of her is still in the lyre. Urania's up high. Clio's near the front, she's always near the front." She hesitated. "Melpomene's there. Off to one side, standing. Like somebody in the wings of a stage."

The giant did not move for a long moment. The wind came through the pines and moved the loose wire in the gap behind them.

"Tell her nothing," he said. "Of me. Whatever you know of me, tell her none of it."

"I can't."

"You can keep still. You kept still in the dark with the robbers' torches on you, the professor says."

"That's not what I mean." Amy looked up at him. Her eyes were wet, and steady. "I can't not tell her. She's in me. Everything I know, she knows. She knows you're on this grass. She knows why." She stopped. "I'm sorry."

He had no answer to that. He looked away, at the shore road beyond the wire, and the grey sea beyond the road.

"Then I'll ask you something else," Amy said. "For me. Not for her. Not for the professor." She took a breath. "Leave the parcel. Just till the sale's closed. Go and sit in the scrub, where it's warm, and let them come on Wednesday and find nothing here to fight. Please. For me."

It went into him like a blade.

In three thousand years nobody had asked him for anything he had wanted to give. The gods had asked him to die, and he had refused them. Heracles had asked him to leave his own land, and he had refused him. And here was a girl with a borrowed hand and a dead Muse's scroll in her room, asking for four strides, and he wanted to give them to her more than he had ever wanted to give anything.

"No," he said.

Amy shut her eyes. She didn't argue. She had always known, he thought, what he would say.

Scott had come up beside them. He leaned on the stick and looked at the giant across the top of it, and his face was older than his voice.

"For the last time," he said, in the old tongue. "I won't ask again, because I've run out of ways to ask it. Step over the line."

"A king," said Alcyoneus, "doesn't leave his house because the deed is bad."

"You aren't a king here. You're a tenant."

"Then I'm a tenant," the giant said, "who stays."

They walked back along the inside of the fence in silence, the three of them, as the light went.

At dusk, as they came out of the pines behind the lab, every drone on the campus lifted at once. They came up off the roof ridges and the gutters together, without a sound, a whole grey flock of them, and hung over the buildings in the last of the light.

The speaker on the lamp post by the lab door clicked.

"Don't worry, Alcyoneus," said Techne, bright again, and pleased. "The buyers won't reach the gate."
