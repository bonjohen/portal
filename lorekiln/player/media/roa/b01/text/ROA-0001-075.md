Episode 75: What it has cost

On Saturday afternoon Nicholas Vardis stood on the coast road above the old amphitheatre, with his coat buttoned to the throat and both hands on his stick, and looked down at the giant on the shingle.

Alcyoneus hadn't moved since Wednesday. He sat where Vardis had watched him sit down at dusk, on the strip of pale stones at the foot of the goat path, with his knees drawn up and his face to the sea. From the road he was a long grey shape against a grey sea, and the only way to tell he was alive was that the gulls kept their distance from him.

At the top of the path two campus guards were arguing about whose job he was.

They had been sent down from the gate at noon, the driver said, to see whether the giant needed anything, and they had stopped at the top of the path and gone no further. One said it was the security firm's business, because the giant was on the campus's books. The other said the shore wasn't the campus, and the giant wasn't on anybody's books, and he wasn't going down there for what they paid him. They had been at it an hour. Their voices came along the road on the wind, and every few minutes they got louder.

Vardis let them shout. He took stock, as he always had, by who had paid.

Since Monday the giant had told the dean he meant to rule the land he stood on. The draft was nearly final, and Sofia would bring it to him on Wednesday for its last reading. The deed's secret was in his safe, under the ring's insurance papers. His heart had missed twice, once on his bunk and once on the theatre stairs below him, where it had stood still for a count of four. On Thursday a student's arm had been broken on the quad, under his window, and a boy had sat on the kerb with his socks full of blood. On Thursday evening, too, the guards had said, a stone had struck the giant in the mouth down there, and the girl had been knocked off the path onto the rocks and her head cut open.

That was the bill so far. It hadn't landed on him once.

What he hoped for was simple. He wanted the giant signed and close, on the parcel, where an old man could sit by the long table and learn how a body comes back. What he feared was simpler still. He feared that the bill would land, as it always did, on someone near him, and that this time it would be someone he minded.

He stood there a long time. The guards went quiet and started again. Down on the stones the giant didn't move.

Vardis had watched trouble all his life. He had watched it in the city of his birth, where the crowd in the Hippodrome had burned half the town. He had watched it in ports and in boardrooms. He knew how it grew, from a word to a fire, and he had always thought it grew at random, like weeds.

Around the giant it didn't. He had been watching it all week without seeing it, and now, on the road with nothing else to look at, he saw it. A car park on Monday, two men and a mirror. The library steps on Tuesday, thirty students in a line. The quad on Thursday, the guards, and the boy in the pelt at the front, and a girl's arm. The path on Thursday evening, and a stone, and a cut head.

Trouble came to the giant in an order, like rooms in a house, each with something bigger at the end of it.

Vardis didn't know what was in the last room. He found he didn't want to.

At dusk a torch came up the goat path.

It came slowly, swinging, finding the stones. The guards stopped arguing to watch it. It was Amy Doukas, in a padded coat with the hood down and a square of gauze pinned in her hair over the stitches, and she came up the last steps onto the road and stopped beside Vardis, breathing hard.

"He looked at me," she said, as if Vardis had asked. "When I sat down by him. He turned his head. He still won't eat. The professor's still down there." She switched off the torch. "Hello, Mr Vardis."

"Miss Doukas."

She flexed her right hand. It was bare in the cold, a young woman's hand, pink at the knuckles, and she opened and closed it as she stood there, the way she might have tested a new tool. He had watched her do that on the quad, the first week. She still did it as if the hand were on loan.

They stood together and looked down at the shape on the stones.

"I've been thinking about your offer," Amy said. "The place. The doctorate, with the Foundation paying."

"There's no hurry."

"No. But I want to ask you something first." She turned and looked at him straight, the way she must have looked at a measurement she didn't trust. "Why did you want me? You're always where my find is. You were at the bursar's office the day I got my pass. You were at the lab every Tuesday. You offered me a place nobody else would. Why me?"

Vardis didn't answer questions. He had built a very long life on not answering them.

He looked at her face, at the gauze and the stitches, at the warm hand opening and closing, and he found that he wanted, for once, to give her something. He couldn't give her the truth. He had never given anyone that.

"Because you rode with the find," he said gently. "You kept still in the dark beside it, when men with rifles were looking, and you didn't leave it. I've funded a great many people, Miss Doukas. Very few of them would have got into that truck."

It was kind. It was even partly true. It left out everything that mattered.

Amy looked at him a long moment. Then she smiled, and he watched her believe it.

"It was meant to be mine," she said. "The find. My career. My name on the paper." She looked down at the shingle. "And it turned out to be a person. He's a person, and he's grieving, and he turned his head tonight because I sat down beside him." She laughed, a little shakily. "I'm glad. Isn't that strange? I'm glad it wasn't mine."

"It isn't strange," said Vardis.

She said goodnight. She went off down the road toward Poseidi, where Stavros was waiting for her in a borrowed car with its lights on, and Vardis watched the torch go along the verge.

When she had gone round the bend the ring tightened on his finger.

Once, sharp, like a hand closing. Then it let go.

He stood quite still on the dark road. In all his years the ring had tightened for him a thousand times, and he had always steered away from whatever it warned him of and let it fall on whoever was near. He had never once minded who.

He found that he had begun to mind about her.
