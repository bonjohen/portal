Episode 72: The land he stands on

On Tuesday morning the dean's office on the campus was too small for the people in it, and much too small for one of them.

Nicholas Vardis stood by the door, where he always stood, with both hands on his stick. The dean sat behind his desk in a good blue suit, smiling as if this were a meeting about a car park. Sofia Andreou sat beside the desk with her draft on her knee and her green pen ready. Professor Scott stood against the bookcase in his rumpled grey suit, leaning on the black stick, looking at nobody.

And Alcyoneus sat on the floor.

No chair in the building would take him. He had folded himself down onto the carpet between the desk and the window, his knees up near his chin and his long grey hands hanging between them, and even sitting he was taller than the dean. He was eight feet of bone and joint, broader than when he woke, with fine pale lines running along his forearms where the bones had drawn together on the long table. Vardis had counted those lines every Tuesday for three weeks. There were fewer of them than last week.

"So," said the dean. "Perhaps we could begin with what he wants. Of the university. In his own words."

Scott said it to the giant in the old tongue.

Alcyoneus didn't look at the dean. He looked out of the window, at the pines and the strip of grey sea beyond them, and answered in a voice that made the glass in the bookcase hum.

"I mean to rule the land I stand on," he said. "Tell him that."

Vardis understood every word. He had heard that tongue spoken by the oldest men in the street when he was a boy, older and slower than the Greek of his own day, and he had never quite lost it. Nobody in the room knew that, except perhaps the professor.

"He says," said Scott, "that he means to rule the land he stands on."

The dean laughed. It was a warm, easy laugh, the one people keep for a donor's joke at a dinner, and he looked at Vardis as he did it, as if to share it with him. Vardis smiled back politely. He didn't think it was a joke. He had heard men say that sentence before, in palaces, and most of them had meant it.

"Well," said the dean. "Sofia. The heads of terms."

Sofia read them slowly, as she always did, so that nobody could say afterwards they hadn't been told. A name, on paper, in the civil register. A home on the campus parcel, the faculty hall and the ground around it, for as long as he lived. The gold and jewels buried with him, held in trust and his to draw on. Protection, from the press and the police. Visits to the old lyre in the conservation store, whenever he liked. And in return, at the end, in one plain line, the waiver of every ancestral and sovereign claim to the parcel.

Scott put each term into the old tongue as she read it, flatly, the way a man reads out a bill.

Vardis listened to the last one and kept his face still.

He had known since the faculty meeting on Friday what that line would do. He had sat by the door and watched the professor lean over and whisper to the conservator, and he had read the professor's face, and then he had read the clause again in his own copy that night. On Pallene the giant couldn't die. The land held him and wouldn't let him go. That one line handed the land back, and on the parcel, and only there, Alcyoneus would be as mortal as a fisherman.

"It's a fair home," Vardis said. "More than fair."

Scott looked at him for the first time that morning.

Then the professor turned to the giant and spoke again in the old tongue, low and quick, and this time he wasn't translating anything.

"Listen to me," he said. "Paper has done to your kind what spears did. They drove you off your soil with an army once. Now they'll do it with ink, and smile."

Alcyoneus turned his head from the window and looked at him. Then he laughed. It started low in his chest and went up, and the dean's pens rattled in their pot.

"No mortal's ink unmakes Gaia's son," he said. "Let them write."

Vardis felt the ring loosen on his finger.

It was a small thing, the gold sliding a little easier over the knuckle, as it did when a danger to him had passed. He knew that loosening. He had felt it a thousand times, and every time someone near him had paid for it. The professor had told the giant the truth and the giant had laughed, and the deal Vardis wanted was still alive. Somebody in this room had just paid for his luck, and he thought he knew who.

"What did the professor say?" asked the dean.

"A point about the drafting," said Scott, in Greek. His right hand was on the head of the black stick, and the knuckles had gone white. "And here is another. This agreement should not be signed. Not this month. Not by him, not until he understands what he's giving up."

"He's giving up a claim," said Sofia, mildly. "Every settlement of land has a waiver."

"He isn't every claimant."

"No," said the dean. "He's the only one with a lawyer the Foundation pays for." He looked at Vardis again, and this time he wasn't laughing. "Mr Vardis?"

Vardis had kept every door he ever stood by. He could have said, there's something in the professor's point, let us wait a month. He could have let the one man in the room who had told the giant the truth have his month.

"He has asked for a home," Vardis said. "He has asked in his own words, and we have heard him. I don't think the university should tell a man older than all of us that he doesn't understand his own mind." He bowed a little to the floor where the giant sat. "With respect to the professor."

The dean nodded as if that settled it. It did. Sofia made a note in green. Scott said nothing more. He stood against the bookcase with his white knuckles, and he looked at Vardis the way the fisherman had looked at him on the quay, without spitting.

At noon the meeting broke up.

The dean shook hands, and Sofia gathered her papers. Scott went out first, fast, with the stick tapping. And the giant unfolded from the carpet, joint by joint, bending his head under the light, and came to the door where Vardis stood.

There he stopped.

He bent down, slowly, until his long grey face was level with Vardis's own, and he breathed in through his nose. Then again, closer, at the collar of the good dark coat. He did it the way a dog does at a stranger's coat, frankly, without apology.

Vardis didn't move. The ring was quite still on his finger.

"You smell of the sea," the giant said, in the old tongue. "And of something older than the sea."

He straightened and went out after the professor, down the stairs and out into the cold. From the window on the landing Vardis watched the two of them cross the quad toward the library, where there was shouting on the steps. At the corner of the paving the giant turned off south, toward the lab, and the professor went on alone, faster, into the noise.
