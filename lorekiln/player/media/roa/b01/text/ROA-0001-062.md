Episode 62: White on the stick

On Thursday morning Zoe Lambrinou was at her bench by the window with the lyre's file open in front of her, reading it for the fourth time since Wednesday evening.

She had told Amy not yet. She had said it kindly, and meant it, and Amy had nodded as if she had expected nothing else and gone out into the dark with her warm hand in her pocket. Then Zoe had sat up until ten reading everything the store held on the object: the courier's papers from October, which said almost nothing, and her own card with its eleven words. None of it said what the thing was for. None of it said who should play it.

At eight Professor Scott came in with his question about humidity.

She gave him the number. He thanked her and didn't leave. He sat down on the tall stool by the door instead, with the black stick leaning against his knee, as if he had nowhere in particular to be.

At a quarter past eight the dean's office arrived.

It came as a young man in a quilted jacket with a clipboard, and behind him two of the security firm's guards in blue shirts, with batons on their belts and their caps in their hands, as if they had been told this was a polite visit. The young man said good morning to the room in general. He said he was from the dean's office, and that he had come to list the store's holdings for the schedule.

"Which schedule?" said Zoe.

"Sofia Andreou's agreement. The giant's." He consulted the clipboard. "The giant has asked that one item in this store be his to visit, whenever he likes, for as long as the agreement runs. It goes in the schedule. An old lyre." He looked up and round the store, at the steel shelves and the wrapped shapes on them. "Which one is the lyre?"

Zoe didn't look at the cabinet. Scott, on his stool, did, and the young man followed his eyes.

"Ah," he said. "Good. The dean's office would like it moved this morning to the Foundation's strongroom in the administration block, for safety, given the interest. The Foundation has offered. There's a van outside."

"It doesn't leave the climate cabinet."

"It'll be in a strongroom."

"A strongroom is a cupboard with a lock. It isn't climate controlled. That object is old tortoiseshell and horn on a bed of foam, older than anything else in this building, and it has been at forty-eight per cent humidity since November, and it stays at forty-eight per cent." She closed the file. "And it isn't an item. It's an object. Items are what you buy in shops."

The young man smiled, the way people smile at a difficult aunt. "I do understand. But it isn't really your decision."

"It's entirely my decision. I'm the conservator. Read the accession sheet."

"I've got a letter from the dean."

"Then the dean can come and read the accession sheet."

He held the letter out. She didn't take it. He went on holding it, and his face went slowly pink above the quilted collar, and his voice went up a step.

"Look. There's a schedule, and a signing, and a giant who has asked for this thing by name. Nobody wants it damaged. We want it safe." He turned to the nearer guard. "Would you?"

The guard put his cap on and came round the end of the bench. He was a big man and not an unkind one, she thought, and he didn't touch her. He just stood in front of the cabinet with his hand out, the way you would stand in front of a child near a road.

"Step aside, please, madam."

Zoe didn't raise her voice. She never did. She stood where she was, between the guard and the glass, with her hands at her sides and her pencil through her hair, and didn't move.

"No," she said.

"Madam."

"No."

The young man's voice went up again. "This is absurd. Will you please move."

The room had gone very quiet. She could hear the cabinet's fan and the young man breathing through his nose. And she became aware, the way you become aware of a draught, that the quiet was coming from the stool by the door.

Scott hadn't moved. He sat exactly as he had sat all along, his left hand on his knee and his right on the head of the black stick. But he had gone still in a way she had never seen a living person go still. He wasn't looking at the guard or the young man. He was looking at the floor a yard in front of his own shoes, and his face had no expression in it at all.

Zoe saw his hand go white on the black stick, the knuckles bone-pale, as if all the blood in him had gone somewhere else and left that hand to hold on.

The guard saw it too. He looked round, as people do when a room changes, and his eyes went to the stick and stayed there. Then he took a step back from the cabinet, and another. He didn't seem to know he had done it. He stood with his hand still half out and a puzzled look on his face, like a man who has walked into a room and forgotten why.

Scott stood up.

He did it slowly. He didn't look at anyone. He took the stick in his hand and walked to the door and out of it, and the door swung shut behind him on its closer, and through the north window Zoe watched him cross the yard to the edge of the pines and stop there, with his back to the building, in the cold.

Nobody in the store said anything for a while.

"Well," said the young man at last. He had stopped being pink. "Well. Perhaps a photograph. For the schedule. And a line." He wrote on his clipboard. "One lyre, ancient, in the conservator's keeping, in the conservation store. Visits by appointment with the conservator." He looked at her. "Is that acceptable?"

"Visits whenever he likes," Zoe said. "That was the request. Write it as it was made."

He crossed something out and wrote it again. The other guard took a photograph of the cabinet on his phone, through the glass, and the flash went off against it and came back. Then they went out to their van, and the van went away without the lyre in it.

Scott stood at the edge of the pines for ten minutes by the clock over her desk. She knew because she looked at it twice.

Then she put her coat on and went out to him.

It was colder than it looked. The wind came across the campus off the sea and moved the tops of the pines, and he stood with his collar up and his hands folded on the head of the stick, looking at nothing. His knuckles had their colour back. She stood beside him, not too near.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For the scene."

"You didn't make a scene."

"No. That was the one I was sorry for." He almost smiled. "The one I didn't make."

"I've corrected worse terms than that young man's," Zoe said. "He called it an item. I've had a minister call a Byzantine chalice a cup."

"What did you call him?"

"A minister. That was enough."

He did smile then, briefly. Then it went away, and he looked down at his own hands on the stick as if they belonged to someone he didn't trust.

"Zoe," he said.

She waited.

He didn't look at her. He looked at the pines, and at the strip of grey sea showing between them, and when he spoke it was in the voice she had heard him use only once, on the night the giant came into her store and laid his hand on the strings.

"I've been truly angry perhaps three times in my life," he said. "Each time someone else paid."

She opened her mouth to ask who.

Behind them the store door banged open, and the young man from the dean's office was standing in it with his clipboard, calling her name across the yard. He had forgotten, he said, to ask her to sign.
