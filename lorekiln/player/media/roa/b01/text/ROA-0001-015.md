Episode 15: Too long to be a man's

On Friday morning the light came up grey and cold over the dig, and the floodlights were still on over the trench when the girl clipped into the rope. Melpomene stood beside the survey lead at the lip of the fissure and listened to him give the girl twenty minutes.

Then the girl went down, feet first, into the dark, and Melpomene went down after her.

A Muse doesn't need a rope. She let herself sink through the cold the way a leaf sinks through still water, and came to rest on the ledge at the bottom a little before the girl's boots did. The girl's headlamp swept the walls. She found an old iron bolt in the rock and tied off to it, and it took her a long time, because the knot needed two hands and one of hers needed telling. Melpomene waited. She didn't look at the knot. She was looking at the wall.

He was there.

He was carved along the stone from the floor to higher than the lamp could reach, among the others, giants and gods locked arm against arm. She knew him before the light had properly reached him. A huge figure further down the passage stood with his arms spread wide, holding back a line of men with spears, and she had seen him stand exactly like that, on the plain, with the dust going up and the men of Philoctetes coming on in a line. Somebody who had loved him had cut that. Somebody who had seen it.

The girl took three photographs, her breath going white in the light.

Then the roof began to come down.

Grit came first, and then a stone the size of a fist. The girl ran forward into the passage with her arm over her head, and the rock came down all round her and behind her, filling the way she had come. Melpomene stood in the middle of it and let it fall through her. She didn't raise a hand. She couldn't have held up a pebble.

And it stopped.

It didn't slow and trickle and settle, the way rock does. It stopped the moment the girl was clear, as cleanly as a door shutting, and one last stone rolled down the slope and lay still. The dust hung in the lamplight. The girl knelt on the far side, coughing and counting under her breath, and Melpomene stood among the fallen stones and looked up at the roof.

She hadn't done it. Her sisters weren't there. And there was no one else in the hill that she could feel, only the stone and the girl. Something had held that roof up until the girl was ready, and let it go, and then stopped it again, and Melpomene couldn't have told anyone who.

The girl talked to the survey lead on her radio and walked on into the hill. Melpomene went with her.

The passage ended in a small round chamber, and in the middle of it was a shelf of rock, and on the shelf lay something long, wrapped in cloth gone brown, sealed over in clay the colour of the walls.

Melpomene knew the clay.

She knew the grey of it, mixed with ash from the hearths, and the way it had been laid over the cloth in long strokes by hands that were careful and crying. She knew the lines of letters pressed into it. She had stood on the hill and watched the followers make that clay, by lamplight, in the nights after the dogs.

The girl stood in the doorway with her breath going white and couldn't take another step. Melpomene could have. She didn't. She stood beside the girl and looked at the shape on the shelf, far too long to be a man's, and she knew whose it was, and she kept it behind her teeth. If she said it once, aloud, in this place, she would never be able to say anything else.

That evening she went over the canal.

The survey lead had rung the professor from the top of the spoil heap, and she had heard the professor's name in his mouth, and she wanted to see the one mortal face that might know what had been found. The campus lay between the pines on the western shoulder of Pallene, low white buildings and gravel paths, and his rooms were on the first floor of an old block near the sea. She went in through the wall.

He was alone. He had his coat still on. He stood in the middle of the room with his phone in one hand and the black stick in the other, and for a long time he didn't move at all.

Then he crossed to a cabinet in the corner, a tall old thing of dark wood, and unlocked it with a key from his waistcoat.

There was a folded skin on the shelf inside, tawny and old, the hair worn short along the folds. A card was tied to it with string. He didn't take it out. He stood and looked at it, with the cabinet door open and the stick in his hand, the way a man looks at a letter he hasn't been able to answer.

Melpomene knew it. She had seen it last on Mount Oeta, in the fire. She had watched Heracles lie down on his pyre on the lion's pelt he had worn all his life, with the robe his wife had dyed for him still stuck to his back. When the flames went up, the robe on his back lost its colour all at once, the way a stain lifts out of cloth in boiling water, and she had never let herself think where it went. Then the pelt lifted from him in the heat and floated up over the smoke, unburned, and the robe went after it, pale as new linen, and drifted away down the mountain on the wind. The followers of Heracles had looked for the pelt for years. Here it was, folded on a shelf in a cupboard by the sea.

The professor shut the door and locked it, and put the key away.

She was afraid then, and she knew what of. He had kept it, which meant he had a reason. A man who keeps a thing like that is keeping it for someone, and whatever the fire had put into it was in it still. She feared for whoever he gave it to.

Late that night by the fire, the three of them took stock.

"She's chosen," Clio said, with the scroll on her knees. "She's been told, and she doesn't believe."

"And I'm counting," said Urania. "Though I've been told not to."

They looked at Melpomene, and she looked at the embers. There was a hope in her that she wouldn't let near her mouth. She gave them something else instead.

"Do you remember the last time Euterpe came to the fire?" she said. "Before she went away. She played for us, all night, and in the morning I asked her where she was born, the way we used to ask each other, for the pleasure of hearing it. She couldn't say. She sat there with the music still in her hands and couldn't say where she was born."

Nobody answered. Urania looked away.

"How long," Melpomene said, "before I can't?"

Clio watched her across the fire, and Melpomene knew that Clio knew she was holding something back, and Clio didn't ask. Melpomene was grateful for that. She didn't tell them about the shelf.

On Saturday afternoon the wind came off the gulf and set every tarpaulin snapping, and she went back down into the cave beside the girl.

The crew had cleared the fall. The girl had a ladder now, and a scale bar that wouldn't lie still on the sloping shelf. Melpomene knelt on the other side of him. She had waited a day to do this. She laid her hand flat on the clay over his chest.

She felt nothing.

Not the cold, and not the grain of the clay. She pressed, and her hand went a little way into it, the way a hand goes into mist, and there was nothing there to feel. She was too thin now to feel stone.

Across from her the girl set the scale bar down for the third time, and for the third time it slid. Without thinking, the girl reached out with her right hand, the grey one, and laid it flat on the clay to brace herself.

She took it away at once, guilty, and put her wrist to the fingertips out of habit.

They were warm. Melpomene saw her face change. The hand that could feel nothing had felt him, and had brought the warmth of him away on its fingertips, and Melpomene, who could feel nothing either, knelt there and watched the girl feel it for her.
