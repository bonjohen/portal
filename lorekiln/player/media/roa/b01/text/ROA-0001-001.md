Episode 1: Back Row

Back row, next to the aisle—my favorite spot—the best place to see everything and not be seen.

Amy Doukas thought it on the stairs and thought it again as she sat down, on a wet Tuesday morning in October, with rain running down the high windows of the lecture hall. The hall was raked, about a hundred seats falling in rows to a low stage and a long chalkboard, and from the top she could see every head in the room and every phone glowing in every lap. Nobody looked up the stairs. Nobody ever did.

She pushed her bag under the seat and rested her right hand on the desk, because there was nowhere else to put it. The hand was carbon and grey silicone, and the fingers closed when she told them to, slowly, if she was paying attention. It was the one thing about her people looked at. The girl at the end of the next row turned at the small click it made on the wood, looked, and turned away again with the quick kindness of someone deciding she hadn't seen it. Amy had been watching people decide that since she was seventeen. She kept the hand still. If she kept still for long enough, the looking stopped, and the room forgot she was there.

The professor was already at the board, writing. He hadn't looked round once. She had heard about him. People talked, and what they said was that he was a bit eccentric and that you never quite knew what he would do next. He wore a grey suit that looked as if he had slept in it, and his hair stood up at the back, slightly wild. A shiny black walking stick, rather too long for him, tapped on the floor as he moved along the board.

Line by line, the board filled with Greek.

It IS Greek, Amy thought, and smiled to herself.

The bell rang. Students were still coming in, shaking water off their coats, and he turned round as if the bell had been his own idea.

"Oh, the Gigantomachy!" he shouted.

Half the room jumped. A boy near the front dropped his coffee. The professor didn't wait for anyone to sit down, and he didn't ask them to.

"The war of the gods and the giants," he said. "The stuff of legend. But today we will look at a quieter moment, before the battle and before the noise. Alcyoneus, the giant of Pallene, and Melpomene, the Muse of Tragedy." He brought the stick down on the stage, and the boom of it went up the rake and into Amy's chest. "Write that down. Both names. You will need them."

She wrote them with her left hand. She had learned to do it well enough that most people couldn't tell.

He talked about the giant first. Alcyoneus was bound to his own land, he said, and couldn't die while he stood on it. On Pallene's soil he got up from every blow. He talked about the Muse who stood near him and watched, who never fought and never schemed, and Amy noticed that his voice went quiet when he said her name, the way a man's voice goes quiet in a church.

Then he told them about the morning of the fight.

He told it in the present tense, walking up and down the stage, and he told it with things in it that Amy had never read. The wind came in off the gulf that morning, he said, and it carried pine smoke from the camp fires down onto the plain, so the men coughed as they walked. The giant's shadow lay long and to the west, because the sun was still low behind the hills. The ground was soft from rain two days before, and you could see where he had stood by how deep the prints went.

Amy stopped writing. She had read the sources for this module over the summer, all of them, in her grandmother's kitchen in Litochoro, with the mountain filling the window behind the table. Her grandmother had told her the old stories of that mountain when she was small, and had died in February, and the kitchen was still full of her things. Amy had read Pindar there, and Apollodorus with every scrap of commentary the library would lend her. Not one of them said which way the wind blew. Not one said anything about rain.

Lovely, she thought. He's making it up. It's a performance.

She would have left it there. But he stopped in the middle of the stage, swung the stick up and pointed it at the back row, straight along its length, like a sword.

"You. By the aisle. Who killed Alcyoneus, and how?"

Every head in the room turned. She felt them turn. The girl at the end of the next row looked first at Amy's face and then, as they always did, at her hand.

"Heracles," Amy said. Her voice came out quieter than she meant it to. "Apollodorus says he shot him with an arrow. The giant fell, and got up again, because the land revived him. So, on Athena's advice, Heracles dragged him over the border of Pallene, and outside it he died."

"Good," said the professor. "That's what it says." He lowered the stick. "That isn't how it went."

Someone laughed. He didn't.

"Nobody dragged him," he said. "You don't drag a thing like that anywhere. He was driven, step by step, to the water, to where the canal runs now at Nea Potidaia. And he knew at every step what it would cost him to take the next one." He tapped the stick twice on the boards. "Apollodorus wrote a thousand years later, from people who wrote it down from other people. He tidied it."

Amy heard herself say it before she had decided to. "Then how would anyone know how it went?"

The room went still. A hundred people had just learned the back row had a voice in it.

The professor looked up at her for longer than she liked. He didn't look at her hand. He looked at her face, as if he were reading a label, and then he smiled, and she had no idea what the smile meant.

"That," he said, "is the right question. Keep it. Bring it every Tuesday."

He didn't answer it. He turned back to the stage and the plain of Pallene came back into his voice, and she sat with her face hot and her question unanswered in her lap.

He gave them the morning of the battle. Philoctetes and his men crossing the plain with their breath short. The giant waiting on a crag above them, not a lumbering brute but a cunning one, letting them come. The professor crouched, holding the stick in both hands, and then slammed it on the floor with a crack that made the boy with the coffee flinch again. A stone the weight of ten men, he said, landing in front of them and bursting into shards. The men running for the gate. The giant stopping just short of it, his chest heaving, because to step off his land was to step out of his strength, and laughing at them over the wall.

Amy wrote it all down. Her pen kept stopping. He told it the way her grandmother had told the stories of the mountain, as if he had stood on the hill and watched, except that her grandmother had always smiled at the end, and he did not.

"And inside the gate," he said, low now, so that the whole room leaned in, "two figures wait. Heracles, with the patience of a hunter. And Athena beside him, watching the giant, already sure how it will end." He planted the stick and stood tall. "They don't speak. They don't move. They wait."

He let the silence go on. Rain ran down the windows. Amy found she was holding her breath, and she let it out, annoyed with herself.

She had come up the stairs this morning to see everything and not be seen, and she had managed it for eleven minutes. Now she knew she would be looking for wind and rain in every source she read this year. She knew, too, that he would remember her.

"So we leave them there," the professor said. "The giant roaring at the gate of his land, and the two in the shadows, ready." He leaned on the stick and grinned up at the room, and it seemed to Amy that he was grinning at the back row.

"What happens next? Get out a piece of paper. Quiz time!"
