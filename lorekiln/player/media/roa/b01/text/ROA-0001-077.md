Episode 77: What leaves the parcel

On Monday morning Sofia Andreou brought the near-final agreement up to the Foundation's room on the second floor of the administration block, and laid it on Nicholas Vardis's desk, and read it to him.

She didn't need to. He had read every draft. But she read every document aloud to everyone it touched, slowly, clause by clause, so that nobody could say afterwards that they hadn't been told, and Vardis had never once seen her skip a line for a rich man. He liked her for it. He sat very still and listened.

She was a small, tidy woman with reading glasses on a cord and a green pen she held like a conductor's baton. Twenty-two years of practice, she had told him once, and never a client who read his own contract.

"Clause three," she said. "The name. He'll be registered as Alcyoneus, no surname, born on Pallene, date of birth unknown. The registrar in Kassandra didn't like it. I told him the alternative was to explain to the press why he'd refused a giant a birth certificate."

"Good."

"Clause six, the gold. Held in trust by the university, his to draw on, audited yearly." She turned a page. "Clause nine, protection. Clause twelve, the lyre, in Ms Lambrinou's keeping, visits whenever he likes. She made me write whenever. She's quite right." Another page. "And fourteen point two, at the back, the waiver. One line."

Vardis didn't look at the line. He looked at the window.

"Tell me," he said, as lightly as he could, as if it had only just occurred to him. "Does the contract cover the parts of him that leave the parcel?"

Sofia looked at him over her glasses.

"A hand, say," said Vardis. "If he lost one. If it fell beyond the fence, on someone else's land. Is it still his parcel's business, or is it nobody's?"

She laughed. It was a real laugh, surprised out of her, and she put her pen down to do it.

"Mr Vardis, it's a land contract," she said. "Not a butcher's bill. If he loses a hand on the coast road it's a matter for the hospital at Nea Moudania, and possibly the police." She shook her head, still smiling, and picked the pen up again. "I'll put it in the schedule of things donors have asked me. It's a long schedule."

He smiled back. He didn't say what he was thinking, which was of the tooth.

The guards had told it at the gate on Friday, laughing. A stone had knocked a tooth out of the giant's mouth on Thursday evening, on the shingle below the theatre, while they stood on the path above. That morning one of them had asked the professor, coming back in from the shore, how the giant ate with a gap like that, and the professor had said there was no gap, and walked on. And Vardis had seen the scan from last month, the shed fingernail that had crept across the floor on its own toward the soil. The shingle wasn't the parcel. He didn't know what the tooth and the fingernail meant, and on the parcel, after the signing, nobody would know.

On the wall behind Sofia the Foundation's monitor carried the campus feed, as it always did, with the sound off. Vardis had paid for the cameras. He liked to see what his money did.

At a quarter to twelve the picture on it changed by itself to the camera outside the student bar.

There was a brawl on the terrace there. Vardis saw it over Sofia's shoulder while she read clause sixteen, the costs. Students, a dozen or more, and two or three of the night guards in blue shirts who should have been asleep, and in the middle of it, as always now, Stavros Petridis in the lion's pelt.

Somebody threw a bottle.

It struck the boy on the side of the head, on the lion's hood, and burst, and he didn't so much as blink. He shrugged the glass off his shoulders like rain. A guard came in under his arm a moment later and hit him in the face with his fist, and that one landed. The boy's head went back. Blood came from his nose, bright on the grey picture, and he put his hand to it, astonished.

Vardis began to count. He didn't decide to. He had counted all his life. He counted under his breath while Sofia read on about the costs, the way he had once counted between the lightning and the thunder on a ship's deck in a storm, to know how near it was.

The boy stood there with blood on his lip and his arms wide, and nobody hit him for a while. Then a student swung at him with a chair from the terrace, and the chair came apart on the pelt and the boy didn't move.

Forty seconds, near enough. Vardis had timed the ring's luck all his life, the hours between a warning and its bill, the days between a gift and its price. He knew a rhythm when he saw one. The pelt turned a blow, and then for a while it turned nothing, and then it was stone again.

He said nothing to Sofia. She was still reading the costs.

"Clause nineteen," she said. "Signature. The rector will come down from Thessaloniki to sign for the university as soon as the giant is willing." She looked up. "Which he isn't. Ms Lambrinou says he's sat on that beach for five days. The professor's been down there every night."

"Leave the willing to me," said Vardis.

"To you? Or to the Foundation?"

"They're the same thing."

She took off her glasses and let them hang on their cord, and looked at him with the same face she had used for the registrar.

"Mr Vardis," she said. "I've been doing this twenty-two years. Donors care about the name on the building. They care about the dinner. They don't care about clause fourteen point two, and you've asked me about it three times in a month. Why does the Foundation care so much about one line?"

Vardis thought of the safe on the Anthousa, and the deed under the insurance papers, and condition eleven. If she knew to look, she would find it in an afternoon. She was that good.

"Because it's the line that makes him ours," he said. "Every other clause gives him something. That one is the only thing he gives back. I care about what the Foundation gets for its money. I always have."

It was true. Every word of it was true, and she could check any of it and find nothing. He had been telling truths that hid things all his life.

Sofia looked at him a moment longer. Then she nodded, and put her glasses on, and began to gather her pages, squaring them on the desk.

On the monitor the brawl was over. Stavros sat on the terrace steps with his head back and a wad of napkins at his nose, laughing at something, and the guards had gone.

Sofia went to the door with the agreement under her arm. There she stopped, with her hand on the frame, and turned round.

She wasn't laughing now.

"I'd like to know," she said, "what you meant about the hand."
