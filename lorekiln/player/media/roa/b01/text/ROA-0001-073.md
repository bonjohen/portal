Episode 73: The stairs

On Wednesday afternoon Nicholas Vardis had his driver take him south down the coast road, past Poseidi, to the old amphitheatre above the shore.

The driver left the car on the verge and offered his arm, and Vardis said no. He went in alone through the gate the giant had broken in the autumn. It still hung off one hinge, and nobody had mended it, because nobody came here in winter. The wind came up off the sea through the gap in the wall, and below him the broken seats went down toward the water in their long grey curve, overgrown with weeds and thistle, to the cracked stone stage at the bottom.

The giant sat on the stage.

He sat in the middle of it, in the round of cracked stone where the chorus had once stood, with his back to the seats and his face to the wings. He hadn't moved since Vardis had last seen him, or that was how it looked. Yesterday afternoon he had got down off the long table in the secure lab without a word, and walked past Vardis on his plastic chair, and out through the steel door and the southern fence. He had come here. The professor had followed him and sat with him through the night, the guard at the gate had said, and gone back to the campus at noon to teach.

Vardis stood at the top of the seats and looked down at him for a while. From up here the giant was only a long grey shape, and Vardis wanted to see his face. He wanted to see the arms, too, where the fine pale lines had been fewer on Tuesday than the Tuesday before.

So he began to go down.

The seats had been cut for people to sit on, not to walk down. Each one was a long stone step a little too high for an old man's knee, cracked and tilted, with weeds in the joints. Vardis went down sideways, the stick first and then the good leg, one seat at a time, as he had gone down a great many stairs in his long life.

On the ninth seat his heart stopped.

It didn't miss a beat, as it had on Monday morning on his bunk. It stopped, and stood quite still in his chest, like a man in a doorway who has forgotten why he came. Vardis put out his free hand and took hold of the step in front of him, a weed-grown edge of cold stone, and held it. The sea went on below. A gull went over. He held the stone and waited, and counted, because counting was what he did.

At four it started again, hard, twice, as if to make up for it.

He stayed bent over the step a long time after, breathing. He looked down at his own hand on the stone, an old spotted hand with the gold ring on it, and at the knuckles, thin and swollen at the joints. He thought of the giant's arms on the table. Of new bone drawing together under grey skin, a little more every week, mending where his own were wearing thin. He had watched that mending for a month and never once said to himself why.

"What does an old man want with me?" said the giant.

He hadn't turned round. His voice came up the seats as if the theatre had been built to carry it, which it had. He spoke in the old tongue, and Vardis answered him in it, carefully, the way you answer a dog that has spoken.

"I want to see you mend," he said.

It was true. It wasn't an answer, and the giant seemed to know it wasn't. He said nothing for a while.

Vardis went down the last seats slowly and sat on the lowest one, at the edge of the stage, a few yards from the giant's back. Close to, he could see the salt in the creases of the coat that wouldn't close across his chest, and the long grey hands lying open on the stone, as if they had been put down and forgotten.

"You are the only thing on this land nearly as old as I am," said Alcyoneus. "I smelled it on you yesterday. Fifteen hundred years, near enough. And you're still afraid of a staircase."

"That's how I got old," said Vardis.

The giant turned his head then and looked at him. His face was long and grey and very tired, and his eyes were the colour of wet slate.

"I have died," he said. "On a hill north of here, with an arrow in me and a god's daughter on my chest. It's nothing much, the dying. The dark is quiet. Why does a man who has outlived fifteen centuries fear it so?"

Vardis had never been asked. In all his names, nobody had known enough to ask him.

He thought of the true answer. It was a burning bath house and a ring on a dead man's hand, and after it every year of his life paid for by someone else. It was that he had taken so much he didn't dare stop, in case the bill came all at once when he did. He had never said any of it aloud.

"I have a great deal still to do," he said. "The Foundation. The campus. People depend on it."

It was a donor's answer. He had given it at dinners for fifty years, and it had always been enough.

Alcyoneus looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned back to the wings, as if Vardis had told him nothing, which was true.

"She stood there," he said, after a while. "In the wings, where you can see the stage and not be seen. She stood there for three thousand years and waited for someone to come and play something worth watching. I came too late by a few breaths." He looked at his hands. "I keep thinking that if I sit here long enough, I'll be on time."

Vardis didn't answer. There was nothing a donor could say to that, and he found he didn't want to say anything else.

They sat there as the light went. The sea turned from grey to the colour of lead. Up on the coast road a car went by with its lamps on, and didn't stop.

At dusk the giant got up.

He did it slowly, one hand on the stone and then the other, and stood, and Vardis saw that he was thinner than on Tuesday. He walked across the stage to the far side of the orchestra, where the old wall had fallen and a goat path went down over the edge of the cliff toward the shore. He didn't look back at the wings. He went down the path in long careful strides, bending under the thorn bushes.

Vardis climbed back up the seats. It took him a long time. At the top he stood by the gap in the wall where he could see the path and the strip of shingle at the bottom of it, pale in the last light.

The long grey shape came out onto the stones and stopped. Then it sat down, with its knees drawn up and its face to the sea, and was still.

Vardis watched it for a quarter of an hour. It didn't move again. It didn't look up at the theatre, where the Muse had stood.

He understood it the way he understood a tightening of the ring, all at once and in his body. The giant had stopped wanting anything. Not the land, not a home on paper. Not even to be on time. And a man who wants nothing signs nothing.
