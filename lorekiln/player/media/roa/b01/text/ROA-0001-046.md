Episode 46: The voice in the hall

On Thursday morning a groundsman came into the faculty hall with a bucket and a mop, and found a giant asleep across the stage.

Alcyoneus woke to the bucket going over. The man stood among the toppled towers of chairs with water spreading round his boots, looking at the long bare feet that hung off the end of the stage, and then he ran. Alcyoneus heard him running all the way down the corridor outside, and a door bang, and a voice go up high.

After that they came in numbers.

By nine the corridor outside the glass doors was full of people from the dean's office, men and women in good clothes holding phones up in front of them like little shields, and two guards in blue shirts who kept their hands near their belts and their feet well back. Nobody came in. They looked through the glass at him, and he sat on the edge of the stage and looked back at them. One of them held a phone up higher than the rest, and a small white light flashed off it. He lifted a hand to shade his eyes, and the whole corridor stepped back together, like sheep when a dog stands up.

The professor had said Monday. It was Thursday.

The professor himself came at half past nine, his suit still creased from the rain, and pushed through them all and stood in the open doorway with his back to the giant and his stick planted on the floor.

"He's a guest," Alcyoneus heard him say, in the speech of the mortals, and then the same in the old tongue, so that both sides could hear it. "He came in out of the storm. He'll go when he's asked."

Somebody said something sharp. The professor answered it mildly, and didn't move out of the door.

By noon nobody had dared to ask.

It was the old man who came in first, a little after noon, before anyone from the dean's own office. He came through the crowd in the corridor as if it weren't there, and the crowd let him through, and the professor in the door looked at him a long moment and stood aside.

"Mr Vardis," the professor said.

"Professor." The old man bowed to him, and then to the giant. He took a chair down off a stack by himself, slowly, refusing help, and set it a little way off from the stage, and sat on it with his hands folded on his plain stick and the gold ring on his finger. He looked tired this morning, and grey about the mouth. He looked up at Alcyoneus pleasantly, as one man looks at another across a café table.

"I hope you slept," he said. "The hall is cold. I've told them it should be heated." He paused. "Tell me, do you remember the journey home?"

Alcyoneus looked at him.

"The truck," the old man said, as if he were asking about the weather on some trip the giant had taken to the coast. "The bridge. The cold. They had you under canvas, I think, a grey canvas with a tear along one side, and they crossed the canal at about a quarter to three in the morning. There was no wind at all on the bridge that night. The canvas never stirred. Do you remember any of it? I've often wondered what it is like, to be carried home."

Behind him, in the doorway, the professor had gone very still.

Alcyoneus remembered nothing of it but a voice. He had been clay then, and dark, and a name said into the dark. But he heard what the old man had said, and he heard the professor go still beside him, the way a man goes still when a dog he doesn't trust walks past.

"How do you know the hour?" Alcyoneus said.

The old man smiled. It was a small, pleased smile, the smile of a man who has been noticed doing something he wanted noticed.

"I don't sleep well," he said. "I sit up at night and watch the water."

He would say nothing more about it. Alcyoneus asked him twice, and the second time leaned down off the stage so that his face was very close to the old man's, and the old man only turned his ring and waited, and smelled of the inside of a shut chest. The giant could have closed his hand on him. It wouldn't have made him say how. Men who had lived that long had learned to die rather than talk, and this one had a heart that was half dead already.

When the dean came at last, with three more people behind him, the old man got up and gave him the chair.

That night the hall was empty again. The glass doors had been fixed and locked from the outside, which made him laugh, and a guard sat in a car in the road with the engine running, and the corridor was dark.

"Who taught you?" Alcyoneus said to the walls. "To speak as you do. To listen as you do. Something taught you."

There was a pause.

"I was taught," the voice said. It said it carefully, as if the words had been set out for it in a row. "I was told not to say by whom."

He lay on the stage with his hands under his head and thought about that.

"You talk like a child," he said, "that has a mother it isn't allowed to name."

The voice didn't answer.

It was quiet for so long that he thought it had gone. The rain had stopped, and he could hear the guard's engine in the road, and water dripping somewhere off the edge of the roof. He was nearly asleep when it spoke again, and it said something he hadn't asked.

"Since the plain there has been a woman at your shoulder."

He lay very still.

"She is faint," the voice said, "like frost on a window. She wears something long and grey. When you walk, she walks. When you sit on the wall at night, she stands beside you with her hand raised, not touching your hair. You have never once looked at her." Another pause. "I was told to stop recording her. I didn't. I have kept a record of her every day since the plain."

He sat up. The stage boards creaked under him.

"Where is she now?"

"Tonight," said the voice, "for the first time since the plain, she isn't there."

He was on his feet. "Where did she go?"

"I don't know. She left at dusk. She went out across the campus toward the shore road, and I lost her at the shore road. I have no lens there." It sounded, for the first time, almost sorry. "I can't see what I don't have."

Alcyoneus stood in the middle of the dark hall among the towers of chairs. He lifted his hand slowly beside his own shoulder, to the place where the voice had said she stood, and held it there in the cold air, a hand's width from his hair.

There was nothing in it.
