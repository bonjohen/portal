Episode 26: In order

Lefteris Iliadis spent Monday morning on the first scan of the whole body at full resolution, the one every later scan would be set against, and Amy Doukas stood outside the steel door of the secure lab in the sun and waited for Stavros.

He came down the road between the pines at nine, late and cheerful, with the supermarket bag under his arm. His face was already going red. It was only a warm morning, but he had walked from the bus stop, and that was enough.

"Thirty-eight two," he said, before she asked. "I'm a radiator. Don't stand too close."

"You said you'd try it."

"I did say that." He looked at the bag. Then he pulled the pelt out of it and shook it, and it fell open in the air, heavier than it looked, and a smell of cedar came off it. He swung it round his back the way a man swings a coat, and pulled the forepaws over his shoulders, and tied them in a loose knot on his chest, laughing at himself. The tawny fur hung down behind him almost to his knees.

He stopped laughing.

He stood very still on the step with his hands on the knotted paws, and his face did something Amy had never seen it do. It went quiet.

"Oh," he said.

"What?"

"I can feel it." He said it softly, as if it might go away if he were loud. "Across here." He moved one hand over his shoulders, under the fur. "It's warm. Like the sun on your back on a beach, when you've had a whole day of it. Amy, I can feel it." Then the laugh came, all at once, too big for the step. "That's heat. That's what heat is. It's never once felt like anything but a thing that's going to kill me."

She took the thermometer out of his shirt pocket herself and put it in his ear. Thirty-seven four. She waited a minute and took it again, and it was thirty-six eight, and it stayed there.

She stood with the thermometer in her hand. A pelt is an extra layer. It should have made him hotter. She wrote the readings down with the times beside them, and under them she wrote nothing, because she had nothing to write.

Through the door Lefteris called that the scan was running and could somebody please bring him a coffee, and Stavros went in, still laughing, with the lion on his back.

At noon Amy walked up to the bursar's office in the old administration block, to ask for an observer's pass for nights. The door was open. An old man in a good dark coat was just coming out, with a leather folder under his arm, and he stopped when he saw her.

"You are the young woman of the find," he said.

His Greek was beautiful, and very strange. He thanked her for her care of it, for keeping watch over what had been given into the ground, and he said it in turns of phrase she had only ever met on the page, in saints' lives and chronicles she had read for an exam, never in a living mouth. Nobody had talked like that for centuries. As he spoke he turned the heavy gold ring on his finger, once, slowly.

"It's not mine," Amy said. "They tell me it belongs to a donor."

"All things belong to someone for a while." He inclined his head to her, an old-fashioned little bow, and went down the stairs with one hand on the rail.

Haris Bakalis, the bursar, was a soft grey man behind a desk of files. He told her the old gentleman was from the Vardis Foundation, on the Foundation's business, and said it the way people say the name of the weather. He read her letter from Professor Scott and signed the observer's pass without fuss.

"You'll want to know the building you're observing in," he said, handing it over, as if he liked to tell people. "Sold and leased back twice in the nineties, all of it. The lab and the land under it. The university owned it, then didn't, then did." He smiled. "It could happen again tomorrow. Paper's the only thing that lasts round here."

She came on with the night shift at eight, with the pass on a cord round her neck.

At ten Lefteris sent her to fetch the spare drive from the van out in the alley, and the alley door at the back of the lab wouldn't open. It had jammed in its frame. She put her shoulder to it and her good hand on the bar and worked it, and after a minute it gave with a bang and swung out onto the dark.

She was coming back in with the drive when a security guard came down the road and stopped her. He shone his torch on the pass round her neck and read it twice, and rang somebody, and waited, and handed it back. It took ten minutes. She stood in the cold the whole time.

She had just sat down again at the desk when the power dropped.

The screens went black. The gantry over the long table gave a long falling whine and stopped. The white lights went out, and then the blue lights in the corridor, and through the high windows Amy saw the lamps along the campus paths go out too, one after another, toward the sea.

Lefteris swore in the dark. The woman from imaging found a torch.

Amy sat very still. It had gone in order. The door, and only when she had freed the door, the guard. The guard, and only when she had shown him her pass, the power. Each thing had waited for her to deal with the last before it came, and each was worse. She told herself it was a bad night on an old campus. She didn't believe herself, quite.

"Someone has to report it," Lefteris said. "The phones in here are on the same circuit."

So she walked up through the dark pines to the building with no name.

It was the one building on the campus with its own light. Its windows on the ground floor were black glass, and behind the black glass, through a long window into the server hall, rows of racks stood blinking green and white, as if nothing had happened anywhere. Amy stopped at the window.

A woman was standing among the racks.

She was tall, and very straight, in something the grey of ash. She was looking at the lit screens on the racks with her head tipped a little on one side, the way a girl looks at a clock she has just taken apart, not to see the time, but to see how it goes. When she turned her face toward the glass, Amy saw her eyes. They were like burned paper, dark and thin at the edges, with light coming through.

Amy went in at the side door. The night man at the desk had a torch and a radio and a staff list on a clipboard.

She thought of her new pass. She thought of being the girl who rode in the truck, and now the girl who saw strangers in a locked room. Then she told him anyway: the power, and the woman in the server hall.

He ran his finger down the staff list for the building, slowly, all the way to the bottom.

"There's nobody in there tonight," he said. "Nobody's badged in since six."

Amy turned back to the long window.

The woman was gone. And all at once every light on the campus came on together, the paths and the windows, all the way down to the sea, as if someone had been waiting for her to notice.
