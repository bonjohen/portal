Episode 49: Only one parcel

On Saturday morning the road behind the conservation building was taped off from verge to verge, and the university sent paper.

It came to the faculty hall at ten, in the hands of a woman in a dark blue coat, with a young man from the dean's office walking half a step behind her and carrying nothing. She stopped inside the glass doors and looked round the hall slowly before she came any further. She looked at the towers of chairs, and the ones still lying where the giant had knocked them over in the storm, and the stage, and the giant sitting on the edge of it. She looked at all of it the same way, as if each thing were a line in a document she had been asked to check.

Then she walked the length of the hall and sat down on the edge of the stage herself, a polite distance from him, and set a grey folder on her knee.

"My name is Sofia Andreou," she said. "I'm a lawyer. I act for the university."

Alcyoneus didn't understand her, and she saw it. She looked round the hall again.

The girl with the tape had come in behind them without anyone stopping her. She stood by the doors in a grey jumper with her notebook under her arm and a stopwatch on a cord round her neck. The lawyer looked at her and something in her face recognised a name.

"Miss Doukas," she said. "You're in the insurer's report."

"I know," said the girl. "I speak some of his language. Not well. I can put it into it for you, if you go slowly."

"I always go slowly," said the lawyer.

So she went slowly, and the girl came and stood between them and put it into the old tongue a sentence at a time, stiff and careful, stopping to find words. The lawyer waited for every sentence to be finished before she began the next one. She said it all so that nobody could ever say afterwards he hadn't been told.

There could be an agreement, she said. It would give him a name in law, so that he existed on paper and could open a door at a bank or a hospital. It would give him a roof, this one or another, for as long as he liked. It would give him protection. Men with shields would not come up the road again. In return the university's ground would be settled once, in one instrument, so that nobody need ever argue about it again.

"Whose ground?" Alcyoneus said.

"The campus," said the lawyer, when it had been put to her. "The land the university holds here. It's all on the map."

He looked at her for a long time.

"I will sign nothing," he said, "that takes my soil."

The girl put it into the lawyer's speech. The lawyer smiled at him, warmly, as one smiles at a nervous client, and the girl turned her answer back into his.

"It's only one parcel," she said.

He didn't like the way the girl said it. He didn't like the way the lawyer had smiled. He didn't know the word parcel, and when the girl tried to explain it she said it meant a piece of land with a line drawn round it, and he thought of the canal, and said nothing.

"I have something," the girl said then, to the lawyer, in her own speech, and then for him in his. "Evidence."

She opened her notebook.

She read the lawyer his numbers. His height, against the wall, and his height again on Monday, the same to the centimetre. The span of his hand. The heat of his skin, half a degree above any man's and steady. His stride, paced and counted on the plain, and the width of his shoulders, growing a little every day. The count his great blow took to come back, which she had timed four times on the plain since Saturday and found the same every time to the second, though she still didn't say what it was. The cut across his palm, which she had seen on Saturday as a pale line and which was gone by Monday.

"He's a man the law can describe," she said. "He's a set of measurements, like anyone. You can write him down. You don't need to settle him like a boundary."

The lawyer listened to every number. She wrote two of them down on the inside of the grey folder in small neat writing. Then she thanked the girl, and it was plain to Alcyoneus, who didn't understand a word of it, that the numbers hadn't moved her an inch. They had told her what he was made of. They had told her nothing about what he was.

He watched the girl hold her numbers out in front of her, as if they could hold off men with shields, or hold anything at all. She had nothing else to hold out. He found that he pitied her, and the feeling surprised him.

The lawyer stood and gathered her papers into the folder.

"I'll leave this with the dean's office," she said. "There's no hurry. Nobody will make you sign anything." The young man held the door for her, and they went out.

The hall was quiet after that. The light moved across the floor. The girl sat down on a chair she took off a stack, with her notebook on her knees, and didn't go.

"Here is where I stand," Alcyoneus said, after a long while. He said it more to himself than to her, the way a man says over the road he has come so as not to lose it. "I woke in the clay to one voice. I have learned your buses, and your money, and the glass that answers. I told the fields I would rule. And the woman at my shoulder has been gone two nights."

The girl wrote it down. He saw her write it as a list, one line under another, with a little dash in front of each, the way she wrote down everything.

"What will you do now?" she said.

"What I have done every evening. Take the bus and look."

She sat with her pencil over the page. She seemed to be deciding something, and not liking it.

"There's something in my notebook," she said at last. "It isn't evidence. I don't know what it is." She turned back several pages. "Professor Scott. Whenever anyone says the word tragedy, in the lecture, in the seminar, anywhere, he looks south. Every time. I wrote it down eleven times before I noticed I was doing it." She closed the book. "It's probably nothing."

Alcyoneus said nothing for a long while.

At dusk he left the hall and went down through the pines to the shore road, to wait for the bus to Kalandra, as he had most evenings. It was the ninth evening.

He looked back once from the road. The girl was standing on the steps of the hall with her notebook against her chest, not looking at him. She was looking past him, down the shore road. A long way behind him, coming along the edge of it in the last light, was a small grey figure with a black stick, following.
