Episode 57: For ever

On Wednesday morning Amy Doukas limped across the campus to the faculty hall with a draft agreement in a plastic sleeve, because Professor Scott had asked her to read it to the giant.

"You've read to him before," Scott had said on the phone at eight. "In front of Sofia. He trusts the way you do it. You go slowly and you don't add anything." He had sounded tired. "His modern Greek will do for bread and bus times, and nothing a lawyer writes. And I'd rather he heard it from someone who isn't on a side."

"Am I not on a side?"

"You're on the side of the numbers," Scott said. "He knows that."

Her hip ached at every step and her head was full of cotton. The hand's wrist still sat a little crooked, and she carried the sleeve in her left.

The faculty hall was warm now. The university had sent a heater, and somebody had stacked the fallen chairs back into their towers, and the giant sat on the edge of the low stage with his coat folded beside him. By the glass doors, on a chair of his own, sat Nicholas Vardis.

"The Foundation's office had the copies made," he said when he saw her looking. "The dean's printer was broken. I hope you don't mind an old man listening."

Amy minded. She didn't say so. She sat on a chair facing the giant, a stride from his knees, and took the draft out of its sleeve. It was nine pages long, with Sofia Andreou's initials at the foot of each page and a note on the front in another hand: draft for discussion, not for signature.

She read it to him a sentence at a time, first in the modern speech as it was written and then, more slowly, in the old tongue, as near as she could make it. He listened with his eyes on her face. When she stopped to find a word he waited, and didn't help.

Most of it she could follow. He would have a name in law, chosen by himself and written down by the state, and papers that said so, and a roof, this hall or another like it, for as long as he lived. He would have the university's protection, which meant that no one would come up the road again with shields. He nodded at each of those. At the clause on protection he said something under his breath that she took for a laugh.

Then she came to clause seven.

She read it once in the modern speech, and stopped. She read it again, slower. It was one sentence, and every word in it was plain. The Signatory gives up for ever every claim, ancestral, sovereign, inherited or of property, in or to the Parcel described in the Schedule, and every claim that may arise from any of them.

Amy read the draft's one clause three times and couldn't parse it however slowly she read: the signatory gave up all claim to the parcel, for ever.

She knew what each word meant. She didn't know what the sentence did. A man could give up a claim to a field, she thought, if he owned the field. But a claim that was sovereign was a king's, and a claim that was ancestral belonged to the dead, and she couldn't see what either of those had to do with a parcel of university land on a map, or why anybody would need to ask a giant to give them up for ever.

"Read it again," the giant said.

She put it into the old tongue as well as she could. She had no word in his speech for parcel, so she said a piece of land with a line drawn round it, as she had in front of Sofia.

"What does it mean?" he said.

"I don't know," said Amy. She turned the page to the margin, where somebody had pencilled a single word beside the clause. "The lawyers have marked it routine."

"Routine," the giant repeated, in the modern speech, as if he were tasting it.

By the door Vardis sat with his stick between his knees and his eyes half closed, like a man listening to music he knew well. He didn't move. He didn't look at the page at all.

She read the rest. It was signatures, and the address of a court in Thessaloniki. When she had finished the giant took the nine pages out of her hand and held them up to the window, as if the light might show him something the words didn't, and gave them back.

"I will think," he said.

At two she was in the kitchen of the graduate block, because her room was too small to sit in all afternoon, when Stavros came in with the lion's pelt over his arm.

He had slept on her floor and gone off to a lecture after breakfast. He filled the kettle at the sink. Then, before he lifted it, he swung the pelt round his shoulders and pulled the lion's head up over his hair and knotted the forepaws on his chest. He carried the full kettle three steps to its base and set it down. Then he took the pelt off again and folded it over the back of a chair.

"Stavros," said Amy.

"Hang on." He had pushed up his sleeve. Across the inside of his left forearm, where the pelt had lain, the skin was red and raised in a line of small clear blisters, new ones, beside old ones that had gone brown. He took his phone out and photographed them, close, and laughed at the picture. "Look at that. Two minutes. That's two minutes."

When the tea was made he went up to his friend's room on the floor above for a cable. He put the pelt on to climb the stairs. She heard him on the landing laughing, and when he came down again he had it over his arm, and he showed her a new red patch on the back of his neck on the screen, photographed in the mirror on the landing.

She took the phone out of his hand. He let her. It was open on a gallery, and the gallery was all skin, his forearms and the backs of his hands, red and blistered or gone brown, rows and rows of it, each one dated.

"Please stop," she said. "Please. You don't need it to carry a kettle."

"I know I don't." He took the phone back gently and smiled at her. "Amy, it's the only time anything feels like anything. In it, I mean. The rest of the time it's like being wrapped in cotton wool. I don't feel it. It's not the same." He put the phone in his pocket. "It's only blisters. They heal."

She had nothing to say to that which he hadn't heard all his life.

At dusk she went back to her room and Urania was at the window, standing between the bed and the glass with her back to the sea, and asked her again to heed them.

"No," said Amy. It was the second evening.

When she had gone Amy sat on the bed and took out her own phone and opened the photographs Stavros had sent her that afternoon, because he had sent them, laughing, every one. She counted them, and set them by their dates. In the first, a week ago, the blisters covered a patch on one shoulder the size of her palm. In the last ones they covered both forearms and the back of his neck, twice the skin, by her rough count, that they had covered seven days before.

In the newest one he was smiling into the mirror.
