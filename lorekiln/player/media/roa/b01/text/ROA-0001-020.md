Episode 20: A note in the wood

Campus conservation store, catalogue card 4471: "Lyre, tortoiseshell and horn, provenance unknown. Strings modern. Do not play."

On Saturday afternoon, three days after the theft, the three sisters walked the Pallene Research Campus and nobody saw them. The paths ran between low white buildings and pine trees, and the sea showed grey beyond the fence where the protected shore began. A student cycled through Melpomene without slowing.

Melpomene was looking for the warmth. She had felt it first on the night the truck crossed the canal at Nea Potidaia, a small heat low in her chest, like a hand held near a stove. It had not left her since. On the campus it came and went, stronger on some paths than others, and she followed it the way she had once followed the sound of a crowd going quiet.

"It is the clay," Urania said. "We know where the clay is. We should be with the girl."

"Not yet," Clio said. She had her scroll in both hands, pressed against her ribs.

They came round the back of a long single-storey building with a loading bay, and the warmth faded to almost nothing. Melpomene stopped anyway. A van stood at the bay with its back doors open. A woman in a grey cardigan and blue gloves was signing a sheet on the driver's clipboard, and two crates waited on a trolley beside her.

A sign on the door said the conservation store was for staff only. The three sisters went in behind the trolley.

Inside, the light was flat and white, and the air was cool and dry and smelled faintly of glue. Steel shelves ran the length of the room. There were numbered boxes and a row of pots with paper tags tied to their handles. At the far end a single window looked north across the campus.

The woman wheeled the trolley to a bench and opened the smaller crate with a flat bar. She did not touch what was inside. First she lifted out the paperwork, a brown folder and a card in a plastic sleeve, and she read the folder through standing up.

"Zoe," the van driver called from the door. "You want me to wait?"

"No," Zoe Lambrinou said, without looking up. "Nobody else wanted it, so nobody's coming back for it."

The van pulled away. Zoe set the folder down and held the card under the lamp. She read it aloud, slowly, the way a person reads a thing to be sure it says what it seems to.

"Lyre, tortoiseshell and horn, provenance unknown. Strings modern. Do not play."

She laughed. It was a short laugh, more breath than sound, and she shook her head at the card. "Do not play," she said. "Eleven words, and three of them are a joke."

Melpomene stood off to one side, by the end of the shelves, where she could see the bench and not be in the way of it. It was where she had always stood. For longer than anyone had kept count she had watched from the wings while other people took the light, and she had never minded. You saw more from the side.

Zoe parted the packing straw with her gloved fingers. The lyre lay on its back in the crate. It was not large. The sound box was a tortoise shell gone dark with age, its plates worn smooth, and the two arms that rose from it were horn, pale and curved like the horns of a young goat. The strings were bright and cheap, the kind sold in any music shop for a few euros.

Urania leaned close to Melpomene. "It's an old instrument. There are a hundred like it in every museum in the land. Come, the girl is on the far side of the campus, and every day she doesn't listen is a day off my count."

"A moment," Melpomene said.

She did not know why she wanted the moment. The warmth of the clay was faint here. There was nothing in this cool white room for her.

Zoe slid both hands under the sound box and lifted the lyre clear of the straw. She held it a moment at arm's length, judging the weight, then turned with it.

As Zoe carried the lyre toward the shelves, her thumb caught the lowest string, and Melpomene heard a note she had last heard sixty years ago.

It was not a plucked note. Nylon on old horn should have given a dull small sound, a thread snapping in a drawer. This was round and full and it went on too long. It rose a little at the end the way a voice rises when it is about to sing the next line, and it was a voice, or it was very like one. Melpomene had heard it last through a closed door, in the late summer of a bad decade, a door none of them had been let through, and for sixty years she had told herself she had heard it wrong.

She put her hand against the steel of the shelf and could not feel it.

Zoe stopped walking. She looked down at the lyre in her hands, and then at her thumb, and she frowned. "Well," she said. "You've got some resonance in you." She set it on an empty shelf at chest height, square and careful, and stepped back to look at it.

Melpomene did not move. She would not let herself hope. Hope was the first thing a tragedy gave you, she had taught that to a hundred playwrights, and it was always given so that it could be taken.

"Sister," Urania said. "What is it?"

"Nothing." Melpomene took her hand off the shelf. "Grief playing tricks. I hear old songs everywhere these days. In the wind, in the pipes. It is what happens when there are so few of us left."

Urania looked at the lyre and then away from it, and her face was kind and tired. Clio said nothing. She stood and watched Melpomene, not the lyre, and she did not stop watching her.

Zoe went back to the bench and began to write up the card for her own records, her pen scratching in the quiet. Outside, the afternoon went to evening, and lamps came on along the campus paths one after another.

Melpomene went to the window.

From there she could see across the dark pines to a building with no name on it, and beyond it the low block of the secure lab with one row of lit windows. She could not see the clay. She did not need to. The warmth came back into her as she stood there, stronger than it had been all day, and with it came something she had not let herself feel since the truck crossed the canal. It was not a word. It was the shape of a word, slow and patient, the way a sleeper says a name. It was her name. Far off across the campus, in the dark, the bones were dreaming it.

"Melpomene." Urania was at her side. "We must go to the girl tonight. You saw her at the dig. She is still telling herself it was a dream. If she will not hear us soon, I will have to keep my word, and you know what my word costs."

"I know what it costs," Melpomene said.

"Then come. Clio agrees."

Clio did not say that she agreed. She looked from Urania to Melpomene, and back to the shelf where the lyre sat in the dark.

Melpomene looked out at the lit row of windows. She thought of the girl somewhere on this campus, and of Urania's soft voice making its promise by the fire. She thought of the note, still in her ears, rising at the end like the start of a line.

"Go," she said. "Both of you. I will follow."

"When?"

"Soon. I want to stand here a little longer. I can feel him from here."

It was true, and it was not the reason. Urania hesitated, then touched her arm and went out through the wall. Clio went last. At the door she turned and looked at Melpomene for a long moment, and Melpomene looked back and gave her nothing, and Clio went.

Zoe capped her pen. She switched off the lamp over the bench, then the long lights, one bank at a time, until only the green glow of the exit sign was left. She took her keys from the hook, stepped out and locked the door behind her, and Melpomene heard her footsteps go off down the path toward the car park.

The store was dark and still. The lyre sat alone on its shelf.

Then, with no hand near it, it hummed once more.
