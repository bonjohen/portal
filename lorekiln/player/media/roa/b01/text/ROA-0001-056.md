Episode 56: From above the stairs

On Tuesday morning Nicholas Vardis sat by the wall of the secure lab through the giant's second scan, on a plastic chair Lefteris had found for him, with his hands folded on his stick, and didn't say a word.

Amy Doukas worked round him. The giant lay on the long table under the gantry with his eyes shut and his feet off the end, as he had the week before, and the scanners went down him slowly and back. She took her own numbers with the tape. He was heavier again. The grey seams along his long arms, where the bones had drawn together on this table, had faded to fine pale lines, and on the left forearm one had gone altogether, as if it had never been there. She wrote it down and looked up, and Vardis was looking at the same place.

He watched the arms the way other people watch a fire, without blinking much, leaning a little forward. Once, when the gantry stopped over the giant's chest, he put out his own hand toward the table, an old spotted hand with the gold ring on it, and stopped it in the air halfway and drew it back. The giant didn't open his eyes. Amy thought he knew exactly where the old man was.

At noon the scan finished, and the giant got up and went back to his hall, and Vardis asked her if he might buy her a coffee.

The lab had a kitchen the size of a cupboard, with a kettle and a machine that made coffee nobody liked. He made two cups of it himself, slowly, and gave her one, and sat on the only stool.

"I'll come to the point, Miss Doukas, since I'm old and you're busy." He spoke the modern speech to her, but carefully, as if he had learned it out of a very good book. "I'd like to offer you a place. A doctorate, funded by the Foundation for as long as it takes. A salary from us, not from the university. Your name on the find, at last, where it should have been from the first morning. Every paper on him with your name first."

Amy held the coffee and didn't drink it.

It was everything. It was what she had asked Scott for on the first day of term, standing by his desk, and what she had asked the dean's office for in writing ever since the find. It was the find of the century, hers.

"The university decides whose name goes on what," she said.

"For now." He turned his cup on the counter. "It owns this building. It owns all of them. But a university is like anyone else. It needs money, and buildings are only a kind of money." He said it idly, the way a man mentions the weather on a long drive. "The Foundation could buy the lab building tomorrow, if the university sold it. And then you would never be locked out of it again."

She looked at him. He looked back at her with mild, interested eyes.

"What do you want for it?"

"Only that you go on measuring," said Vardis.

"Measuring what?"

"Him. How he mends. What grows back, and how fast." He smiled. "You're very good at it. Nobody else has thought to do it at all."

"I'll think about it," Amy said.

He nodded as if she had said yes, and gave the ring a turn, and stood up to go.

At three she went down the lab's back stairs with a box of sample bags.

They were concrete stairs, steep and narrow, lit by one long tube, going down to the store room under the lab. She had gone down them a hundred times. The box was light. The offer was going round and round in her head, the salary and the building, and her foot came down on the edge of the third step instead of the middle.

She went the whole flight.

She knew she had hit the wall at the turn, and she knew that the box went one way and she went the other. Then there was a white noise in her ears, and nothing.

And then she was above the stairwell.

She was looking down into it from somewhere near the light, though she had nothing to look with. She could see the whole flight at once, steep and grey, and the sample bags all over the bottom steps like dropped leaves, and the box upside down. At the bottom, on a stretcher laid on the concrete, lay a young woman with her hair come loose and her right hand at a bad angle. A medic in a green jacket was bending over her with a torch. Lefteris stood above them on the stairs with his hands over his mouth.

She knew it was herself. She knew it the way you know your own coat on a hook. She looked down at the top of her own head and thought, quite calmly, that she would need to write this down.

Then the medic was talking.

"Amy. Amy. Can you hear me? Look at the light. Good. What day is it?"

She was on her back on the stretcher, looking up at the long tube light, and her hip hurt, and the medic's torch was in her eyes. She said it was Tuesday. She said it again, because her mouth didn't seem to have done it the first time.

It was a mild concussion, the medic said later in the campus surgery, and a bruised hip. The hand's wrist joint had been knocked out of true, which the hand's own people would have to see to. She wrote it all on a form. Amy watched her write and didn't tell her about the light.

"You mustn't sleep alone tonight," the medic said. "Is there someone?"

"There's someone," said Amy, who had no idea.

In her room in the graduate block, at dusk, she sat on the bed with a bag of frozen peas on her hip and her notebook open, and tried to write down what she had seen from the top of the stairwell. She drew the flight from above, with the stretcher and the medic at the bottom of it. Then she drew herself, small, from overhead, and looked at the drawing for a long time, and couldn't write a single word under it, because she had no word for where she had been standing.

When she looked up Urania was sitting on the end of the bed.

Amy hadn't heard the door. The tall woman sat in her grey with her long hands in her lap, quite at ease, as if she had sat there often. She looked at the drawing, and then at the grey hand lying on the blanket, crooked at the wrist.

"You were hurt today," Urania said.

"I fell down the stairs. People do."

"Heed us, Amy." Her voice was soft. "Only that. Listen to us, and let us show you what you are carrying. That is all we ask."

"No," said Amy.

It was the first evening.

Urania didn't argue. She sat a little longer, and looked out of the window at the sky going dark over the sea, as if she were counting something there. Then she stood up.

"I have counted the evenings left," she said.

"How many?"

Urania looked down at her, and didn't say the number, and went.

At nine Stavros knocked, with a sleeping bag under his arm and a packet of biscuits. The surgery had rung the graduate block, he said, and the graduate block had rung him, because he was the only name on her form. He lay down on the floor by the door and talked until she slept.
