Episode 80: The signature

Draft agreement, clause 14.2, as marked up by counsel: "The Claimant waives every ancestral, sovereign, inherited and property claim to the Parcel. (Routine.)"

On Wednesday morning, with frost still white on the olive terraces below the window, Nicholas Vardis sat in the rector's office on the campus and read the final draft for the last time. The rector had come down from Thessaloniki the night before, signed for the university at eight under the giant's empty line, and left him the room and a pot of coffee. The coffee had gone cold. The ring had gone tight.

It had been tightening since the rector's car came in at midnight, a slow pressure at the base of his finger, the way it always did when danger was coming and not yet in the room. He tried to turn it with his thumb and it wouldn't turn. He had worn it longer than any man alive had worn anything, and he had learned long ago not to argue with it.

Clause 14.2 sat at the bottom of page nine, with Sofia Andreou's note beside it in green ink. Routine. Vardis read the clause twice. He had read enough deeds to know that routine was the word lawyers put beside the lines that mattered, and he knew what this line would do to a man who could not die on Pallene. On the parcel, and only there, the giant would bleed like anyone. Two weeks ago he had found the other thing too, the university's kept right to sell its buildings, which would make him as easy to kill as anyone whenever a sale was open, and he had told no one.

He had brokered the contract anyway. The giant wanted a home and a name on paper, and Vardis wanted the giant close, on the campus, where he could go on watching a body mend that should have stayed dust. He put the draft back in its folder. His hands were steady. They were always steady, because other people's hands shook for him.

In the afternoon the faculty hall smelled of floor polish and wet coats. The long table had been pushed against the wall, because no chair in the building would take Alcyoneus, and the giant sat on the low stage with the agreement across his knees like a child's picture book. Sofia stood beside him and turned the pages. She read each clause aloud, slowly, the way she said she always did, so that nobody could say afterwards they hadn't been told.

Professor Scott leaned at the back of the hall on his black stick, in the grey suit he seemed to sleep in. Amy Doukas stood next to him with her arms folded, watching the giant's face and not the paper. A little way along the wall Zoe Lambrinou waited with the Lyre in its case at her feet. Alcyoneus had asked for it to be in the room, the way another man might ask for a friend to be there, and Zoe had brought it herself because she would not let anyone else carry it.

Vardis stood nearest the door. It was where he always stood.

"Clause fourteen," Sofia said. "Point two." She read it in the same warm, careful voice she had used for the drainage easement. The Claimant waives every ancestral, sovereign, inherited and property claim to the Parcel.

Alcyoneus listened with his head on one side. "Waives," he said. "That means I let go."

"Of the claim," Sofia said. "Not of the land. You'll have the use of it for as long as you live."

The giant laughed, a sound that rolled round the hall and set the windows humming. "For as long as I live. You mortals are generous with your words." He looked across the heads at Vardis. "And you, old man. You say this is good?"

Vardis felt the ring bite. "I say it's a home," he said, which was true, and was not an answer.

Alcyoneus took the pen. It was a fat black fountain pen Sofia had brought from Thessaloniki for the purpose, and it looked like a matchstick in his fingers. He wrote his name in the old letters, slowly, pressing so hard the nib scratched, and Sofia didn't flinch.

The pen left the page. The machines woke then, not a moment before, as if they had been waiting for him to finish.

The lights stuttered first. Every lamp in the hall dimmed and came up again, once, like a long breath. Through the tall windows Vardis watched the delivery robots on the paths outside stop where they were, a dozen white boxes on wheels, and turn together toward the hall. On the roof of the building with no name on it the maintenance drones lifted, one after another, and hung in the grey air. Nobody had called them. Nobody in the room had a hand on a phone.

Sofia lowered the pages. Scott straightened off his stick. Amy said something to him that Vardis didn't catch.

The ring was so tight now that his finger had gone white past the knuckle. Vardis knew this pressure. He had felt it in the Zeuxippos with the roof coming down, and on a hundred nights since, and every time he had walked out of it alive while someone near him didn't. He had watched trouble come round the giant all winter like rooms in a house, each with something worse at the end of it, and here was the next door opening. He looked round the hall the way he had always looked, to see who the bill would land on.

It would land on everyone standing near him. And nearest of all, a few feet from his own shoes, was Zoe, with the Lyre at hers.

A delivery robot had rolled up to the glass doors and stopped there. Its camera wasn't turned on the giant. It was turned on the case.

Vardis crossed to her. He made his walk older than it was, which was easy, because he had been practising for seventy years. "Miss Lambrinou," he said. "Let me take that."

Zoe put her foot against the case. "No."

"The Foundation keeps a strongroom in the administration block. Steel doors and two guards. I can have it there tonight. Somewhere safe."

"It's safe with me." Zoe didn't look at the robot. She was looking at him, the way Vardis had seen her look at an object with no provenance. "I've kept it since the day it came, Mr Vardis. I don't hand it to donors because the lights flicker."

He had never in his life given anything away. He had bought and traded, he had let things be taken from other people, but he had never once opened his hand. He found he didn't know how to begin, so he lied, which he did know.

"Then read the paperwork first," he said, and took a page from Sofia's spare copy and wrote on the back of it, in his small donor's hand, a receipt for one lyre, conservation case, to be held in the Foundation's strongroom pending its return. He signed it Nicholas Vardis, which was the name it would be found under. "You always read the file first. Here is the file."

Zoe read it. Her mouth tightened. Outside, a second robot had joined the first.

"I'm an old man with a bad heart," Vardis said gently. "Forgive me. Let me do one useful thing before the end."

She held on to the handle a moment longer than she meant to, and then let go.

By evening the wind had swung round to the north, and the harbour at Nea Fokea was empty apart from two fishing boats and the long white shape of the Anthousa at her winter mooring. The light in the harbour office was on. Vardis told the man inside he was taking her out for an hour to try the engines, and the man shrugged, because rich men did such things.

He sent his skipper home. He cast off the lines himself, slowly, with his bad heart and his good hands, and took the Anthousa out past the end of the quay with the Lyre lashed to the chart table beside him. The sea was black and short-tempered. Behind him the coast ran south in a string of shuttered towns, and far down it, between Kalandra and Poseidi, the campus lay lit up like a ship at anchor.

The ring didn't loosen. Every time it had tightened, he had stepped aside and let it fall on someone else. Tonight he held the wheel and kept the bow pointed out to sea, and let it squeeze. He had given something away at last, and it turned out to be himself.

He looked back once over the stern.

Behind the Anthousa the first drones lifted off the campus and came out over the water, low and in a line.
