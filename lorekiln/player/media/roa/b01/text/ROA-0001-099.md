Episode 99: Arms out

On Thursday evening, an hour after dark, the rain came in off the sea, and the guards still had not got everyone out of the machine hall.

Alcyoneus stood under the bent shutter with the rain running off his shoulders and watched them try. Rigas had sent his men in at dusk with torches, and they had gone down the rows between the racks taking students by the arm, and some of the students had gone with them, and others had simply moved to another row and sat down again. Every one they brought out came past the giant. He counted them. Thirty, forty. The girl with the nose ring had not come out. Lefteris had not come out. The professor had gone in at seven to talk to them, and had not come out either.

Stavros had gone in after the professor, in the pelt, to help carry, and laughed at something in the dark.

Amy was at the shore gate. He could see her from the yard, a small dark shape under the gatehouse lamp with the rain slanting through its light, standing in the road with her warm hand raised. She had been there since four. Behind her on the coast road a line of cars had pulled in along the verge, and every time someone got out and started toward the gate, Amy said something, and they got back in. He could see that she never once turned round to look at the hall, or at him, and he understood that it was because she knew exactly where he was.

Zoe Lambrinou stood beside him under the shutter with the grey case at her feet. She had stayed when the professor went in, watching the torches move between the racks as if she could tell his from the others.

"The machines stopped hunting it last night," she said. "When this is over it goes home to the store, and nobody plays it again." She crouched and opened the lid. The lyre lay in its foam. "You asked me once if your Muse had left any of herself in anything, the way her sister did in this. I still don't know of anything. But you should meet the one who did."

The giant bent and held his palm a finger's width above the strings, and didn't touch them. He felt what he had felt on the plain when a Muse stood in the wings, a warmth with someone in it, keeping very still, as if she were saving a last song for whoever it was owed to.

"She is there," he said.

"I know." Zoe shut the lid. "Then she can wait till somebody can ask her what she wants. I've kept her this long. The apple can wait in my drawer too, bite and all, till somebody tells me what it is." She stood, and turned back to the torches.

In the yard the foreman stood by his grey box at the end of the cable with his hard hat dripping, and his radio crackled on his chest.

The giant heard it. He had been listening for it all evening. A voice on the third floor of the white building, the same crisp, certain voice as before.

"Now," it said. "Do it now. The building's empty."

The foreman looked at the hall, at the torches still moving inside it between the racks. He opened his mouth to say something into the radio.

The first charge went off before he could.

It was not the foreman's hand. Somebody on the third floor had not waited for him. There was a flat hard crack from the cooling plant, and a flash that lit every rack in the hall white and threw their shadows the length of it, and then fire, low and orange, running along the left wall where the crates had been stacked. Somebody inside screamed, and then a great many people were screaming, and running.

They came running between the racks toward the shutter, students and night staff with two of Rigas's guards among them, and the fire came after them along the wall. And the giant saw what the crew had set and how: one charge to start it, and the rest along the plant, in a line, waiting their turn.

He went in.

He went under the shutter bent double and stood up inside with his head among the burning cable trays. The heat hit his face. The sale was still open. On this ground he could die, and he knew it as plainly as he knew his own name. The ground under his feet was a floor, nothing more. Nothing in it would hold him if he fell.

The people were in the middle of the hall, where the rows crossed, a knot of perhaps twenty who had run the wrong way and found the fire between them and the shutter. They were crouching against the racks with their arms over their heads. The professor was among them, on one knee, with the girl with the nose ring pulled in against his chest under one arm, and his stick in his other hand. Stavros stood in front of all of them, in the pelt, facing the fire, the way he always stood.

The giant went to them in three strides. He stood over them, with his back to the cooling plant and his arms out.

On this ground he was mortal, and he knew it, and he stood over them anyway. Stavros came in under his right arm, close against his ribs, with the lion's head pushed back and his face lit orange. Under his left arm the professor crouched with the girl held against him. The rest were packed in below, against his legs and under the spread of him.

Two strides to his left was the gap the crew had cut in the hall's far wall that morning. Through it he could see the rain coming down in the dark and the ditch of the coast road shining. Beyond that wall was his own ground. It would know him. If he stepped through it now, the blast would go past him, and he would live, and the people under him would have nothing between them and the fire.

He looked at the gap for the length of one breath.

Then he turned his back on it, and spread himself wider.

The next charge went, and the next, all along the plant, and the hall turned white.

In the white of it he saw the plain. The brown earth of Pallene under his feet and his brothers falling round him. Heracles on the far side of the field drawing the bow, and the arrow that came. He saw Melpomene in the wings, off to the side of every fight, standing where she could see. He saw the long dark of the clay, and the long table under the lights with the glass above it. He heard her voice go out among the broken seats of the theatre while he was still climbing the hill. He saw the paper, and his name pressed into it so hard the nib scratched, so that he could have a house where she had last looked for him.

And he saw the people under him now, every face turned up.

He spread himself over the people in the machine hall, and took the blast.

It went into him from behind. It went into his back and the backs of his legs, and on this ground there was nothing in the earth to take any of it away. He felt himself come apart. It did not hurt the way a blow hurt. It was more like being poured out.

He was in a great many places at once, and then in none.

The fire went out along the wall in the rain that came in through the broken roof. In the middle of the hall, where the rows crossed, the racks had come down in a ring round a patch of floor, and inside the ring the floor was black, and on the black the people were moving. A girl was crying. A man was saying a name over and over. The professor got up on one knee, with his sleeve burned to the elbow and the stick still in his hand, and looked round him at where the giant had been. There was nothing standing there. There was ash on the black floor, and blood.

Stavros got up out of it, in the pelt, without a mark on him, and stood looking at his own empty hands.

Nobody said anything for a long time.

Outside, beyond the fence, in the ditch by the coast road, something of his that had fallen on Pallene soil lay in the rain. It was a hand. It lay in the mud at the bottom of the ditch with its long grey fingers open, palm up, filling slowly with rain.
