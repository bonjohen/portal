Episode 70: What the music took

Letter from a house on Athos, 1453, to a scholar who signs himself only S.: "We send the skin you asked after. The brothers could not burn it, and none who wore it would take it off."

On Thursday evening, with the light going off the water, Zoe Lambrinou carried the Lyre down the goat path below the old amphitheatre in its grey conservation case, holding it level with both arms the way she would have carried a tray of eggs. Below her, on the shingle, the giant sat with his back to the theatre and his face to the sea, as he had sat for a week.

Amy went ahead with a torch, a square of gauze still pale over the stitches in her hair. Professor Scott came behind, and Zoe could hear the end of his walking stick finding the stones.

She had not wanted to take the object out of the store. She had wanted, very much, to come. For weeks she had kept the thing in a climate cabinet and written careful reports about its strings, and she still didn't know what it was. When Amy asked her that afternoon, Zoe had said yes before she had read anything at all. In nineteen years as a conservator, that was a first.

Up on the coast road a car door slammed, and two voices started again. Two campus guards had been sent at noon to move the giant on, the second pair since Saturday, and they had spent the afternoon at the top of the path shouting at each other about whose job it was. Two lab technicians had come to blows over a parking space. Every quarrel on the campus seemed to grow as it rolled downhill, and the bottom of the hill was here.

The giant didn't look round at the voices. He sat with his knees drawn up and his long hands hanging between them, all eight feet of him folded on the stones, and there was a fine crust of salt on his grey shoulders. Scott said he hadn't eaten since the theatre. Scott had sat with him every night, and every morning the professor climbed the path a little greyer, his rumpled suit wet to the knees.

"He won't stand," Scott said quietly, at the bottom. "He hasn't stood in a week. If those two come down with more than words, he'll stand for that, and then I don't know what will be left of them."

"Then let's give him something else to stand for," said Amy.

Zoe set the case on a flat rock and opened the catches. She lifted the Lyre out on its bed of foam, the shell of the sound box and the crossbar polished dark by hands that weren't hers. Its catalogue card was eleven words long, and she still couldn't remember writing it.

Amy sat down on the shingle with the Lyre in her lap. The torch lay on the stones beside her and lit her from below, and Zoe saw her flex the right hand, which not three weeks ago had been carbon fibre and wire and now was flesh. Amy still looked at it as if it were on loan.

"Just the first lines," Scott said. "Amy. Just the first lines, and then stop."

Amy nodded, and put the warm hand to the strings.

Zoe had heard her play once before, in the store. Three notes, no more, picked out to see whether the strings would speak. Afterwards Amy had asked her, quite cheerfully, what they'd had for lunch. They had eaten together not two hours before. Zoe had written that down in her log, with the time.

This was not three notes. The first line came off the strings low and slow, like water finding its way between stones, and the second rose under it. The giant's head came up off his knees. Zoe hadn't known a sound could be that quiet and still reach the sea.

"That's enough," Scott said, after the second line.

Amy didn't stop. She looked up at Zoe instead, over the strings, her fingers still moving.

Scott turned to her too. "Zoe. Tell her. You saw what three notes did."

Zoe had seen. She had the page of her log open in her head, the time beside Amy's puzzled, cheerful face. She also had the giant in front of her, who had lifted his chin for the first time in a week and was listening the way a sick man listens to rain. Up on the road, she noticed, the guards had gone quiet.

"Let her finish," she said.

Scott looked at her for a long moment. Then he leaned on the stick and said nothing more, and Amy played on.

Zoe could never afterwards describe the music, though she tried that night in her log. It wasn't sad, exactly. It sounded like someone setting a weight down beside you and telling you it would still be there in the morning, and that you could pick it up then. The giant put one hand flat on the shingle, then the other. He came up onto one knee, and then, very slowly, the whole long length of him stood, until his head was among the first stars and he was looking down at the three of them in the torchlight.

Zoe watched the warm hand on the strings take the music and give back a gap. She knew it the way she knew a varnish was wrong before the tests came back.

Amy played the last line, let the strings ring out, and laid her palm across them.

The giant spoke first. "That," he said, in a voice like thunder a long way off, "is the first thing since the theatre that has held me up." He looked down at Amy. "Who taught you?"

"Nobody," Amy said. She was smiling, a little dazed. "My grandmother sang sometimes, I think. Mostly she told me to eat." She laughed. "I don't know where it came from. Zoe, did you hear it?"

It hadn't taken all of her grandmother. Amy still had the woman's name, and the funeral in February. Zoe asked about the stove carefully. The winter after the accident, she said. Your grandmother, by the stove in Litochoro, telling you about the mountain.

Amy frowned at her. "She wasn't a storyteller."

Amy had told Zoe that story in the conservation store more than once, first while they cleaned the crossbar and last week on the spare stool because Zoe asked, and every time her voice had gone soft on the same words. So Zoe told it back to her. A girl of seventeen with one hand, who wouldn't go out and wouldn't talk to anybody. An old woman who put another log in the stove every night and told her where the Muses were born, on the slopes under the mountain, and how the gods had fought the giants for the sky. How Amy had pretended not to listen all that winter, and had listened to every word.

Amy listened politely, with her head on one side. It was a good story, her face said. It belonged to someone else.

"I'd remember that," she said at last. "Wouldn't I?"

Nobody answered her. Zoe settled the Lyre back on its foam and closed the catches, and she thought it in the plain words she used for her reports: the price was a memory. She would write it down tonight, in ink.

They left the giant standing on the shore, looking at the water. Amy went ahead up the path with the torch and didn't speak. Halfway up, where the path turned under the broken seats, Scott stopped, and Zoe stopped beside him with the case heavy in her arms.

"You were right to let her finish," he said. "I'll hate it for a while. But you were right."

"I'm usually right. People find it tiresome."

"I don't." Scott was quiet. Down on the shingle the giant was a long dark shape against the sea. "I love you, Zoe. I've been a coward about it for weeks, and tonight I watched you choose, and I'm too tired to be a coward any more."

Zoe didn't answer at once. With anything new she read the file first, and there was no file for this.

"There's something else," Scott said. He lifted the black stick a little off the stones, so that she had to look at it. "If I'm ever angry enough to strike with this, it will kill you. Not whoever I'm angry at. You." He set it down again, very gently. "I wanted you to know that before you said anything back."

Above them, on the coast road, the guards had begun to shout again.
