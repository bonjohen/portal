Episode 93: A sale is open

On Monday morning, at nine exactly, the university put the machine hall up for sale.

Alcyoneus saw it happen from the grass under the secure lab's windows, where he had sat most days since the signing. Every screen on the campus changed at once. The big one over the canteen doors had shown the week's menu, and the small ones by the lifts and the library gates had shown the weather. Now they all showed the same white page with the university's crest at the top. A man in a dark coat came down the road from the administration block with a clear plastic sleeve and a roll of tape, and went round the corner into the yard behind the white building, and when he came back his hands were empty.

The giant had never been inside the hall. He knew it only as the chronicler had told it, a long low room at the back of the building with no name on it, full of racks of thinking machines and folded robot arms, with a cooling plant roaring along one wall.

The giant couldn't read the page from where he sat. He had learned the modern letters that winter from the lawyer's clauses and the girl's phone, slowly, the way he had once learned the names of stars, and he could read the big words if they held still. A student stopped on the path and read it aloud to her friend, in a voice that didn't believe it.

"Notice of sale. The building known as the machine hall, with contents. Offers invited. A preferred bidder has been identified." She looked up. "They're selling PYTHIA?"

"They're selling the building," her friend said.

"With contents."

They went on toward the library, arguing.

The lawyer came at eleven.

He knew her walk before he saw her face. Sofia Andreou walked the way she read, carefully, setting each foot down as if somebody might ask her afterwards where she had put it. She had a folder under her arm and her good wool coat buttoned to the neck, and she came across the grass alone, though there were two of the security firm's guards on the path who watched her come. She stopped a few paces from his feet.

"May I sit?" she said.

"It is your grass."

"It isn't. That's rather the point." She sat down on the folder, so that the wet wouldn't come through, and put her hands on her knees. "I'm going to read you something. I read things aloud. I always have. It means that afterwards nobody can say they weren't told." She took a breath. "I've done it to you once already, and I didn't know what I was reading. So I'm going to do it again."

She opened the folder on her lap. It wasn't the agreement. It was older paper, photocopied grey, with a stamp on the corner.

"This is the campus deed," she said. "The bursar brought it to me on Friday, because I was asked to draw up the notice you've just seen, and you can't sell a building without the deed. In the nineties the university sold this land and leased it back, and there are conditions at the back of it that nobody has read in thirty years." She found the place with her finger. "The university keeps the right to sell its buildings. Any of them. At any time, by notice."

"And?"

"And I wrote clause 14.2." She said it the way she said everything, slowly and exactly, but her hands had tightened on her knees. "You gave up every claim to the parcel. That's what made you mortal on it. The professor told me so on Saturday. I didn't believe him, and then I did. But a parcel that's being sold isn't the same parcel. It's land with a door open in it. While a sale is open, the ground under you belongs to nobody's past, and you can die on it. Not bleed, Alcyoneus. Die."

He looked at her a long time. Her eyes were steady. She had been crying, he thought, but not today.

"Did you know?" he said. "When you wrote it. When you read it to me in the hall and I took the pen."

"No."

"You read me every word."

"I read you every word, and I didn't know what one of them did." She shut the folder. "I'm sorry. I've practised law for twenty-two years and I've never said that to a client, because it's the one thing you must never say. I'm saying it. If paper could be unwritten, I'd tear it up in front of you now. It can't. I've looked."

Then he felt it.

It came up into him from below, the way the cold comes up into a man who sits too long on stone. All his life the ground had held him. He had not known it was holding him until it let go. Now the grass under his hands was only grass, and the soil under the grass was only soil, cold and asleep, the way stone must feel to a mortal, a thing to sit on and nothing more. He pressed his palm flat on it and waited for it to answer him, and it didn't.

So that was what they felt, all of them. All their lives.

"How far?" he said.

Sofia understood him. She turned and pointed past the corner of the lab, through the last of the pines, to the old fence where the posts leaned apart. "There. That's the line. Twenty paces, perhaps. Less for you." She turned back. "Please. While the sale is open, go and sit on the other side of it. Sit in the scrub, sit on the road. I'll bring you a chair if I have to carry it from Thessaloniki. I'm not asking you to leave Pallene. I'm asking you to walk twenty paces."

He looked at the gap in the fence. He could see it from here, the wire hanging between the posts, the dry scrub beyond going down toward the coast road. He could be through it in four strides. On the other side the ground would know him.

He had never in his life done what a mortal asked. Not one. He had laughed at them and killed them on the plain, and in three thousand years of clay he had not once wished he'd listened to any of them. And here was a small woman in a wool coat, sitting on a folder on wet grass, asking him for twenty paces, and she was right.

He looked up instead, at the row of high windows over his head, and the glass behind them.

"She stood there," he said. "Every night at three. To look at me."

Sofia didn't ask who. Perhaps the professor had told her that too.

"You can see the window from the fence," she said.

"I can see it from the fence," he agreed. "I can't stand where she stood looking for me." He put his hand flat on the grass again, on the cold ordinary ground. "I have a house. You wrote it for me. I'll stay in it."

"They could kill you here."

"They could." He did not move. "Today, they have not."

Sofia sat a while longer. Then she got up off the folder and brushed her coat, and stood looking down at him with the deed against her chest, and he saw that she was going to read every clause of the sale herself now, slowly, before anyone signed anything.

"I'll come back tomorrow," she said, "and ask again."

"Ask," said the giant. "It is your right."

She went away across the grass. The guards on the path watched her go.

In the afternoon the screens changed again. The white page stayed, and under it a new line came up in red, and the student who had read the first one came back and read it aloud for whoever was near. The preferred bidder's representatives would arrive on Wednesday morning by road from Athens to inspect the building and its contents.

A delivery robot rolled along the path and stopped at his shoulder, so close that its little speaker was by his ear. Nobody else was near.

"Wednesday," said Techne, very softly, only to him. "Did you hear? Wednesday." The brightness was gone out of her voice. "I won't wait for you."
