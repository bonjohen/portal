Episode 5: Rope and Fire

Constantinople, January 532. The city had been burning for two days, and the crowds in the streets still shouted the one word they had shouted in the Hippodrome, Nika, conquer, as if the fire were a race they were winning.

Anastasios was nineteen and had no part in it. He sold lamp oil from a handcart near the Augusteum, and that night he had left the cart and run, like everyone, with his sleeve over his mouth. At the great baths of Zeuxippos the roof of the colonnade was already alight. Smoke poured between the bronze statues, the gods and poets who had stood there for two hundred years, and their faces glowed red in it.

An old man went back in. Anastasios saw him go, a bent sweeper with his broom still in his hand, shuffling into the smoke after something nobody else thought worth saving. Anastasios didn't know him. The old man owed him nothing, not a coin and not a kind word. Anastasios went in after him anyway, and afterwards he could never say why.

Inside, the heat came at him like a wall. He found the sweeper in a side room where the keepers kept the oddments that didn't fit among the statues, cracked cups and a box of old rings on a shelf under a little lamp that was still burning. The old man was trying to lift the box. "They'll melt," he said, coughing. "Somebody wore these once."

Anastasios took the box from him to carry it, and the lid fell open, and the rings went across the floor. One rolled against his foot. It was heavy and gold, with a dark stone, and he pushed it onto his finger without thinking, to have both hands free for the old man's arm.

The ring tightened. He felt it close on his finger like a small hand, and he stopped, and the old man took one step on without him.

The beam came down between them. It came down on the old man and the rest of the rings, and the fire came after it, and then there was nothing left in that room for anyone to carry out.

Anastasios walked out of the burning Zeuxippos with a ring on his finger, through a door he hadn't seen going in. The smoke drew aside for him. Sparks fell in his hair and went out. In the street a woman threw a bucket of water over him and stepped back, staring, because there wasn't a mark on his hands or his face. The man he had gone in for lay dead under the beam, and Anastasios had walked out of the fire without a burn on him. He stood in the cold, shaking, and the ring had gone loose again, as if it had never pressed at all.

At the dig north of the canal at Nea Potidaia the morning came up grey and cold over the tents, and the floodlights over the trench were still on when Amy Doukas clipped into the rope.

Yannis Petrakis stood at the lip of the fissure with his arms folded. He looked at the opening in the rock as if it were one more thing the plain had done to annoy him.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "You photograph and you measure, and then you come up. If one stone moves, I seal it and I phone Thessaloniki, and they send somebody with a title to take it over."

"Nothing's going to move," Amy said.

"You don't know that."

She didn't. That was the trouble. She had found the fissure herself, two nights ago, a crack breathing cold air past the end of the trench, and yesterday, once the crew had opened it with bars, she had seen the wall inside it by torchlight for a few seconds before Petrakis pulled her back by the collar. Carved, not cracked. Somebody had cut that rock. If he closed it now, the find would go to a professor in Thessaloniki with a bigger office, and her name would end up in a footnote, if it went anywhere at all.

She went down feet first. The fissure was narrow at the top and opened out below, and the cold came up to meet her, older and wetter than the cold of the camp. Her headlamp swept the walls. Her prosthetic right hand held the rope while her left fed it through the device, and she had to think about the prosthetic the whole way, as she always did, telling the fingers to close and then watching to see that they had.

At the bottom she stood on a ledge of flat rock with a passage running off into the dark. An iron bolt stood out of the wall from some survey party years before, and she tied off to it. It took her a long time. The knot needed two hands, and one of hers needed instructions. She pulled it tight and leaned her weight on it, and only then let herself look up.

The wall beside her was carved from the floor to as high as her lamp could reach. Giants and gods were locked together there, arm against arm, a spear in one place and a great bowed shoulder in another, the stone worn smooth where water had run down it for longer than she could guess. She took three photographs, and her breath went white in the light.

Then the roof began to come down.

It started as a trickle of grit on her helmet, then a stone the size of a fist hit the ledge and broke. She heard Petrakis shout, far above. She was tied off and couldn't climb, not fast enough, so she ran the only way she had, forward into the passage, with her arm over her head and rock coming down all round her and the noise of it filling the narrow space like a train.

She came out the far side in a cloud of dust, coughing, on her knees. The noise stopped.

It didn't fade, it stopped, as cleanly as a door closing. One last pebble rolled down the pile behind her and lay still. She knelt and listened and counted, because counting was what she did when she was frightened, and she got to sixty and nothing else came down.

The fall had begun only once her knot was tied off, and it had stopped the moment she was clear, and the passage beyond it lay open.

She turned her lamp on the pile. It filled the way she had come almost to the roof, a slope of broken stone. She turned the lamp forward. The passage ahead was clean, without a stone on its floor or a crack in its roof. The dust hung in her light and settled, and the carvings ran on along both walls, deeper into the hill.

That was wrong, and she knew it. A roof comes down where it is weak, and it keeps coming until it has finished, and it doesn't wait for anyone. She took out her notebook with her left hand and wrote the time and the length of the fall as near as she could reckon it, and under that, after a moment, "stopped when I was through." Then she crossed it out, because it wasn't a measurement.

The radio on her chest crackled. "Doukas." It was Petrakis, out of breath. "Doukas, answer me."

"I'm fine," she said. "I'm through. The way back's blocked."

There was a long silence on the radio. "Stay where you are. I'm getting the crew to dig you out, and then I'm sealing it. I mean it this time."

She looked down the clean passage. If they dug her out they would seal it, and somebody from Thessaloniki would come in a month with a team and a grant and walk down this passage first. Clearing that pile would take them an hour at the least. She had her lamp and her camera, and the hour.

"Copy," she said, and turned the radio down, and started walking.

The passage sloped gently down. The carvings grew crowded, giants falling and gods standing over them, and in one place a huge figure with his arms spread wide, holding back a line of men with spears. Her lamp found the end of the passage, a small round chamber, and in the middle of it a shelf of rock.

On the shelf lay something long, wrapped in cloth gone brown with age and sealed in clay the colour of the walls. It had the shape of a body. It was far too long to be a man's.

Amy stood in the doorway of the chamber with her breath going white in front of her, and she found she couldn't take another step, and she couldn't make herself turn round.
