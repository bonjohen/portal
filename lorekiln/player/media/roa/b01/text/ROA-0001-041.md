Episode 41: The wire beyond the olives

From Clio's tapestry, the panel begun in 2028, a thread added later: a woman in grey at the giant's shoulder, stitched so pale it shows only when the cloth is turned to the light.

On Friday at first light Alcyoneus woke on a terrace wall above the stubble, where he had lain down the night before after telling the fields he would rule. The stones were wet with dew under his cheek. He lay a while and watched the sky go from grey to white, and felt the ground under the wall the way a man feels a hand laid flat on his back.

He was the eldest son of the earth, and he had been bones in clay for longer than mortals had kept records. He was not yet what he had been. He had come off the long table an eight-foot stick of a man, and every day since the soil had filled him out a little, the way rain fills a spring that has run dry. He knew what had woken him. In the dark of the clay a voice had said his name, gently, as you would wake a sleeper, and he had answered it.

He walked west, away from the fences of the place where he had woken, to see what was his. The land had changed its clothes and not its bones. The olives climbed the hill in rows where olives had always climbed it, though every tree was younger than any he had known, and under them lay nets spread for the harvest. Below the terraces ran a road of black stone, and machines ran on it with mortals sitting inside them, faster than horses and much louder, each one going somewhere as if it mattered. Past the road, at the edge of the sea, stood a town of white houses with every shutter closed. A sheet of painted tin hung off a wall by one nail and knocked in the wind off the water.

At the top of the terraces a farmer had strung a fence of wire, with little knots of iron twisted into it every hand's width, the kind of thing men make to keep goats in. He put his hand on the wire and swung himself over, and the iron bit into his palm and opened it.

He looked at his hand. The blood came up dark and ran into his fingers and fell, a few drops and then a thin stream, onto the grass on the far side of the fence. He watched it go into the green.

Then he climbed down, and looked again.

The grass was clean. There was no red on any blade of it, and no stain on the earth at its roots. He turned his hand over. Across the palm, where the wire had opened it to the meat, there was only a pale line, already closed, like a cut a week old.

He laughed out loud on the empty hillside, and a crow went up out of the olives. It had always been so on Pallene. What he spilled on this ground, the ground took back and gave him again. The gods had known it, and that was why they had needed to drive him off his own soil before they could kill him.

At his back, close enough to stir the hair on his neck, nothing stirred.

At noon a man came across the stubble on foot.

He came from the direction of the fences, slowly, a small man in a grey suit gone shapeless at the elbows, with a shiny black walking stick in his hand that was rather too long for him. His hair stood up a little in the wind. He stopped a long stone's throw off and leaned on the stick and looked up at the giant, and the giant looked down at him.

"Greetings, son of Earth," the man said.

He said it in the old tongue. Nobody had spoken it to Alcyoneus since he woke. The accent was bad, the stresses all in the wrong places, the way a man talks who has learned a language from the dead and not the living. But every word meant what it ought to mean.

Alcyoneus knew the kind. He had fought it on the plain for the length of a war. It was in the way he stood at his ease in front of something eight feet tall, as if he had stood in front of worse and walked away.

"You are of the heroes' kind," Alcyoneus said. "What is your business with me?"

"I tell stories," the man said. "Yours among them. I've followed you since the plain."

He didn't say which plain. Alcyoneus let it go.

"Then tell me where she is. The Muse of Tragedy. Melpomene."

The small man was quiet for a long time. He looked at the ground between them, and then at the sea, and the wind went over the stubble and came back. His hand moved on the head of the stick.

"I don't know," he said.

He was lying. Alcyoneus heard it the way he would have heard a stone turn under a foot in the dark. He could have reached him in two strides and lifted him by the front of his coat and shaken the truth out of him like a fig from a branch, and whatever the stick was, it would not have been quick enough.

But the man's face was the most tired face he had ever seen on a mortal. It was tired all the way through, the way old wood is grey all the way through. A liar who looked like that was no danger to anyone. He was only a man carrying something heavy a long way.

"Keep your silence, then," Alcyoneus said. "For now."

The man let out his breath. He set a cloth bag down on the stubble and stepped back from it, as you would set down meat for a dog you didn't know.

There was bread in it, round loaves still warm from some oven. Alcyoneus tore one in half and ate it standing, and it was the best thing he had eaten in three thousand years, though it was also the only thing. Under the bread lay a coat, dark and heavy, made for the biggest mortal the man could find. He put it on. The sleeves stopped halfway down his forearms, and the front wouldn't close across his chest by a hand's width, and the man looked at it and nearly smiled.

"There's money in the pocket," he said. "They'll want it for bread."

He held out a folded paper. On it were rows of numbers and the names of places, and the man read the names out, and some Alcyoneus knew from older days: Kalandra, Poseidi, the canal.

"That's the bus." The man pointed at the black road, where a long machine full of faces was going by. "It stops where the paper says, when the paper says, and never sooner. Walk where you like. I won't stop you, and I couldn't. But harm no one."

"And?"

"And come to me before you come onto the campus." He tipped his head back toward the fences and the white buildings in the pines beyond them, the place where Alcyoneus had opened his eyes on a metal table under lights. "Come to the southern fence at dusk, any night. I'll be there."

"Why should I?"

"Because they're frightened in there," the man said, "and frightened people do stupid things, and I'd rather they did them to me."

He turned and went back the way he had come, the stick tapping on the hard ground, and didn't look round. Alcyoneus watched him until he was a small grey mark at the fence. He hadn't asked his name, and he found he didn't need it. He would know that walk anywhere.

He spent the afternoon on the terrace above the road with the paper on his knee, watching the buses. Each one came when the paper said it would, to the minute, and went on. He thought the mortals had made a god of time and fed it with their days, and it was the first thing in their world he understood.

At dusk he sat on the terrace wall where he had slept. The sun went down into the sea beyond the shuttered town, and the hills went dark one behind the other.

"Melpomene," he said to the hills.

The wind had come off the water steadily all evening, cold on his left side. At his shoulder it went still, all at once, as if someone had stepped into it and stopped beside him, close enough to touch.

He turned his head.

There was nobody. There was the wall, and the dark field, and the first lights coming on in the town far below. Then the wind came back and went on past him into the olives.
