Episode 2: A Fish for the King

At twenty to eleven on that first Tuesday the rain was still running down the high windows of the lecture hall in Thessaloniki, and Professor Scott, who had talked for an hour and forty minutes without a note, had just left Heracles and Athena waiting in the shadows and called his quiz.

A groan went down the raked rows like a wave going down a beach. Amy Doukas, in the back row next to the aisle, didn't groan. She took a pen from her bag with her left hand and laid her right hand, the prosthetic one, flat on the paper to hold it still. That took a moment's thought, as it always did. The fingers closed when she asked them to, a little slower than she asked.

"Nothing you'll be marked on," Scott said. "I only like to see what stuck."

He turned to the chalkboard. It was full already, top to bottom, with his Greek, the names of giants and the hills they had fallen on, and he found a clear corner low on the left and began to write the questions there.

Amy watched how he did it, because she always watched how people did things. He held the chalk in his right hand. The walking stick was in his left, as it had been all morning, a shiny black stick rather too long for a man of his height, so that the handle came nearly to his ribs. The chalk ledge ran the length of the board at exactly the right height for leaning a stick on. Anyone else would have propped it there, or hooked it over the end of the desk. The black stick never left Scott's hand, not even when he wrote on the board. He wrote four lines of Greek with it pressed along his forearm like a splint, and when he wanted to rub out a word he did it with the heel of the hand that held it, and the stick came with the hand.

She had noticed it in the first ten minutes. He had paced with it and pointed with it, and once, describing the giant, he had lifted it over his head, and she had started to count. In an hour and forty minutes it hadn't left his hand once. She had wondered whether he needed it to stand, but he didn't seem to. He walked quickly, faster when he got excited, and the stick swung clear of the floor as often as it touched it.

A habit, she decided. Some men can't take their glasses off.

He finished the fourth question and turned round. His grey suit was rumpled at the elbows and white at one cuff, and his hair, which had been only slightly wild at nine o'clock, had given up. He looked like a man well past sixty who had dressed in the dark. Something about him didn't sit right with her, and she put it down to his voice, which sounded younger than the rest of him.

"The first three are what I told you today," he said. "The fourth I didn't tell you. You'll know it anyway, or you'll guess."

He read it out, though it was there in Greek behind him, and he read it slowly, the way a man tells a story to children at bedtime.

"Polycrates of Samos," he said. "A tyrant, and a lucky one. His luck was so perfect that it frightened his friends: every ship came home, and every war went his way. So he was told to throw away the thing he valued most, to pay for all of it, and he took the ring off his finger and threw it into the sea." He let that sit. "Some days later a fisherman brought a fish to the palace kitchen, a fine big one, a gift for the king. The cooks cut it open, and there was the ring." He tapped the board with the chalk. "Question four. What is the story for?"

Someone near the front said, "Fish," and got a laugh.

Down in front of her, a few seats along, a boy laughed louder than any of them. It was a real laugh, out loud, with his head back, the kind people let out alone in an empty room and not in a hall of a hundred strangers. Heads turned, and he didn't seem to mind. Amy knew him a little. Stavros Petridis, a third year, from Kalandra down on Pallene, who had signed up for the dig north of the canal and told everyone at the sign-up table he was going for the tan.

Still laughing, he turned round to say something to the girl behind him, and Amy saw his mouth. There was blood on a lip he didn't know he had bitten. It wasn't much, a bright bead at one corner and a smear under it, where at some point in the last hour he had bitten down hard enough to break the skin. He was smiling straight through it.

The girl behind him stopped smiling. She touched her own lip to show him. He put a finger to his mouth and looked at the red on it with real interest, the way a man looks at a coin he has found in the street, and laughed again.

"Huh," he said. "Didn't feel it."

He wiped the finger on his jeans and turned back to the board. The girl behind him went on looking at the back of his head.

So did Amy, for longer than she meant to. Everybody bit their lip now and then, and everybody noticed when they did. She ran her tongue along her own lower lip without deciding to, found it smooth and felt foolish.

She did the first three questions quickly, in small neat Greek, because they were facts, and facts were easy. Over the fourth she sat with the pen still.

She knew the story. Herodotus, book three. Her grandmother had told it too, at the kitchen table in Litochoro, with the fish a little bigger every time, and her grandmother's version had ended with a warning: the sea gives back what you didn't really mean to give. Amy could hear her saying it. She could hear, too, exactly what Professor Scott wanted on the paper, the jealousy of the gods, in two neat lines.

What Amy believed was that fish swallow shiny things, and that a busy harbour sees a great many fish. It was the sort of thing that happened about once in a lifetime of fishing, and a palace with a good storyteller in it had made a moral out of a coincidence.

She wrote: "A story about a coincidence, told by people who had no statistics. Fish swallow rings. Kings like to believe the sea is paying attention."

She read it over, and it was rude. She left it.

Scott came up the aisle to collect the papers himself, stick in one hand and the growing stack in the crook of the same arm, so that his right hand was free to take each sheet. The hall was already emptying round him, students pulling on coats and checking phones. Stavros went past Amy up the steps with his bag over one shoulder, whistling, the blood dry now at the corner of his mouth.

When Scott reached the back row he took her paper and, instead of adding it to the stack, read it there, standing over her. She watched his face for the moment it would close against her. It didn't. One corner of his mouth went up.

"Miss Doukas," he said.

She hadn't known he knew her name. She had chosen the back row so that he wouldn't.

"You watched my hand all morning," he said, "instead of the board."

Her face went hot. "I was counting."

"I know. It's a good answer," he said, and tapped her paper. "It's wrong, but it's a good one. Find out what became of Polycrates afterwards. Herodotus tells you, and nobody ever remembers that part." He put her paper on top of the others. "Petrakis is short of eyes on the plain this season. You'll go out with the crew this week."

She had asked in the spring for a place on that dig and been told there was no room. She opened her mouth to say so, and found she didn't want to remind him.

"Thank you," she said instead.

"Don't thank me. He complains all day. You'll get used to it." Scott turned to go, and turned back. "He rang me this morning. In nine years on that survey he has never once told me anything was odd, and this morning he said the plain was odd this autumn, and he wouldn't say how."
