Episode 66: A frieze on the steps

On Tuesday morning Zoe Lambrinou came out of the conservation store to fetch a trolley and found a man in a green fleece pointing a laser at her corridor.

He stood at one end of it with a small yellow box held up to his eye, and a red dot lay on the far wall by the fire door. He pressed a button, and the box beeped, and he wrote something on a tablet. Then he walked ten paces and did it again at a doorway.

"Can I help you?" said Zoe.

"Just measuring." He smiled. He was young and pleasant and had a lanyard round his neck with no name on it. "Floor areas, corridor widths, that kind of thing. For the insurer."

"Which insurer?"

He thought about it. "For the Foundation, I mean. They're very interested in the building."

"The Foundation doesn't own this building."

"No," the man agreed. He looked at his tablet as if it might tell him something. "To be honest I don't know who it's for. The office sends me a list and I go and measure what's on it. You're on it." He pointed the box at the ceiling, and it beeped. "Conservation building, all floors. I'm doing the secure lab after lunch."

She went back into the store without her trolley and wrote it in her log, with the time, under the heading for the building. She had never had a heading for the building before.

At noon she crossed the quad to the library to return a book, and walked into the feud.

It had begun as a quarrel on the library steps between two groups of students, one with a painted sheet asking for the giant to stay and one without, and she could see it had begun as words because some of them were still shouting words. Then someone had pushed someone. Now it was a fight, thirty or forty young people on the broad stone steps, pulling and shoving and swinging, and a girl's sheet trampled under them.

Zoe stopped at the bottom of the steps.

It was the strangest thing. She had seen fights before, on the quad and in the town, and they were always a knot, people piled on people, one behind another. This wasn't. Everyone fighting had spread out along the steps into one long band, left to right, the whole width of the library front, like a frieze on a temple. Every one of them stood side by side, nobody behind anybody, as if the steps had been laid out so that each of them could be seen at once.

She stood and stared at it, because she had spent half her life looking at friezes, and she couldn't make it be anything else.

A hand took her elbow.

"Come away," said Professor Scott.

He had come up beside her without her hearing him. He held the black stick flat behind his back in his other hand, out of sight, as if it were something he didn't want the fight to notice, and he walked her backwards off the bottom step and across the paving and into the shade of the pines, without hurrying, and didn't let go until they were there.

"I've just come from the dean's office," he said. He was breathing quickly. "With the giant. He told the dean he means to rule the land he stands on. The dean laughed." He looked back at the steps. "And now this."

"It was like a frieze," Zoe said.

"Yes," Scott said, after a moment. "It was."

In the afternoon the giant asked for the lyre.

He was in the secure lab for his Tuesday scan, and he had sent word up through Lefteris on the console that he would like the old instrument brought down, if the keeper would bring it. So at three Zoe carried the grey case down the road behind her building with both arms, holding it level, and the steel door read her badge and let her in.

The corridor was lit blue. Through the long pane of glass she saw the lab, and the long metal table under its gantry of scanners, and the giant lying on it with his bare feet off the end. Amy had done her measuring at two and gone to the library with her numbers. Scott stood by the wall with his stick.

And beside the table, on a plastic chair, sat Nicholas Vardis.

He didn't look round when she came in. He sat leaning a little forward on his plain stick, and he was looking at the giant's arms. Along the long grey forearms ran fine pale lines, like the mends in old pottery, where the bones had drawn together on this table weeks ago. Some had gone already. Vardis was looking at the ones that hadn't.

Zoe set the case on the steel bench by the wall and opened the catches, and waited for the scan to end, and watched him.

He sat there for an hour, studying the scars closing on the giant's arms. He didn't speak. Once he put out his own hand toward the table, an old spotted hand with the gold ring on it, and drew it back. He had the face of a man reading his own will, slowly, a clause at a time, and finding nothing in it he hadn't expected.

At four the gantry folded back, and the giant sat up on the table.

Zoe brought him the case. He laid his long hand flat across the lid without opening it, and kept it there, and looked at her.

"Keeper," he said, in the old tongue.

She had read that tongue all her working life on cards and pots and the edges of coins. She had never had it spoken to her by a mouth that had spoken it first. He spoke it slowly, for her, and she found she could follow most of it, and Scott said the rest quietly from the wall.

"You keep the old things," the giant said. "So I ask you, and not him. Her sister left herself in this wood. Did she leave anything? Anything at all. A mask she wore. A stone in her theatre, a string. A thread in a cloth." His hand pressed a little on the lid. "You have catalogued old things for years. Have you ever had in your hands a thing that breathed?"

Zoe thought about it honestly, as he deserved. She thought of all her years of shelves, the pots and the coins, the church textiles in their acid-free boxes, the wooden saints. She thought of everything she had ever written a card for.

"Only this," she said. "I know of nothing else. I'm sorry."

He looked at her a long moment. Then he took his hand off the case.

He got down off the table without a word, bending his head under the gantry, and walked past Vardis and past the glass and out of the steel door. Through the high windows Zoe saw him go down the slope behind the lab toward the southern fence, a long grey shape in the dusk, and step over the wire, and go on through the pines toward the coast road.

Scott went after him.

He stopped in the doorway first, with the stick in his hand, and looked at her as if he owed her something.

"There's a theatre," he said. "The old one, above the shore south of Poseidi. Three weeks ago, on a Saturday night, he heard her die there. Melpomene. He came too late by a few breaths, and there was nothing in the wings where she'd stood. That's where he's going." He hesitated. "I'll stay with him."

Then he was gone too.

Zoe closed the lyre's case and did up the catches. She found her hands weren't quite steady.

Vardis rose from his chair. He did it slowly, with both hands on his stick, and came past her to the door with his coat over his arm. There he stopped, and turned the ring on his finger, once.

"Miss Lambrinou," he said. "How long has the lyre been on the campus?"

"Since the fourteenth of October," said Zoe.

"Yes," said Vardis, as if he had known all along and only wanted to hear her say it. And he bowed to her, a small stiff bow from the neck, and went out.
