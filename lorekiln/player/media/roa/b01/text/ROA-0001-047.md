Episode 47: Two thousand years without

On Friday morning Alcyoneus hunted the campus for her in daylight, building by building.

He went out of the faculty hall at first light, past the guard asleep in his car, and nobody stopped him. He went into the library, ducking under its door, and walked the length of every row of shelves while a woman at the desk held a telephone to her ear and said nothing into it. He went through a building full of glass jars and dead insects pinned in boxes, and a building where young men sat in rows in front of lit screens, all of whom stood up and pressed themselves against the walls as he went by. He went into the canteen and stood among the steaming trays, breathing in, because she might have left a smell there as she had on the hills.

She hadn't. Nobody had seen a woman in grey. Nobody had seen anything but him.

So he went back to the one place where he had heard her breathe. Strings in an old wood had breathed in a rhythm he knew, and it was all he had.

The conservator let him in this time. She opened the door at his knock and looked up at him for a moment, and then stood aside without a word and went back to her bench, the way she would have let in a colleague with a box to sign for. He came in bent double under the ceiling.

The professor was there before him.

He sat on a tall stool beside the conservator's bench with his stick leaning against his knee, and he was asking her a question about the humidity in the room. She answered it as she worked, without looking up, the way you answer a question you are asked every morning. She told him the number. He thanked her for it, and nodded, and didn't write it down. Alcyoneus saw that he didn't need it.

"I've checked the lyre," the conservator said to the giant, in her own speech, and the professor put it into the old tongue for him. "Twice yesterday and once this morning. Nothing's changed. The strings are where they were." She lifted the lid of a grey box on the bench and showed him the lyre lying on its foam, and it was only an old instrument, with nothing breathing in it.

He looked at it, and then he stopped looking at it, and looked at the professor instead.

The professor was watching her hands.

He watched them the way a man watches a fire he has been told he mustn't touch. She was lifting something small out of a tray with a pair of tweezers, a flake of painted plaster no bigger than a fingernail, and setting it on a glass slide. Her hands were quick and sure in their blue gloves, and the professor's eyes went with them everywhere they went. When she turned the slide under the lamp, his face changed a little, and when she set it down it changed back.

Alcyoneus had seen that look before. He had worn it himself, on the plain, in the long years before the war, every time a woman at the edge of the field had looked at him across the dust.

The conservator got up and went to a tall cabinet at the far end of the room, and bent to the lowest drawer, with her back to them.

"You love her," Alcyoneus said to the professor, in the old tongue. He said it plainly, as he would have said the sky was clear.

The professor didn't move.

He had gone two thousand years without, Alcyoneus thought, looking at him. It was written on him the way age was written on the old man with the ring. A man didn't look at a woman's hands like that the first time he loved. He looked like that when it was the first time and he had waited much too long for it.

The professor didn't deny it.

"Please," he said, very low, without turning his head. "Don't say it in a language she understands. She reads the old texts. She knows more of that tongue than she lets on."

"Then she should hear it."

"No."

"I loved once where it was forbidden," Alcyoneus said. "I said nothing, and she said nothing, and the gods said a great deal. All that came of keeping quiet was a long time in the clay." He watched the woman at the cabinet. "You should tell her."

"I can't," the professor said. "You don't know what I carry."

The conservator came back with a folder and sat down. She looked from one of them to the other, a little sharply, as if she had heard the shape of the talk if not the words, and then she opened the folder and began to read it.

Alcyoneus sat on the floor with his back against the shelves, and his knees up, and asked them both.

"You keep the old things," he said to her. "And you tell the old stories." That was to him. "Then tell me this. Where does a Muse go, to fade? Not the wood. The place. Where does she go at the end?"

The professor put it into her speech. The conservator looked up from the folder and shook her head.

"I don't know what he means," she said. "Go where? Tell him I'm sorry. I don't."

"I don't know," the professor said.

This time Alcyoneus heard it. He heard it as clearly as he had heard it on the stubble a week ago, the stone turning under the foot, only louder. The small man knew. He had known on the plain and he knew now, sitting on his stool with his eyes on the woman's hands, and he was choosing every minute not to say.

Alcyoneus leaned forward, and spoke low in the old tongue, so that it was between the two of them.

"A trade," he said. "I'll keep your secret from her. I won't say a word of it, in any speech she has. And you give me mine. Where is she?"

The professor looked at him then, for the first time since he had come in. His face was more tired than it had been on the plain, and something in it had set hard, like clay in a kiln.

"No," he said.

"You would sooner I told her?"

"I'd sooner you told her," the professor said, "than I sent you where you want to go."

He held the giant's eyes while he said it, and didn't look away. Alcyoneus had seen few mortals do that. He had seen fewer gods.

The conservator had stopped reading. She was looking at the window.

"Those are ours," she said, in her own speech, and this time the professor didn't need to translate, because Alcyoneus had turned and seen them too.

Out on the road behind the conservation building a white van was drawn up on the verge with the security firm's name along its side, and its door was sliding open. Six men were getting out of it. They wore black and they had helmets, and they were lifting long clear shields down out of the back one after another and buckling them on their arms.
