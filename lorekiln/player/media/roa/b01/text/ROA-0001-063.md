Episode 63: Routine

On Friday morning two police officers from Kassandra brought the conservation store a sealed evidence box, and set it on Zoe Lambrinou's bench as if it might go off.

The older one had the paperwork. A museum in Thessaloniki had been robbed in October, he said, one case forced in the night and nothing else touched. Last week the object had turned up at a dealer's in Nea Moudania, who swore he had bought it in good faith from a man he couldn't describe. The museum wanted it back, and its own conservator was on leave, and before it went home the insurers wanted a condition report from the nearest lab that could write one.

"Which is you," said the younger one.

"Which is me," Zoe agreed, and cut the seal.

Inside, on a bed of crumpled tissue, lay an apple of gold.

It was the size of a real apple, a big one, and it had its own small museum label tied to the stalk with red thread. Ceremonial reconstruction, the label said, in two languages, and a date in the last century. Zoe put on her gloves and lifted it out with both hands, and nearly dropped it.

It was too heavy. She knew the weight of gilt over plaster, and of gilt over wood, and this was neither. It weighed in her palms like a stone from a river. She set it down on the bench under the lamp and turned it slowly with one finger, looking at the surface through her loupe.

The gilding was good and old and worn at the high points. On one side, near the stalk, there was a small bite in it, a crescent where the gold had been pressed in and split, as if somebody had lifted the thing to their mouth and pretended to eat it. Under the split the metal showed darker than gold.

She wrote it down in her log, with the time. Gilt over an unidentified core, heavier than a gilt reconstruction should be. Small bite in the gilding near the stalk, as if someone had pretended to eat it.

"Sign here," said the older officer.

"No, she signs here," said the younger one. "That's the transfer. You sign the receipt."

"I sign the receipt when it goes back."

"That's not what the sergeant said."

Zoe signed both, to be safe. The officers went out still arguing, and she watched them through the north window all the way across the yard to their car, the older one jabbing a finger, the younger one with his arms folded, and they were still at it when the car doors shut.

At three she went to the faculty meeting.

It had been called in the long seminar room on the top floor of the administration block, to approve the terms of the giant's agreement before Sofia Andreou took them to him. The lyre was in the schedule now, so the conservator was asked. Zoe sat at the back with her notebook. Professor Scott came in late and took the chair beside her, and leaned the black stick against the wall.

By the door, on a chair of his own, sat Nicholas Vardis.

He sat very upright in his good dark coat, with both hands folded on a plain stick, and the gold ring on one of them catching the window. Nobody introduced him. Everybody knew him, the Foundation's man, whose name was on half the buildings on the campus. He said good afternoon to the dean and nothing after that.

The meeting went wrong almost at once.

It began with parking. Somebody from biology said that if the giant was to have the faculty hall, the staff who parked behind it would need spaces somewhere else, and somebody from engineering said the spaces behind the hall had always been engineering's, and in two minutes they were both on their feet. Then it was budgets. Whose budget fed a giant? Whose paid for the heater? The head of agronomy said his department had lost a whole season of bird counts to the man walking across the plain, and the dean told him to sit down, and he didn't, and the dean shouted.

Zoe had sat through more faculty meetings than she could count. She had seen people sulk and seen them walk out. She had never seen a room go like this, every small thing catching light off the next, so that by four nobody could agree what time it was.

Vardis watched it all from the door with his hands folded and said nothing.

When they had shouted themselves into a lull, Sofia Andreou stood up at the front with her papers.

She was the only calm person in the room. She waited until there was quiet, and then a little longer, and then she read the agreement through, clause by clause, slowly and clearly, the way she always read, so that nobody could say afterwards that they hadn't been told. Name. Roof. Protection. The gold buried with him, held in trust and his to draw on. Visits to the lyre, whenever he liked, in the conservator's keeping. Zoe heard her own words from Thursday read back to her, and felt Scott beside her shift on his chair.

"Fourteen point two," said Sofia. "The Claimant waives every ancestral, sovereign, inherited and property claim to the Parcel." She looked up. "That's routine. Every settlement of land has one."

Somebody at the front asked about the parking again.

Zoe was writing the clause number down when she felt Scott lean toward her. He didn't look at her. He was looking at the front of the room, and his voice was so low she could barely hear it under the talk.

"It was clause seven," he whispered, "when Amy read it to him. Longer, then. They've moved it to the back and cut it down to one line." His hand had found the head of the stick against the wall. "Zoe. On this ground he can't die. The land won't let him. That clause gives the land up. It's how they did it the first time, only with an army. They drove him off his own soil and then they killed him." He breathed out. "If he signs that, he is mortal on the parcel."

Zoe stopped writing.

She looked at the front of the room, where the dean was arguing with engineering about the heater, and at Sofia with her pen, waiting patiently to go on. Nobody looked frightened. Nobody looked as if they had heard anything at all.

"Does anyone else in this room know?" she said, under the shouting.

Scott didn't answer. He turned his head, very slightly, and looked at the door.

Vardis was sitting where he had sat all afternoon. He wasn't watching the dean, or Sofia, or the quarrel about the heater. He was watching the professor, quite steadily, with his mild old face and his chin almost on his stick. When Scott looked at him, he didn't look away.

Then he gave the ring a quarter turn and looked at the window.

The meeting approved the terms at half past five, eleven votes to four, with two people still shouting about the parking as the room emptied.

Zoe went back to the store. She had the apple's report to start, and she didn't want to go home and sit with what Scott had said, so she put the kettle on and the lamp over the bench, and took her gloves out of the drawer.

The apple sat where she had left it, on its tissue under the lamp.

She put her hand on it to turn it, and stopped.

It was warm. Not the warmth of a lamp on metal, which she knew, which came off a surface and went. This was warm all the way through, the way a stone is warm when you pick it up off a beach at evening, as if it had been holding the heat of something for hours.

She took her hand away.

Out in the corridor, beyond the store door, she heard the two night guards come on shift. She heard them say good evening to each other. And then, all at once, she heard them start shouting, both together, their voices going up and up, about nothing at all that she could make out.
