Episode 3: How the heroes ended

The seminar met at four that afternoon, in Professor Scott's office on the third floor of the old arts building in Thessaloniki, because the room it was meant to meet in had been given to a conference on supply chains. Eleven students fitted into the office if four of them sat on the floor. Amy Doukas didn't sit. She stood by the door with her back against the bookcase, which was the nearest thing the room had to a back row.

It was a narrow room, warm now that the rain had gone and the sun was out, and it smelled of dust and pipe tobacco, though nobody had smoked in the building for twenty years. Scott sat on the corner of his desk in the same rumpled grey suit he had lectured in that morning, the black stick across his knees. His hair stood up at the back as if he had slept on it.

"This morning was the giants," he said. "This afternoon is the men who beat them. I want you to notice that none of them ends well."

He started with Heracles. Amy had heard it before, from her grandmother, by the stove in Litochoro, but her grandmother had told it as a story about a jealous wife. Scott told it as a story about clothing.

"His wife sent him a robe," he said. "She'd been told it would keep him faithful. He put it on to make a sacrifice, and it stuck to him, and it burned. It went into his skin like fire into pitch. He tore it off and the flesh came with it. In the end he built his own pyre on Mount Oeta and lay down on it, because dying in the fire was better than living in the thing he wore."

There was the usual quiet after a story like that. Then a laugh came from the floor by the window, loud and sudden, the way a laugh comes in a library.

It was Stavros Petridis, the third-year from the dig crew, sitting with his long legs folded and a litre bottle of water between his feet. His face was flushed from the warm room, though everyone else had only loosened their collars. Amy had heard about him from people on the crew, and this morning she had seen it for herself: he couldn't sweat, and he couldn't feel pain, and he talked about both as if they were the weather.

"Sorry," he said, still laughing. "Sorry. I'd take that over an August afternoon in the trench, that's all. At least he got to burn on purpose."

A few people laughed with him, because it was easier than not. Scott didn't laugh. He looked at Stavros a moment longer than Amy expected, and at the bottle of water between his feet, before he went on.

"Odysseus," he said. "Everyone knows he got home. Fewer people know how he died. He had a son by Circe, Telegonus, who grew up on her island and came looking for his father. He landed on Ithaca, there was a fight on the shore over some cattle, and he killed a man without knowing who it was. His own father. With a spear his mother had given him, tipped with the spine of a stingray."

His hand closed on the black stick across his knees. It was a small movement, but Amy was watching his hands, because she always watched hands. The knuckles stood up white. Then he set the stick against the desk and folded his arms.

"His own son," Scott said again, more quietly. "The story doesn't say whether he knew, at the end. I've always hoped he didn't."

A girl near the desk asked whether the stingray was real or symbolic. Scott said it was both, which was a professor's answer, and Amy smiled to herself. He moved on to Orpheus, who had sailed with Heracles on the Argo, and who was the only one of them who never needed a sword.

"He played his way into the underworld," Scott said. "Past the ferryman and the dog, all the way down to the throne. Music did what no hero's strength could do. And he came out again without what he went for." He spread his hands. "So that's your three, and not one of them got the ending he'd earned. Next week, the gods. They don't end well either, but they take longer."

The students got up off the floor in a scrape of bags and chairs. Stavros unfolded himself last, drank half his bottle in one go and gave Amy a nod on the way out, as if they already knew each other. The door swung shut behind him, and the room was suddenly much bigger.

Amy stayed where she was. She had been waiting all afternoon for the room to empty, and now that it had, she found she didn't want to start.

Behind her the bookcase ran floor to ceiling, and while Scott tidied the papers on his desk she let herself look at it properly. The top shelves held ordinary books, monographs and a long run of bound journals. The lower shelves held notebooks, dozens of them, bound in leather or cheap card, some so old the spines had gone the colour of tea. Each spine had a few words written on it by hand.

It was the hand that held her. In the notebooks the writing ran in one hand through Greek, Latin and Venetian. The darkest spines were labelled in Greek, the next in Latin, and after them a long run in a language it took her a moment to place, until she saw "Venexia" on one spine and knew it. It was the same slant on every one, with the same hooked tail on the long letters.

He copied them out, she told herself. A scholar's habit. Old men made fair copies of their notes, and he had copied an old archive into new covers and labelled them all himself. That was the measured answer, and she took it, and kept looking anyway.

"Miss Doukas," Scott said behind her. "You've been holding that envelope since four o'clock. Either post it or give it to me."

She turned. The envelope was in her good hand, the left, a little creased now. She held it out, and he took it and read the form inside without sitting down.

"Finds recording," he said. "You want it in writing that whatever comes out of Petrakis's ground, you're the one who examines it."

"I want it in writing that I'm on the record as having found it, if I find it," Amy said. "And that it doesn't go to the lab in a crate the next morning with someone else's name on the label."

"Has that happened to you?"

"It happened to my supervisor. Twice." She kept her voice level. "I'm not going to spend this season at Nea Potidaia washing someone else's potsherds."

Scott read the form again. Then he folded it back into the envelope and set it on the desk, under his hand, and didn't pick up a pen.

"I won't sign this," he said.

She felt her face go hot. "Because I'm a graduate student."

"Because you haven't found anything yet. Paper made in advance of a thing is worth exactly what the thing turns out to be." He looked at her prosthetic hand, briefly, the way people did, and then back at her face, which people mostly didn't. "Find it first. Then come to me."

"And by then it's in a crate."

"No." He took a card from his breast pocket, wrote on the back of it and held it out. It was a phone number, in the same slanted hand as the spines behind her. "If you find something in that ground that you can't explain, you ring me before you ring Petrakis. Before you ring anyone. Day or night. I'll see that it stays yours."

She took the card. It was an odd thing to be given in place of a signature, a promise with nothing behind it but a man in a rumpled suit, and she was a person who trusted paper. She turned it over once in her fingers.

"Why would I find something I can't explain?" she said.

Scott picked up the black stick again and leaned his weight on it, and for a moment, in the warm office with the sun going down, he looked a great deal older than his hair.

"Because you watch hands," he said. "Go home, Miss Doukas. You've an early start."
