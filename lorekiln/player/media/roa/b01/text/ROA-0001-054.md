Episode 54: The car park

On Thursday morning, when Amy Doukas came down the road behind the conservation building to the secure lab, Nicholas Vardis was waiting at the steel door.

He stood with both hands folded on his plain dark stick, in a good coat buttoned to the throat, as if he had been there some time and didn't mind. His car waited further up the road with its driver in it. Amy had met him once before, outside the bursar's office in October, when he had bowed to her and spoken to her in a Greek nobody had used for centuries. She knew his name now, and the year he was meant to have been born.

"Miss Doukas," he said, and bowed, a small stiff bow from the neck. "Forgive me. I came to ask you a favour, and I didn't want to ask it by letter."

"The lab isn't mine."

"The dean's office tells me the observer is you." He smiled. "On Tuesdays, when the giant lies on the table. I wonder whether I might sit by the wall and watch. I won't speak. I won't touch anything."

"Watch what?"

"Watch him heal," said Vardis.

She waited for the rest of it. He let her wait. The ring on his finger was heavy and gold, with a stone in it that caught the light off the steel door.

"Why?" she said.

"At my age," he said pleasantly, "one takes an interest in mending." And that was all he would say, though she asked him again, differently, and he answered her differently, and none of the answers was an answer.

He talked instead about the find. He had read everything, he said, every report the university had sent the Foundation, and he spoke of the cave as if he had stood in it, the round chamber and its lintel. Then he said, as an old man says a thing he has said many times, "And so close to the floodlights. The cave was off the north side of the trench, under the second floodlight, wasn't it, where you tied the orange tape to a peg."

Amy said nothing.

She had tied the tape herself, on her first night, to a peg she had knocked into the scrub with a stone. It was in her own notebook. It was in no report she had written or seen, because nobody writes a roll of tape into a report. Petrakis hadn't known which floodlight. She had never told this man anything at all.

"I'll think about Tuesday," she said.

"That is all I ask." He bowed again, and went back up the road to his car without hurrying, and the car went away between the pines.

She stood at the steel door with her badge in her hand and didn't put it to the lock for a long time.

At dusk she came out of the lab with her keys in her hand and cut across the staff car park toward the graduate block.

It was nearly empty, a dozen cars and the security firm's white van under lamps that were just coming on, orange and weak. Halfway across she heard a laugh she knew.

Stavros Petridis was standing at the far end of the car park in the lion's pelt, with the lion's head pulled up over his own as a hood. Two of the security firm's guards stood in front of him. One of them had a strip of tape across his nose, and Amy knew where he had got it. The whole campus had heard about the line of shields on the road on Friday, and the six men the giant had sat down on the verge.

"Go on," Stavros was saying. He held his arms out wide. "Go on, I'm the giant's mate, aren't I? Free go. Both of you."

The one with the taped nose hit him with a baton across the shoulder.

It didn't land. Amy saw it stop on the pelt as if it had hit a wall, and saw Stavros's face under the dead jaws. It flinched, sharp and bright. And then it opened, all of it, in a look she had seen on people at a window when a band starts playing.

He liked it.

"That's it," he said, laughing. "That's it. Again."

They hit him again, both of them. Amy stopped where she stood, between two parked cars, with her keys in her hand.

She didn't decide to. She simply went still, the way she had gone still in the pines under the guard's torch and on the plain in front of the dog, and the second guard turned his head toward the cars and looked straight at her and away again. His eyes went over her and on, as if she were one more parked car. She was eight metres from them under a lamp, and she wasn't there.

She counted. She had timed the pelt with a stopwatch in October while Stavros laughed and let her drop weights on him. She knew its number better than her own phone's. One blow turned, and then forty seconds of nothing.

The third blow landed inside them. It went across his ribs under the fur with a sound like a hand slapping meat, and Stavros didn't flinch at all. The fourth caught him across the brow, where the lion's head had slipped back, and he only laughed and shook his head like a dog coming out of the sea.

Amy moved.

She came out from between the cars at a run and took him by the arm, the left, with her good hand, and pulled. He was heavy and he didn't want to come. She pulled harder, and he came, stumbling, and she got him behind the security van and down the line of cars before the guards had turned round. When they did turn, they looked at the place where he had been, and at each other.

Then their radios went quiet.

It happened all at once, across the whole campus. The guards' radios stopped hissing, and a dog that had been barking somewhere out by the gate stopped in the middle of a bark. The two guards stood a moment in the silence. Then they walked away toward the main gate together without a word, as if somebody had called them.

Stavros leaned on the van and laughed until he had to bend over.

"You're bleeding," Amy said.

He touched his brow and looked at his fingers. The blood ran down past his eye in a line and dropped off his jaw onto the fur. He looked at it with interest, as he would have looked at someone else's.

"So I am," he said. "I didn't notice." He grinned at her. "The first two, though, Amy. Did you see? I felt the first one."

"I saw."

"I don't feel it, the rest of it. It's not the same." He wiped his face on the back of his hand. "Where did you come from? I didn't see you."

"Nobody did," said Amy.

She walked him to the graduate block with her hand on his arm. The campus was still so quiet she could hear her own shoes. At the corner by the library she looked down the road toward the sea, where the old quarter stood, the first stone buildings the university had taken over, with Professor Scott's rooms among them.

The gate under the arch had been locked since the road was split, for a week now, with a chain and a notice from the security office. The chain hung loose. The gate stood open on the dark.

And under the arch, just past it, a car was waiting with its lights off. Amy knew it. In the back seat, in the glow of a phone or a dashboard, she could see an old man sitting very upright, turning the ring on his finger, slowly, round and round.
