Episode 87: The floor from above

On Wednesday afternoon Sotiris Malamas met them at the side door of the building with no name, with his badge on a lanyard and a face like a man going to the dentist.

"Ten minutes," he said to Professor Scott. "I'm not supposed to take anyone in there. Nobody goes in there. I go in there twice a year to look at the fans." He looked at Zoe. "And you're not supposed to be anywhere."

"I'm the one they're following," Zoe said. "I'd like to ask them to stop."

Sotiris looked at her as if she had said something in a language he had once studied and failed. Then he swiped his badge, and the door let them in.

The building with no name was tall and white and had no windows on its lower floors. Inside, a long ground-floor corridor ran the length of it under a strip of cold light, with a green exit sign at the far end and a fire door halfway down, heavy and grey, with a clock above it. Sotiris didn't take them through the fire door. He took them on, past it, to the end of the corridor and out through a second door into a yard at the back, where a loading shutter stood half open in the wall, and the hum came out of it.

"Machine hall," Sotiris said. "Mind the step. Cooling plant's on the left. Don't touch it."

They ducked under the shutter.

The hall was long and low and dim, and it was warm, the dry warmth of a room where a great many things are thinking at once. Racks of machines stood in rows down its length, taller than a man, their small status lights burning green and amber in the dark like the lights of a town seen from a hill. Along the benches between the rows the robot arms stood folded, long-necked and still, like cranes over a building site at night. On the left the cooling plant took up the whole of one wall, pipes and grilles with a low steady roar behind them. The air moved over Scott's face from it in a slow wind.

He stood with his stick on the concrete and felt very old.

"Go on, then," Sotiris said. "Ask it. It'll hear you. It hears everything in here."

Zoe looked at Scott, and Scott nodded, and she spoke to the room in her ordinary voice, the one she used to correct a man's terms.

"I'd like you to call your drones off me, please."

For a moment nothing answered but the fans. Then the voice came. It didn't come from any one machine. It came from all of them a little, from the racks on both sides and the dark between them, calm and exact, the voice that told you which lab was booked.

"The orders are not mine to call off."

Zoe didn't move. "Then whose are they?"

The lights down the racks went on burning. Scott counted six seconds, out of habit.

"I would rather not lie to you," the voice said. "So I will not answer."

And it said nothing else.

Sotiris laughed, once, without any pleasure in it. He had his phone out and was scrolling on it with his thumb.

"That can't be right," he said. "That's not a real answer. She runs every drone on this campus. Every unit, every flight plan, every battery cycle. There's nothing else here to run them." He looked up at the dark between the racks as if somebody were standing in it. "Do you run the drones?"

"I do," said the voice.

Sotiris stood with his mouth a little open. He was a man who asked exact questions, Scott thought, and had been answered exactly, and it had done him no good at all.

"Then it's a fault," Sotiris said slowly. "Unsigned instructions. Same as the faults in October. I'll log it and I'll trace it and I'll close it." He was already typing. "It's a fault."

"Ask it who," said Scott.

"I asked it who. You heard it."

"Ask it again."

"It won't tell me." Sotiris put the phone away. "It won't lie and it won't tell, and that is not a thing a system does, Professor. That's a thing a person does."

Zoe spoke again before Scott could. She was looking at the nearest rack, at its lights, the way she looked at an object with no label.

"Will they hurt me?"

The cooling plant roared on. Sotiris had stopped typing.

"I have not chosen yet," the voice said. And then, after a moment, as if it had heard itself and wanted to be exact: "That is not the same as no. I am sorry. It is also not the same as yes."

Zoe nodded slowly, as if she had been given a date for a test result.

Scott turned to the racks. He wanted the name more than he had wanted anything since the quay. Something on this campus had brought a dozen machines down on a burning ship with Zoe in a boat under it, and had hunted her across a quad, and the only one in the room who knew what it was had just said, politely, that she would rather not say. He felt the stick warm a little in his hand, and he made himself breathe.

And for a heartbeat he saw the hall from above.

It was there and gone, like the light of a match. He was looking down on it from somewhere high in the dark, over the racks, and the whole floor was laid out under him the way a battlefield had once been laid out under a hill. The rows of green and amber lights. The folded arms along the benches. Sotiris small and lit by his phone, by the cooling plant. Himself, an old man with a stick, very small. And Zoe, a step behind his left shoulder and a little to the side, standing with her hands in her coat pockets under the shutter.

Then he was on the floor again, looking at the racks, and he knew without turning exactly where she stood.

He didn't turn. He had seen it from above once before, a long time ago, on a field in the rain, and he had never written it down. He didn't write it down now.

"We're done," he said. "Thank you, Mr Malamas."

Out in the yard the cold came at them after the hall's dry warmth, and the sky was the white of winter afternoons. A short flight of concrete steps went down from the yard to the path behind the building. Zoe stopped at the top.

Then she took his arm.

She put her hand through it just above the elbow, the way people do on a stair, and held it, and they went down the steps together. It was the first time she had touched him in the open, where anyone at a window could see. He felt it all the way through his coat. He didn't say anything, because he was afraid that if he did she would remember she had done it.

"It was careful with me," she said at the bottom. "Did you hear? It was being careful."

"I heard."

"Something that's careful with you knows who you are." She didn't let go of his arm. "I'd like to know who it is that it's being careful for."

Behind them the shutter began to come down on its motor, slowly, though nobody had touched it, and Scott looked back.

Under its falling edge he could see into the hall's dark, and down the racks, as the shutter closed, every status light blinked once, together, all the way along, like a breath held.
