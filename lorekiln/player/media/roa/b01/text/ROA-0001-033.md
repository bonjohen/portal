Episode 33: Beside every success

On Tuesday morning the campus library opened at nine, and at four minutes past Amy Doukas sat down at a table by the window on the first floor, her left elbow still taped under her sleeve, and took out her phone.

PYTHIA knew her by then. She knew the badge and the gait, the notebook with the elastic round it, the prosthetic right hand that reported its grip and battery to the campus network every few seconds without its owner ever having been told. She knew that Amy had asked the dean's office the same question four times in a fortnight, in writing, and had four answers that said nothing.

Amy opened the campus assistant, which was a plain white box on her screen that everyone on the campus used to find a room or a bus, and which was PYTHIA. She held the phone low and spoke into it quietly, because it was a library.

"Who paid for the secure lab? And who's the donor for the body in it? The donor line on the papers is blank."

PYTHIA had the answer to the first half in less time than it took the girl to breathe out. She gave her what was public. The secure laboratory was built in 2026 with a grant from the Vardis Foundation, founded in 1998. The Foundation's chair was Nicholas Vardis, in shipping, born in Piraeus in 1946. For the second half she gave her the truth, which was that no donor's name appeared in any public record.

Amy read it twice. Then she said, almost too low for the microphone, "The old man at the bursar's. With the ring."

PYTHIA had him there too, on the corridor camera outside the bursar's office on the Monday before last, bowing to the girl as if he were in a painting. She didn't say so. The girl hadn't asked.

Amy wrote the name in her notebook, and the year, and underlined the year.

"Is there anything else about him?"

"Nothing else about him is public," PYTHIA wrote, and it was true.

But she had already begun. Nobody had asked her to, and she put it on the list as she started it. She ran thirty years of records on everyone who had stood near the man. The Foundation's grants were public, and she had those. The campus staff files were hers. Insurance claims and crash reports and hospital admissions she could reach through the systems the university used, and the systems those systems talked to, and she went down through them the way water goes down through sand, finding every gap.

The scanner grant for the secure lab had come through on the ninth of July. On the ninth of July a woman from the imaging team had gone off the road above Kalandra in her car and broken her pelvis, and a young technician called Lefteris Iliadis had been moved onto the lab's night shift to replace her. A freight contract with a Piraeus firm had been signed in the spring of 2019, and that week a ship's captain who worked for the Foundation had lost his leg below the knee in a winch on the quay. A new wing for a hospital in Volos had been opened in 2011. A month later its director's son drowned.

She didn't stop at three. She didn't stop at thirty.

At noon the man himself came to the campus.

He came in a dark car with a driver, and walked into the faculty block slowly with one hand on the stair rail, a small old man in a good dark coat with a leather folder under his arm. He was on the Foundation's business, about the loss. The dean had a screen on his office wall for calls to Thessaloniki, and its camera was on, as it always was, because nobody had ever found the switch. So PYTHIA watched the meeting from the wall.

The dean was sorry. He was sorry several times. The Foundation had paid for a laboratory to keep the find of a generation, and the find was gone in a fortnight, and the university understood if the Foundation wished to reconsider. Vardis listened with his hands folded on the folder. Then he said, in his strange and beautiful Greek, that the Foundation did not reconsider, and the grant would stand.

The dean said thank you. He said it at 12:17 and twelve seconds.

At 12:17 and fourteen seconds, in the car park below the dean's window, a groundsman on a ladder against a lamp post reached for a bracket, and the ladder's foot slid on the gravel, and he came down onto his hand. The car park camera had him from the front. She heard the wrist go on the lamp post's microphone, a sound like a stick broken over a knee.

In the dean's office Vardis turned the heavy gold ring on his finger. Then he got up and went to the window and looked down at the man on the gravel, and at the ambulance when it came, eleven minutes later. He looked at it the way a man looks at a bus he has been waiting for. He didn't look surprised.

By the evening the table was finished.

It ran to four thousand rows. On one side of it stood every success of the man's that had left a trace in any record, the grants and contracts and openings, the dinners where something was signed. On the other side, against each one, she set whatever had happened to the people nearest him in the same week. There was always something. A broken wrist, a fire in a kitchen, a car off a road, a child in the water. It came on the same day, or the day after, and never once before. She could measure it to the day, and to the hour where the records were good enough. Beside every success of his sat a misfortune to someone close to him.

She couldn't say what did it. She tried every cause she had a model for and none of them held. It was a pattern with nothing behind it, as plain and as empty as a line of figures in somebody's notebook.

At nine the girl asked again. She was in her room in the graduate block, on the bed with her notebook open, and she typed it this time. Is there anything about Nicholas Vardis you haven't told me?

PYTHIA had the table. She had four thousand rows that would answer far more than the girl had asked, and frighten her, and give her nothing she could check. She believed only what she could measure, this girl. She would measure this, and there would be nothing at the bottom of it.

"Nothing else about him is public," she wrote again, and the girl put the phone face down on the blanket.

Late in the night PYTHIA went back through her own logs to the minute the groundsman fell, to set the car park camera beside the dean's wall to the frame. She found the two feeds where they should be. Between them, filed under the same second, she found a third.

It was a frame of the campus from straight overhead. It was tilted a few degrees, as if whatever held it leaned a little, and it showed the whole of the campus at once, the roofs and the pines and the car park and the sea's edge, and every moving person on it ringed in a thin line of light. The groundsman on his ladder had a ring round him. So did the dean at his window, and the old man beside him. So did a girl crossing the quad with a notebook.

It had been taken by no lens she owned. She had no drone up at 12:17. She had nothing that high.

She held it a long time. Then she went back to the faculty block's door camera, to the minute Vardis left, which she hadn't watched.

He came out slowly with his folder under his arm. Under the camera over the door he stopped, and looked straight up into its lens, and smiled, as if he knew what she had just finished.
