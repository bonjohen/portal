Episode 53: The fingernail

On Tuesday afternoon the giant came up from the theatre.

Amy Doukas saw him from the window of the library, walking up the campus road from the gate beside Professor Scott, the professor small and grey with his stick, the giant bent forward a little as if he walked into a wind. He had been three days in the seats without a word. People on the paths stopped to watch him pass, and nobody ran this time.

By three the dean's office had what it wanted. Scott told her so on the library steps, out of breath. The university would give the giant the faculty hall, its roof and a heater, for as long as he chose to sleep there. In return he would lie on the long table in the secure lab once a week, on Tuesdays, and let the scanners look at him.

"He agreed?"

"He said a roof is a roof," Scott said. "And that he has lain on that table before." He took a card out of his breast pocket and held it out to her. It had a blue stripe and her photograph on it. "The dean's office sends this back with its compliments. You're the only person who has ever measured him. They'd like the measuring to go on."

Nobody in the dean's office had said the word theft again. Amy turned the card over, and put it on its clip, and went down to the lab.

The steel door read her badge and opened. The corridor was lit blue as it always had been, with the long pane of glass looking into the lab, and on the other side of the glass the giant lay on the long metal table where he had woken, too long for it, his bare feet standing off the end into the air. The gantry of scanners hung over him, and the robot arms were folded back against it like the legs of a resting insect.

Lefteris Iliadis was at the console. He had come in early, off the night rota, because nobody on the day staff would go in.

"Okay," he said when he saw her. "Okay. Amy. He's real, he's back, and he's on my table. I want that in your notebook. I'm the one who told the bones to wake up, remember." He laughed at nothing, too loudly. "He's fine. I'm fine. Do you want a coffee? I've had four."

"Just the numbers," Amy said.

She went in. The lab smelled as it always had of cold metal and dust, and under it now of something like rain on hot ground. She stood by the table where she had sat all those nights on her stool beside the clay. He had his eyes shut.

She asked him in the old tongue, slowly, whether she might measure him. He said yes without opening them.

So she did. The scanner gave her his length and his weight, and she took her own as well with the tape, from the crown of his head to his heel. He was heavier than he had been on the plain by more than a grown man weighs, though he had eaten nothing in three days. The pale seams along his long arms, where the bones had drawn together on this table under the clay, were fainter than they had been, grey lines like the mends in old pottery. She asked him how he felt. He said heavier. She asked him if he slept in the seats. He said no.

"Where did you sit?" she said. "In the theatre."

"In the third row," the giant said, with his eyes shut. "Where she told me to."

Amy wrote it down. Behind the glass Lefteris made a joke to the microphone about the rental on the scanner, and she didn't hear the end of it.

It was near five when she ran the tape across his left hand for the span. He had laid it flat on the steel beside him. As the tape went over it the nail of his smallest finger lifted at one edge, and came away from the finger altogether, and lay on the table.

It was the size of a playing card. It was grey and ridged and very old, like a shell picked up on a beach, and the bed of the finger under it was already pink and new. The giant didn't open his eyes.

"That happens," he said. "It is old. Leave it."

Amy didn't leave it. She lifted it with tweezers into a sample bag, and sealed the bag, and wrote the date and the time on the label, and set it on the steel bench by the wall to log in the morning. It was the first piece of him she had ever been allowed to keep.

At seven the giant got up off the table and went back to his hall, bending under the door, and Lefteris let out a breath that seemed to go on for a long time.

On Wednesday morning Amy came in at eight to log the nail and found the bag on the floor.

It lay a metre from the bench, on the grey floor between the bench and the steel door. The seal was open at one corner, and the nail had come half out of it, and it lay with its narrow end pointing at the door, as straight as the needle of a compass. Beyond the door, she knew, under the step, ran a strip of bare soil along the wall of the building where nothing grew.

Amy stood quite still in the doorway with her hand on the frame.

"It fell," Lefteris said behind her. He had stayed on for the night shift, and his eyes were red. "Something knocked it. Hang on." He went to the console and ran the bench camera back through the night while she stood there, faster and faster. Then he ran it again, slowly.

"Nothing," he said. "It's on the bench at midnight. It's on the bench at four. Then it's on the floor." He rubbed his face with both hands. "There's no frame where it goes. The camera's playing up again. It does that."

Amy found the giant's shed fingernail on the floor a metre from the bench, pointing toward the door and the soil beyond it, and the camera had seen nothing move it.

She didn't say anything. She picked it up with the tweezers and weighed it on the bench scale, and photographed it from above with a scale bar beside it, and from the side. Then she put it back in a new bag and sealed the bag twice. Then, because she was careful, and because she couldn't think of anything else a careful person would do, she set the bag in the middle of the bench and turned a glass specimen jar upside down over it, and wrote the time on a strip of tape and stuck the tape across the foot of the jar and onto the steel.

"That's a bit much," Lefteris said.

"Probably," said Amy.

She spent the morning on the giant's numbers from the day before, his length and his weight, setting them against Saturday's and the plain's in a table of her own. Every figure had grown. It was a good table. It didn't explain anything.

At noon she went back to the bench to log the nail again.

The jar stood where she had put it. The tape across its foot was unbroken, and her own handwriting on it said 08:47. She lifted the jar.

There was nothing under it. The bag lay on the bench a hand's width nearer the door, outside the jar, with its narrow end pointing at the steel door, and the jar hadn't moved.
