Episode 48: One band at a time

On Friday afternoon the dean's patience gave out, and six men with shields came up the road behind the conservation building to walk the giant to the fence.

Alcyoneus went out to meet them laughing.

"Stay with her," he told the professor at the door, in the old tongue, and the professor looked at the conservator at her bench and at the men on the road, and stayed. Alcyoneus ducked out under the lintel into the low sun and straightened up to his full height on the asphalt, and the six men stopped walking.

They stood in a line across the road with their clear shields up in front of them, and the one in the middle called out something in the mortals' speech, loud and slow, the way men speak to a horse that might kick. Alcyoneus didn't understand a word of it. He understood the shields. He had seen lines like that on the plain, though the shields then had been bronze and the men behind them had been better at it.

He walked toward them.

They came at him all together, as they had been taught, shields first, to push him back the way they had come. He let them reach him. Then he put his hands on the top edges of the two middle shields and leaned on them, only leaned, and the two men behind them went down on their backs on the road. He took the next shield away from its man and threw it into the pines. He did not reach for the blow. He did not need it for six men.

In a little while the six were sitting on the verge, or lying on it, holding their arms and their knees. Nobody was dead. One of them had a bleeding nose and was swearing about it in a high voice.

Alcyoneus stood in the road and waited for the next.

It was a long time coming. The six got up slowly and limped back to their van, and none of them came at him again, and nobody else came either. It was as if the road were holding its breath. He stood there and watched the shadows of the pines get longer across the asphalt, and he understood that they were waiting for him to be ready. He laughed at that.

When the next band came it was police, two cars of them from Kassandra with blue lights turning on their roofs, and a man among them carrying a thick black gun with a fat round mouth. Alcyoneus reached into his shoulders for the blow, to split their cars in front of them and send them home, and it came, and he struck the ground beside the first car, and the asphalt cracked across in a long line and the car's front wheel dropped into it.

Then the gun went off with a hollow cough, and a net came open in the air and fell over him.

It was heavy and it was weighted at the edges, and it wrapped his arms against his sides. He reached for the blow again to tear it, and his arms were only arms. He went down on one knee on the road with the net round him, and pulled at it with his thin strength, and the strands held.

The police didn't come any closer. They stood behind their open doors and waited.

So he waited too, kneeling in the net, while the weight went out of his shoulders and slowly, slowly came back. When it was there he stood up, and the net came apart round him like wet bread, and he dropped the pieces on the road. The police got back into their cars without a word and reversed down the road with one wheel scraping.

Again nothing came. Again the road waited for him.

It was dusk when the last band came, and he knew before they reached him that they would be the worst. There were more of them, twelve or fourteen, in black, with batons and the long shields both. They had a van with a bar across its front. And they had a man to lead them who was heavier than the others and angrier, a big man with a shaved head and a thick neck who came up the road at a run, shouting, and didn't stop.

He didn't stop when the others did. He came straight at the giant with his baton raised and hit him across the knee with it, hard, and then again. Alcyoneus caught the baton and took it off him, and the man hit him with his fist instead, in the ribs, which hurt the man more than the giant, and he kept hitting.

The others came in behind him. Alcyoneus went down under them, under the shields and the weight of them, on his back on the cracked road with the stars coming out above the pines.

They held him down. Then, for no reason he could see, they waited.

He lay there and looked at the sky and noticed something about himself. All afternoon he could have killed them. He could have killed the six with his hands and the police with the blow and every one of these, and on the plain he would have done it without thinking, and laughed. He didn't want to. He didn't know why. It sat in his chest like a stone he had swallowed.

The weight came back into his shoulders.

He got up through them. Men fell away from him on every side. The heavy man came at him again with nothing in his hands, roaring, and Alcyoneus struck, not at him but at the road a stride in front of his boots, and the asphalt split from verge to verge with a crack that shook the windows of the conservation building and threw the man flat on his back.

The band broke. They ran for their van, dragging the heavy man with them by the arms, and the van backed away down the road with its lights wheeling over the pines.

Only then did the professor come out.

He came down from the door with his stick and stood in the road between the giant and the place where the heavy man had fallen, as if there were still someone there to protect. He was breathing hard. He held the black stick in both hands across his body.

Alcyoneus looked at it. He had seen it every day for a week and never looked at it properly. Now, in the light of the one lamp on the conservation building, he did.

"Put that down," he said quietly.

"I can't."

"No. You can't." Alcyoneus looked at the dark shaft and at the small hands round it. "That weapon was made to wound the one who carries it. Through someone else."

The professor went white.

"How do you know that?" he said. "Who told you that?"

"Nobody told me. I saw weapons like it, in the hands of men the gods disliked." He shrugged his long shoulders. "They carried them a long time, and they looked tired."

The professor didn't ask him what he meant. He stood a long time in the road looking down at the stick in his hands, and didn't put it down.

Later, in the dark, with the vans gone and the road split open between them like a broken plate, he spoke again.

"They'll try paper next," he said. "The university. They'll send someone with a folder." He looked up at the giant. "Paper has beaten giants before, you know. It's beaten bigger things than you."

Alcyoneus laughed.

Then he thought of the canal at Nea Potidaia, a strip of water a child could swim across, and of how long it had kept him off his own soil, and he stopped.
