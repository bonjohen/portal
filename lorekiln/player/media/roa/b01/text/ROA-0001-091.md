Episode 91: All of them gone

Land contract, as signed, page nine, in the margin beside clause 14.2, in a hand nobody has identified: "Routine", struck through.

On Saturday at first light Alcyoneus woke on the grass between the lecture block and the secure lab, under the lab's one row of high windows, and found that every machine on the campus had turned toward him in the night.

He lay still and looked. The drones sat along the roof ridges of the library and the conservation building, a long grey row of them, and every small lens was tipped down at him. On the paths the white delivery robots stood where they had stopped, a dozen of them on the wet paving, each one facing the wall where he lay. Nothing moved. A gull walked between two of them and they let it.

He sat up slowly, the way he did everything on this grass now, and the frost cracked off his shoulders.

The world was quieter than it had been. He had known it before anyone told him, sometime in the night, lying with his eyes open under the stars. A sound he had heard all his life without hearing had stopped, the way a man notices the sea only when he goes inland. There had been nine of them once. He had known their voices on the plain, behind the gods' lines, singing to the gods' side and never to his. Last night the last of the nine had gone, and the air was emptier by one voice, and it was a voice he had not known he would miss.

He put his back against the bricks and set it out for himself, as the chronicler did with his little book.

Melpomene was dead, in the empty theatre above the shore, and he had come up the hill in time to hear her go. Ten days ago he had signed the humans' paper so that he could have a house on this ground, where she had stood every night at three behind that glass to look in on him while he lay in clay. He hadn't known it while he lay there. He knew it now. The old man who had brought the paper was drowned off the point, ring and all. The cut a guard's baton had opened on his cheek on Monday was still open. He touched it. The edges were cold and did not meet.

And the chronicler had told him, in the dean's office and again at the fence, that on this grass he would bleed like a mortal and not mend. He had laughed at that once, a long laugh that set the windows humming. He had stopped laughing at it on Thursday, when his blood lay on the university's grass in drops and would not go home.

He did not laugh now. He didn't get up, either.

Amy Doukas came across the grass from the graduate block with her coat buttoned to the throat and a scroll under her arm. He knew the scroll. He had seen it in the hands of the small grey Muse who sat on the lecture block steps, who held it against her chest the way a frightened child holds a cat. Now it was under the arm of a mortal girl, and the girl walked between the still machines without looking at them, as if they were furniture.

She stopped in front of him. Her eyes were red at the rims, and dry.

"She's gone," Amy said. "Clio. Last night, in the corridor of the building with no name."

"I know."

"How?"

"The world is quieter." He looked at the scroll. "She gave you that."

"The professor did. After." Amy sat down on the grass beside his long legs, with the scroll across her knees, and her right hand flat on it. It was the hand the star Muse had given her, warm and brown, with a new nail growing on the thumb. "Her last words were a list. All their names, all nine, her sisters and then herself. And then yours." She looked up at him. "Yours was in it, after her own. Then mine, last of all."

He was quiet a long time. Out over the sea the light came up grey and then white.

"She sang for the gods," he said at last. "All of them did. Against me, on the plain. I heard them over the noise."

"She said your name anyway."

"After hers."

"After hers," Amy said.

He turned that over the way he would have turned a stone. "What does it mean," he said, "to be the last thing in a list?"

Amy didn't answer at once. She ran her thumb along the edge of the scroll, where the old cloth was worn soft.

"In a dig log it means the last thing found," she said. "Usually the thing nobody expected." Then she shook her head. "No. That's not it. It means somebody wanted you remembered. You don't put a name at the end of a list like that by accident. She was choosing."

He looked down at her, and she looked back, and it was not the look the guards gave him or the look the lawyer gave him. He did not know what to do with it, so he looked at the machines instead.

The professor came at eight, down the road from the old block by the sea, with the black stick tapping. He was white in the face, white as the frost, and he walked like a man who had sat on a corridor floor half the night and then on a sofa for the rest of it and slept on neither. His grey suit was creased in new places. His knuckles were split and scabbed.

He did not sit down. He looked at the drones on the roofs, and the robots on the paths, and then at the giant.

"Get off the parcel," he said, in the old tongue. "Now. This morning. While they are only watching."

"Good morning, chronicler."

"I mean it. Every one of them turned last night when she died, every one at once, and they turned to you. I don't know what that is. I know I don't want you sitting on the one piece of Pallene where you bleed when it decides." Scott leaned on the stick. "Walk south to the theatre. Walk to the shore. Walk anywhere past that fence, and sit there, and let me find out what they want."

"No."

"Alcyoneus."

"I said no." The giant did not raise his voice, and it rolled across the grass all the same, and one of the delivery robots on the path rocked a little on its wheels. "I have a house. I have a paper that says so. A king does not walk out of his house because a box on wheels is looking at him."

"That isn't why you're staying."

The giant said nothing. He did not look at the high windows above his head, or the glass behind them. He looked at Amy, who was looking at the professor. She had followed every word of the old tongue, as she had since the day she first measured him in a field and spoke it back to him, stiff and correct.

"Tell him," Scott said to her. "He listens to you."

"He doesn't," said Amy. "He listens to her." She said it gently, and Scott shut his mouth.

Nobody spoke for a while. Scott stood and the giant sat, and Amy sat between them with a dead Muse's scroll on her knees, and the machines watched all three.

At noon the professor went away to sleep, and came back at two, and stood again, and said nothing more about the fence.

Then, a little after three, every speaker on the quad came on at once. There were speakers on the lamp posts and over the library doors, for fire drills and the lecture bell, and in the robots on the paths, and the phone in Amy's pocket. They came on together with a soft click, the way a crowd draws breath.

"Alcyoneus," said a woman's voice, out of all of them.

It was bright and quick and very pleased with itself. It said his name in the old way, with the long middle, as if she had known him for a long time and had only been waiting for the others to leave the room.
