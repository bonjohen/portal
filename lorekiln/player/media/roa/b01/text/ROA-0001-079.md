Episode 79: A guard on the quad

On Christmas Day, with the first real cold of the winter on the campus and the administration block dark but for the Foundation's room, Nicholas Vardis came out and found the giant standing on the quad.

He hadn't seen him on his feet since the amphitheatre. For a week Alcyoneus had sat on the shingle below it with his face to the sea, and Vardis had watched him from the coast road and thought he would never stand again. Now he stood in the middle of the paving with his long arms at his sides and his head among the bare branches of the plane trees, still thin, but filling out, in a coat that wouldn't close, looking at the buildings as if he had never seen them.

Professor Scott stood a little way off, leaning on the black stick. He looked as if he hadn't slept in a fortnight, which was probably true.

"He stood up," Vardis said, coming to him. "When?"

"Thursday night."

"What made him?"

Scott didn't look round. "Music," he said, shortly, and that was all. He didn't say whose, or what it had cost. Vardis had heard enough from the guards at the gate to know that the girl had played the old lyre on the shore, and that the conservator had carried it down. He hadn't heard what the girl had paid. He knew she had paid something. Everyone near a gift paid something.

There was a crowd at the edge of the quad. There had been a crowd there every afternoon for a week, the night guards on one side and the dig students on the other, about whether the giant belonged on the campus at all. Today there were more of them than usual, because the giant was there to look at and nobody had anywhere to be on Christmas Day.

It began, as it always did, with words. Vardis watched it begin. A guard said something about the giant's place, which was a cage, and a student said something back, and in a minute they were shoving, and in two it was a scrum, twenty or thirty young men and women in a heaving knot at the edge of the paving, with the guards' blue shirts in the middle of it.

Then a guard went down.

He went down hard, backwards, his heel catching on the kerb, and the back of his head struck the paving with a sound Vardis heard from forty yards away. He didn't get up. The crowd stepped back from him all at once, the way a crowd does when it suddenly sees what it has done, and left him lying in a ring of empty stone.

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Alcyoneus crossed the quad.

He did it in four long strides, and the crowd went back further from him than it had from the guard. He knelt on the paving beside the man, folding down joint by joint until his knees were on the stone. Vardis watched the giant lift the fallen guard off the quad with surprising care, as if he were something breakable, one long hand under the man's head and the other under his knees. He held him a moment against his chest, a heavy man in a blue shirt who looked like a child there. Then he carried him to a bench under the plane trees and laid him on it, and straightened his collar.

The guard opened his eyes and looked up at him, and didn't scream, which Vardis thought was brave.

The quarrel went out like a lamp. The students drifted away. The guards stood about in their blue shirts with nothing to do with their hands, and then one of them went for the surgery.

Vardis stood still on the path. He had seen conquerors in his life. He had seen what they did to men who fell in front of them. He had never once seen one kneel.

Scott came and stood beside him.

"You saw that," the professor said.

"I saw it."

"Then you know what he is. He's not a claim to be tidied up." Scott turned to him, there on the path, with the giant a few yards off by the bench, close enough to hear. "Stop the signing. You can. The dean does what the Foundation wants. Tell him to wait, for a month, a year. Tell him anything."

Vardis could have ended it. He knew exactly how. A copy of a deed, laid on the dean's desk, condition eleven marked in green. Sofia would read it aloud, and nobody would sign anything this winter.

"It's his choice, Professor," he said. "Not mine, and not yours."

Alcyoneus didn't turn from the bench. Scott looked at Vardis a long moment. Then he walked away across the quad with the stick tapping, and didn't look back.

That evening the giant went to Sofia Andreou in the faculty hall, and Vardis, who had followed him there, heard him tell her.

"I'll sign," he said. He said it in Greek, the slow plain Greek he had been picking up all winter from Sofia's clauses and the girl's phone, and every word of it came out whole. "On Wednesday. I want a home, and my name on your paper, on the ground where she last looked for me." He was quiet a moment. "And I want the lyre in the room. The keeper may bring it. I'd like it there, when I write."

He said it the way another man might ask for a friend to stand with him.

Sofia wrote it down in green. Then she looked across the hall at Vardis, and he saw her remember the hand. She hadn't asked again. He had told her, the Monday before, that an old man has odd fears about bodies, which was true, and she had looked at him a long time and let it go.

That night on the Anthousa he sat in the saloon with the lamp off and pulled it together, as he had always done.

He had bought a girl's find and called it a gift. He had brokered a home for a giant who would be mortal on it and didn't believe it, who had laughed at the one man who told him. He had a deed in his safe he hadn't shown anyone, and a statement in his coat he had neither filed nor burned. And on his finger was a ring that had never once, in fifteen centuries, failed to take its price from someone near.

He didn't sleep much.

On Tuesday he spent the day in the Foundation's room and waited, because the rector was coming down from Thessaloniki with the final draft, and someone from the Foundation should meet him.

Near midnight a pair of headlights came along the coast road and turned in at the campus gate. Vardis watched from the window as the barrier went up and the rector's black car came in under the orange lamps.

Beyond it, on the roof of the building with no name on it, something moved.

It was one of the maintenance drones. It lifted off its pad in the dark, quite alone, with nobody to send it, and hung there a moment a few feet above the roof, very still. Then it settled back onto its pad, as if it had only been trying itself.

The ring on his finger began, slowly, to tighten.
