Episode 61: Three times a day

Zoe Lambrinou's conservation log, the old lyre, 14 October 2028, a line added later in another ink: "An hour unaccounted for. Check the clock."

On Tuesday at eight Zoe Lambrinou unlocked the conservation store, switched on the lamps over the benches and read the night's numbers off the climate cabinet before she took her coat off. She had done it in that order for nineteen years. She always read the file first, and the cabinet was a kind of file.

The old lyre lay inside it on its bed of grey foam, as it had since November. Its catalogue card was eleven words long and in her own handwriting, and she still couldn't remember writing it. In October it had hummed when a student's hand passed over it. Three weeks ago it had breathed under a giant's palm. She had written both down in her log, and under them, honestly, that she didn't know what she had heard.

At eight Professor Scott put his head round the door with a question about humidity.

He stood in the doorway in his rumpled grey suit, leaning on the black stick, and asked what the cabinet was holding at. She told him. He thanked her and didn't write it down. He had asked her the same thing most mornings since the lyre came, and she had stopped asking herself why, or told herself she had.

"Amy will come in this morning," he said. "Be kind to her."

"I'm kind to everyone."

"Be kind the way you are to the old things," he said, and went.

Amy Doukas came in at half past eight with her hair still wet from the shower. She shut the door behind her and stood by the bench without taking her coat off, and held out her right hand.

Zoe had seen that hand a dozen times since October. It had been grey and smooth, carbon fibre over wire, with a small light at the wrist that blinked when the battery ran low, and Amy had used it with the frown of someone threading a needle.

This hand was flesh. It had a white fleck in the thumbnail and a small scar at the base of the ring finger, and the knuckles were pink from the cold walk across the quad. Amy held it out palm up and then turned it over, the way she would have held out a find, and waited.

Zoe looked at it for a long time. Then she pulled on a blue glove, out of habit, and took the fingers in her own. The warmth came through the nitrile. There was a pulse in the wrist, and she found it without trying.

"Give me the file," she said.

"There isn't one." Amy's voice was quite steady. "A woman did it. On the roof of the archive building, last night, as the sun went down. Her name was Urania. She held it in both hands and the stars came down through her, and when it was done she was gone." She swallowed. "She died doing it. She said she would, and I didn't listen, and she did."

Zoe let go. She peeled off the glove and dropped it in the bin, because it was used, and stood with her own bare hands flat on the bench.

Her whole trade was to believe nothing she hadn't examined. A varnish was what the tests said it was, and the story the owner told came last, never first. Now a student was holding out a miracle and asking her, with her eyes, to treat it as an object. To look at it properly and say what it was.

"It's a hand," Zoe said at last. "A right hand, a young woman's, in good condition. That's as far as I can go without tests."

Amy laughed, a short surprised breath, and her eyes filled. "Thank you."

"For what?"

"For not telling me it can't be. Everyone I've shown it to has told me it can't be. Then they go white." She flexed the fingers and watched them move. "Can I do something? With my hands. Anything. I can't sit in my room with them."

So Zoe gave her work.

She lifted the lyre out of the cabinet on its foam and set it on the bench by the window, and gave Amy a tray of cotton swabs and a beaker of distilled water, and showed her the crossbar. It was polished dark by hands that weren't theirs, and there was a century of grime in the grain where the strings were wound. Amy bent over it. She worked slowly and well, with the warm hand on the swab and the left one steadying the arm, and she didn't touch the strings once.

After a while she began to talk, the way people do over careful work.

"My grandmother would have liked this," she said. "Cleaning something old. She'd have told me who made it."

"Did she know about lyres?"

"She knew stories." Amy turned the swab and took a clean one. "After the accident I went to live with her in Litochoro, the winter I was seventeen. I had one hand and I wouldn't go out. I wouldn't talk to anybody. And every night she put another log in the stove and told me the old stories of the mountain. Where the Muses were born, on the slopes above the village. How the gods fought the giants for the sky." Her voice went soft and slow on the next words, as if she were setting them down on the bench. "I pretended not to listen, all that winter. And I listened to every word."

She worked on in silence for a while. Then she came back to it and told Zoe about the stove, which smoked when the wind came off the mountain, and the old woman's hands, and how she always began in the middle. Each time she reached the end, her voice went soft on the same words. I listened to every word.

Zoe listened too. She didn't write it down. It wasn't hers to write.

Over the next two days she watched Amy pass the lyre's case.

The store's one window looked north across the campus, and the lyre's cabinet stood between the window and the door, so that anyone going from one to the other passed it. On Tuesday afternoon Amy came back with a box of sample bags to sort, and passed the case on her way to the shelves, and slowed, and didn't stop. On Wednesday morning, after Scott had been and gone with his humidity, she came in early to finish the crossbar and passed it twice. At noon she brought sandwiches and passed it again.

She never touched the glass. She never even looked straight at it. She looked at it out of the corner of one eye, like a dog she'd been told bites, and the warm hand stayed in her coat pocket while she passed.

On Wednesday at four Zoe sat down at her desk and opened her log.

It was the best log on the campus, kept to the minute in plain words anyone could follow in fifty years. She wrote the cabinet's numbers. She wrote the crossbar's cleaning, with the swabs and the water. Then she sat with her fingers on the keys.

She wrote the hand down. She wrote it the way she would have written a loan coming in: a young woman's right hand, flesh, warm, a pulse at the wrist, previously a prosthesis, examined by eye on the morning of Tuesday, cause given by the owner. She wrote the cause as the owner had given it, a woman on the archive roof who had died, and she didn't add a question mark, because that wasn't how she kept her log.

She read it over. It was the most ridiculous entry she had ever made, and she couldn't think what else to do with it. She saved it.

At six, when the light had gone outside the window and she was rinsing the beakers, Amy came back for her coat.

She stopped at the case.

She had passed it six times in two days and never stopped. Now she stood in front of the glass with both hands at her sides, and looked straight at the lyre on its foam, at the dark shell and the pale arms and the cheap bright strings, and the warm hand came out of her pocket and hung there.

"An old woman told me something on the roof," she said. "After. The other sister." She didn't turn round. "She said there's something in it that only this hand can play."

Zoe turned off the tap.

"May I?" Amy said.
