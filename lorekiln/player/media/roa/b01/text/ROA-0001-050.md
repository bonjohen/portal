Episode 50: The empty seats

Erato's journal, a page found in a hedge below the city, undated: "M. still goes to the old theatre. She says somebody has to sit in the seats."

On the ninth evening of his hunt, a Saturday, Alcyoneus sat in the bus shelter at Kalandra with his knees up near his chin, because the bench had been built for men half his size. He had woken eight feet of bare bone, and every day since had put a little more weight back on him. In nine days he had learned the buses, which came when the paper on the wall said and never sooner. He had learned money, which mortals gave up for bread more easily than they gave up their lives. He had learned the phone, a flat stone of glass that answered any question put to it, and it had answered every question but his.

He had asked it for her by name. It gave him statues and a picture of a mask that wept. It gave him nothing that breathed.

The professor came along the shore road on foot as the light went, tapping a shiny black walking stick that was rather too long for him. His grey suit was rumpled, his hair slightly wild. He sat down at the far end of the bench as if he caught this bus every night.

"You have followed me since the plain," Alcyoneus said.

"Since before that," said Scott. "Long before."

"Then you know what I am looking for."

Scott looked at the sea for a while. When he spoke it was low and tired, nothing like the voice he used on his students. "I shouldn't tell you. I've told other people's stories all my life, and I've never once reached in and changed how one ended. I don't know what happens if I start."

"Tell me anyway."

"Don't ask the phone where she is. Ask where tragedy was last played for people who wept. Not filmed, not sold. Played, to people in seats, and they wept." He turned the stick slowly in his hands. "There's an old theatre above the shore south of Poseidi. The seats are broken and nobody goes there now. I think she still does."

Alcyoneus was already standing. The roof of the shelter rang where his head struck it.

"Whatever you find there," Scott said, "don't tear it down."

The giant laughed, and the sound rolled out over the dark water like weather coming in.

He ran the coast road south at dusk. The beach towns stood shuttered for the autumn, their café chairs chained in stacks, their signs creaking in the wind off the sea. Pine scrub crowded the verge. A car passed him and swerved, its horn going a long way down the road behind him.

Past Poseidi a track climbed from the road toward the hill, and across it ran a steel bar on two posts, locked. He did not stop to think. He reached into his shoulders for the blow that had split the rock at the edge of the plain, and it came, and the bar went spinning into the scrub with one post still bolted to it. He took the track at a run.

At the top stood a wall of old stone, higher than his head, and in it an iron gate on a chain. Through the bars he saw the dark curve of seats going down toward the sea.

And from somewhere inside a voice was speaking.

He knew it before he knew the words. He had heard it from the hills above the plain, begging him to turn back. He had heard it in the dark of the clay, for longer than mortals had kept records.

At the gate he reached for the blow that splits stone, and it wasn't there yet.

His arms were only arms. He took the bars in both hands and hauled, and the iron bent a finger's width and held. He set a foot on the wall and the old stones slid away under it. His body was still too thin for climbing, and the wall went on into the dark on both sides further than he could see.

So he stood at the gate and waited out the count, as he had waited on the plain, while inside the voice went on.

"What is tragedy without understanding?" she said, to no one. "Who am I, if there is nobody here to share it with?"

He counted. He had never begged a god for anything, and he did not beg now, but his hands shook on the bars. The voice was growing thin, the way a fire's sound grows thin near the end, and the count would not hurry for him.

Then it came, all at once, the weight dropping back into his shoulders. He struck the gatepost and the stone split from top to bottom, and the gate fell in with its chain still on it.

He came over the broken seats three rows at a time. Weeds grew out of every crack, and the stage below was a slab of cracked stone, grey under the first stars. Nobody stood on it. The voice was coming from off centre stage, in the wings, where she had always stood to offer insight to men who would not have known her face.

"You came," she said. It was hardly more than breath. "Then sit, my love. Somebody has to sit in the seats."

He heard her last words from the edge of the stage, and when he reached the wings she was gone.

There was nothing in the weeds where she had stood. There was no print in the dust and no warmth in the stone. He went down on his knees and put his hands flat on the ground, the way he had put them on the earth of Pallene to feel it answer him, and nothing answered.

He did not tear the theatre down. He climbed back to the seats instead, slowly, and sat among the weeds in the third row from the stage, and there he grieved.

No god had ever held him still. Heracles had needed to drive him off his own land to kill him. Now there was no one on the hill but himself, and his grief held him where he sat as surely as any chain, and he did not fight it.

Some time later headlights swept the hill and stopped. Footsteps came up the track, and then a man breathing hard at the broken gate. Alcyoneus didn't turn. He heard the walking stick stop tapping, and knew the professor was standing with it held ready in both hands, looking for the fight he had driven out here to stop.

There was no fight. There was a giant in the third row, and an empty stage.

Scott came down the seats with care, testing each step in the dark. He sat beside him, close enough to touch, and laid the stick across his knees. For a long time neither of them said anything. The sea went on below them.

"Did you hear her?" Alcyoneus asked at last.

"Yes. From the road."

"Then I did not dream it."

"No," said Scott. "You didn't."

Alcyoneus looked down at the wings, where the weeds moved a little in the wind. "You told me true," he said. "You could have sent me north, and I would have gone. I will remember that you didn't."

Scott didn't answer that. After a while he said, "This place used to hold thousands. They came from all along the coast to sit here and weep. Tonight it held one."

"Two," said Alcyoneus.

The professor bowed his head, and they sat on together in the broken seats.

It was Scott who heard it first, and looked up. Then Alcyoneus heard it too, a thin hum, like a wasp trapped behind glass. High above the top row of the theatre a small light hung in the dark, red and steady, not moving with the wind.

"A drone," Scott said quietly. "The campus flies them along the coast."

"What does it do?"

"It watches. It records. Someone sees whatever it sees."

Alcyoneus looked up at the red light for a long time. He thought of the phone that had answered every question but his, and the cameras over the bus shelters, and the glass eyes on the poles along every road he had run in nine days. He had taken them for ornaments. The voice in the hall had told him where she stood beside him on the wall at night, and he had never asked it how it knew. Now he knew. It had been looking out of all of them.

It had seen him break the gate. It had heard her go, and it had watched him weep in the seats, and it was watching still.

The light did not look away. Neither, now, did he.
