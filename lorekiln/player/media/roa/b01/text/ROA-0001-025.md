Episode 25: The cabinet by the sea

On Sunday afternoon a hot wind came up off the gulf, out of season, and Amy Doukas walked across the Pallene campus with Stavros Petridis to show him her find.

He had been out of hospital two days. His rib was strapped under his shirt, and he walked with his easy uneven step, favouring the left knee, and talked the whole way. He had come down on the bus from Kalandra that morning because, he said, he'd been lying on his back for a week and he wanted to see the big fellow before somebody else got bored of him.

Amy let him talk. As they went along the gravel paths between the pines, she counted what had changed since the morning after the theft, the way she counted the steps of a section drawing. The body lay on a table she had asked for and been refused. Her name was nowhere on its papers, and the donor's wasn't either. A letter of love had been read off its clay by a man who wouldn't give his source. Its ribs had moved a millimetre. And beside her walked a boy who ought still to be in bed.

What she hoped for was simple. She wanted her name on the find of the century, in print, where nobody could take it off. What she feared was simpler still: a donor's van backed up to the steel door one morning, and the long table empty.

Halfway across the quad Stavros stopped talking.

She looked round. His face had gone the dry red of a roof tile, all at once, and he was standing quite still in the hot wind with his mouth a little open. He took the thermometer out of his shirt pocket and put it in his ear and read it, and read it again.

"Thirty-nine six," he said. He laughed, but the laugh came late. "Climbing. That's a nuisance."

"Sit down. In the shade."

"There isn't any shade." There wasn't. The quad was open gravel, and the wind came across it like the air from an oven door.

Then Amy heard the stick. Professor Scott came along the path from the faculty rooms with his grey suit flapping, and he took one look at Stavros and didn't ask a single question.

"My rooms are cool," he said. "Come with me. Both of you."

He walked them out past the library to an old block near the sea, older than anything else on the campus, with thick stone walls and deep windows, and up one flight of stairs. Inside, the rooms were dim and cool as a cellar. Stavros sat on a hard chair by the window with his eyes shut. Scott filled a glass at the tap and gave it to him, and put a wet cloth on the back of his neck with his own hands.

Amy stood by the wall, because she didn't know where else to stand.

There was a framed photograph on the wall beside her, black and white, of perhaps forty people in rows on some stone steps. A typed card under it said, Department of Classics, 1968. She looked along the rows, because she always read a photograph all the way across. In the back row, at the end, stood a man with Professor Scott's face. He had a long black stick in his hand, and he was leaning on it exactly the way Scott leaned on his.

She looked at it for a long time. It was his father, she told herself. Fathers and sons stood the same way, and a good stick was the kind of thing a family kept. That was the simple answer, and she took it, though she didn't much like it.

Behind her Scott took a key from his waistcoat pocket.

In the corner of the room stood a tall old cabinet of dark wood, taller than he was. He unlocked it, and the door swung open with a smell of cedar and dust, and something else under them, faint and bitter, like pitch that has been near a fire. Inside on a shelf lay something folded, tawny and heavy. He lifted it out in both arms.

It was a lion's pelt. A forepaw hung down out of the folds, the claws still on it, dark and curved. Where the fold had turned back, Amy saw that the inside of the skin was darker than it should be, almost black in a broad band with one straight edge, like the line of a hem, and stiff, as if something had been spilt on it once and dried there long ago.

Scott laid it in Stavros's arms.

"Wear this when the heat comes," he said. "It will keep you cool. And nothing will hurt you, in it."

Stavros opened his eyes and looked down at the fur in his lap. Then he laughed, properly this time, the laugh arriving before the sentence did.

"Professor, nothing hurts me anyway. That's my whole problem."

"I know," Scott said. "Wear it anyway."

Amy opened her mouth. It was an old skin out of a cupboard, and he had promised it would do what no skin could. She could say so now, plainly, the way she would say it about a bad date on a label. She looked at Stavros, who was holding the pelt the way a boy holds a present on his name day, turning a paw over in his fingers and grinning at it.

She closed her mouth again.

"Where did it come from?" she said instead.

"Off a fire, a long time ago," Scott said. "There was a robe on the fire with it. The robe went one way and this went the other, and neither of them burned." He shut the cabinet and locked it and put the key back in his pocket. "That's all I'll tell you today."

By six the wind had dropped and Stavros was down to thirty-eight. He went off to the bus with the pelt rolled under his arm in a carrier bag from the supermarket, waving.

That night Lefteris let Amy sit in the blue corridor of the secure lab on his break. She sat on the floor with her back to the wall opposite the long pane of glass and her notebook on her knees, and wrote nothing, and watched the time go past three.

At ten past three a woman was standing at the glass.

Amy hadn't heard the steel door. She hadn't heard anything. The woman stood very close to the pane, a hand's width from it, with her back half turned, looking in at the long table. She was pale, and thin, and dressed in something dark. Amy had seen that face before, she thought, and couldn't think where.

The woman leaned closer to the glass. Her breath fogged it, a small round patch at head height.

"Alcyoneus," she said.

She said it gently, as if waking a sleeper, the way you'd say a child's name at dawn when you don't want to frighten him. Then she was quiet, and she stood there with her hands at her sides, and didn't go in.

Amy looked at the glass. It held the lit lab, and the long table, and her own face, pale and staring, sitting on the corridor floor. It held nothing else. Where the woman stood, a hand's width from the pane, the glass showed the corridor wall behind her, and nobody in front of it.

Amy shut her eyes and opened them.

When she looked again there was only her own face in the glass, and the fog going from the glass, and she couldn't make herself believe there had ever been another one in it.
