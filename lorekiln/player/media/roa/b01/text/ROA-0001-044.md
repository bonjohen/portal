Episode 44: Nearly as old

On Tuesday morning a black car stopped on the coast road below the terraces, and an old man got out of it alone and began to climb the field.

Alcyoneus watched him from the terrace wall. The man came slowly, with a stick of his own, a plain one of dark wood with a rubber foot, and he stopped every twenty paces to rest with his free hand flat on his chest. He wore a good dark coat buttoned to the throat though the sun was warm. On his hand, when he lifted it to shade his eyes, there was a ring, heavy and gold, with a stone in it that caught the light.

He didn't hurry, and he didn't turn back. When he reached the wall he stood a moment getting his breath, and then he bowed to the giant, a small stiff bow from the neck.

"Forgive me for coming without being asked," he said. "My name is Nicholas Vardis. My foundation paid for the room where you woke. I wanted to see you with my own eyes, before the others do."

He spoke the speech of the mortals now, but not as they spoke it on the buses. Every sentence went round a corner nobody had turned in centuries, the way the speech of the court had gone round corners when Alcyoneus was last alive to hear courts, and he understood more of it than he understood of anyone else's. He listened to the man's voice and not his words.

Then the wind shifted a little, and he smelled him.

Mortals smelled of salt and bread and fear and the oil they put in their machines. This one smelled of all that, and under it of something else, dry and faint, like the inside of a chest that has been shut a long time. It was the smell of age. Alcyoneus knew it the way he knew the smell of rain coming. He had smelled it on the oldest trees on the mountain and on his mother's stones, and never once on anything that walked.

"You are not what your paper says," he said.

The old man smiled politely and waited.

"You are older than any wall on this hill." Alcyoneus leaned down from the wall to look at him closer. "Fifteen centuries. Under other names."

The old man didn't deny it. He didn't say anything at all. He stood leaning on his plain stick, and after a moment he turned the gold ring on his finger, as if it were a thing he did when he was thinking.

"Then we have a great deal to talk about," he said at last. "But not today, I think. Today I came to make you an offer."

He made it well. He had a house, he said, on the coast past Nea Fokea, with a wall round it and a gate that locked, and nobody would come there who wasn't asked. He could have a name made, on paper, the kind that let a man open a door at a bank or a hospital and be let through. He knew doctors who asked no questions and were paid not to. He said it all quietly, without any hurry, the way a man lays out goods on a cloth, and when he had finished he waited.

Alcyoneus laughed.

He laughed until the wall shook under him and a stone came loose and rolled down into the stubble. A house with a wall round it. A name on paper, to show to a door. He had been offered terms once by gods who were afraid of him, and had turned those down too.

The old man waited until he had finished, and nodded as if the laugh were a kind of answer, the one for today.

"I'll leave you in peace," he said. "For now."

He went back down the field as slowly as he had come up it. Alcyoneus watched him go, and saw him reach the car at the bottom, and saw a driver get out and open the door for him.

On the road behind the car a delivery van was backing out of the farmer's gate. It backed too far and too fast, and its rear end went into the farmer's wall with a long grinding crash, and stones went down into the ditch. The farmer came running out of his yard with his arms up, shouting. The old man looked at the van and the wall and the shouting farmer for a moment, through the car window. Then the black car pulled away, smooth as oil, and went off toward the canal.

Alcyoneus sat on the wall a long while after that and didn't think about him at all.

In the afternoon the wind turned.

All day it had come off the sea, salt and warm. Then it dropped, and the olive leaves hung still, and it came again from the other way, down off the hills behind the plain, cooler and slower. It came over the terrace and into his face.

He was on his feet before he knew why.

Pine smoke and wet stone. That was all it was. It was the smell of the hills above the plain on the last morning, when the dew was still on the rocks and the shepherds had their fires lit, and a voice on the slope had begged him to turn back. It was her. It was the smell of her in that place, and it was on the wind now, here.

He ran uphill into it.

He went up through the olives and over the farmer's wire without feeling it, and across a road, and into the pines above. The wind kept coming, and he kept running into it, until the slope went steep and stony under his feet and the trees thinned out to scrub. Then the wind dropped, all at once, as if a door had closed.

He stood on the bare hillside breathing hard. There was only pine and dust and his own sweat, and goats somewhere, a long way off.

He hunted the ridge until dark.

He went along it calling her name into every gully, and the gullies gave him back his own voice, smaller. He found a shepherd's hut of piled stone with a tin roof and a dead fire in it, and he put his hand in the ashes and they were cold. He found a little white chapel on a knoll, with its door locked, and he stood at the door and said her name through the keyhole like a fool. Nothing answered. Nothing in all that hillside breathed but him and the goats.

When the stars were out he came down.

He came back to the terrace wall in the dark with his feet cut and already closing, and sat down where he had slept every night since the plain. His hand went down on the stones beside him and found something that hadn't been there in the morning, a small stone set flat on top of a piece of card.

He lifted the stone and held the card up to the light of the shuttered town.

It was stiff and white, with a name printed on it in the mortals' letters, which he couldn't read. Below the name someone had written a line by hand, in ink. That he could read. The letters were made the old way, each one separate and upright, the way the scribes made them when the court still spoke round corners.

When you want to talk about houses, it said, I am at the quay.
