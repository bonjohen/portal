Episode 30: I do, Melpomene

Inscription in the carved cave, north wall, as read by the survey: "He loved where he should not, and the ground loved him back."

At ten on Friday night the secure lab was the only lit room in its building, and the long metal table lay under the gantry of scanners like something waiting to be served. Amy Doukas sat on a stool beside it with a clipboard on her knee. The room was kept at eighteen degrees.

Stavros Petridis wore a lion.

The pelt hung from his shoulders to the backs of his knees, tawny and heavy, the forepaws knotted across his chest. He sat with his trainers on the console desk.

"I'm cool," he said. "Amy, I'm actually cool. Do you know how often I get to say that?"

"Feet," said Lefteris Iliadis, without looking round, and Stavros took them off the desk.

It was meant to be a two-person shift. Lefteris had come on at eight with a woman from imaging, and by nine she had gone home and Stavros was in her chair. He had told her he would do the night for nothing. He told Amy he would have paid.

"Why?" Amy had asked.

He had shrugged inside the pelt. "Because something in here might hurt."

Now Lefteris tapped the screen and brought up two scans side by side. "Monday," he said, pointing left. "Tonight. The ribs have moved two millimetres since Monday. That's fine, that's settling, clay shrinks. But they've moved four millimetres since eight o'clock."

"So the scanner's drifting," Amy said.

Amy got up and went to the table. The mummy lay on its back as it had lain since the truck, a long shape of grey clay over cloth gone the colour of tea. In the story Scott told, his followers had sealed him like this to carry him home and be born again, and she had written the story down and not believed it. A crack ran along the spine. On Wednesday it had been a hairline. Tonight she could have slid a coin into it.

She took the digital calipers in her left hand and steadied the clipboard with her right. The prosthetic fingers closed slowly, the way they always did, and she waited for them. Then she set the jaws across the crack at the marked point and read the number and wrote it down.

22:00. Spine crack, point A. 3.2 mm.

Numbers were what she had. Nobody had believed her about the truck, or about the smugglers' torches passing over her in the dark. They would have to believe a column of figures.

At eleven it was 4.0. At midnight it was 5.9.

Stavros came and stood beside her and watched her write. He had his left forearm under a fold of the pelt, and she saw the fold move as he pinched the arm hard through it. His face went still for a moment, as if he were listening to a song in the next room. Then he grinned.

"There," he said. "That's one."

"You'll bruise."

"I always bruise. I just never know about it." He pinched again and laughed out loud, alone in the quiet, and Lefteris turned his head and turned it back.

At twenty to one a plate of clay the size of a palm slid off the left hand and broke on the floor. Something smaller went with it. Amy heard it tick on the tiles and roll. She crouched and found it under the table: a sliver of bone, pale and curved, from the smallest finger. She didn't touch it. She tore off a strip of yellow tape and boxed it where it lay, and photographed it, and wrote it down.

00:40. Fragment, bone, approx. 9 mm. Floor, 31 cm from the north leg. Left in situ.

When she stood up, Lefteris had stopped typing.

"I recalibrated twice," he said. "At ten and at twelve. It's not drifting." He looked at the table and not at her. "It's not the machine, is it?"

"No," Amy said.

"Okay, bones," he said, and his voice cracked on it. "If you're going to wake up, here's your chance."

By four the clay was coming away in plates, and they stopped measuring and watched. Under the clay the bones lay brown and shining. Then the shine thickened. Something grey and wet ran along the shins and between the joints of the knees, and the joints drew closer, and the gaps in the ribs began to close like a hand closing. Amy wrote 04:12 and nothing after it.

At half past four the bones had skin. It came up thin and dark over the shins first, like water rising in sand.

Lefteris was on his feet. He had the clear plastic cover of the alarm flipped up and his finger on the red button.

Amy put her hand over it. Her right hand, the one that did not feel, whatever it had done over the lyre that afternoon, flat across the button and his finger both.

"Don't."

"Amy, there's a man growing on the table."

"And who are they going to send? Security? The dean?" She kept her voice low, as if the thing on the table could hear. "I told them a truck crossed the canal with this in the back and they gave me a form. We press that, and by eight o'clock it's in a sealed room and we're the two who were on drugs."

"I'm not on drugs!"

"I know. Nobody will believe either of us." She did not lift her hand. "Not yet. Just watch with me. Please."

He looked at her hand, and slowly he took his finger out from under it and sat down.

When Amy looked back at the floor the yellow box of tape was empty. She walked round the end of the table and bent over the left hand. The smallest finger lay whole, the knuckle smooth under new skin. On her phone's photograph there was a notch, and on the finger there was none.

She wrote nothing at all.

By half past five a giant lay where the mummy had been. He was too long for the table, and his feet stood off the end of it, bare and grey. He was thin as a stick, eight feet of him, with the ribs plain under the skin. His hair lay black on the steel.

He opened his eyes.

They were dark and wet and they looked past all three of them, at nothing, somewhere up among the gantry and the dead fluorescent tube. His lips moved. When the voice came it was low and dry, and it rolled in the room like thunder a long way off.

"I do, Melpomene."

He said it gently, as if answering a question asked a long time ago.

Lefteris made a sound in his throat. Stavros stepped round the console and stood between the table and the other two, his arms out a little, the lion's paws swinging at his chest. He was laughing.

"It's fine," he said. "It's fine, he can't hurt me. Nothing can, in this. Go on."

The giant's eyes closed. His chest rose once, slowly, and fell.

Amy took Lefteris by the arm and walked him out to the corridor. She told him to go home and not to ring anybody yet, and that she would write it all up.

Then she went back in, because the find was hers.

Grey light had come into the high window. On the table the giant breathed, slow and deep, a sleeper's breathing. Stavros stood watching him.

"Six o'clock," he said. "End of shift. My mum's rules. Look at everything at the end of the day." He nodded at the door to the locker room. "Two minutes."

She followed him. On Wednesday she had seen red marks across his shoulders, and he had blamed the strap of his bag, and she had let him.

In the locker room he undid the paws at his chest and let the pelt slide down his back. He pulled his T-shirt off over his head and turned to the mirror.

Across both shoulders, where the pelt had lain heaviest, the skin had risen in blisters. Some were small and clear. Some had run together into one long welt the colour of raw meat, and at the top of his left shoulder one had broken and wept down his back.

Stavros looked at them in the mirror for a long time. He touched one with his fingertip, pressing, the way his mother had taught him to press a swollen knee.

"They don't hurt," he said. "They can't."

He did not laugh. Amy waited for it, and it didn't come.

She left him there with his shirt in his hands and went back into the lab.

The long table was empty, and the door to the alley stood open on the morning.
