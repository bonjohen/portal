Episode 76: The night at the canal

On Sunday evening the administration block was empty, and Nicholas Vardis sat alone in the Foundation's room with the desk lamp on and Sofia Andreou's draft open in front of him, not reading it.

He came in on Sundays because nobody else did. The corridor lights were off. The heating ticked in the pipes. Through the window the campus lay dark under a low sky, the paths lit orange, the building with no name on it standing white among the pines with one row of small lights along its roof.

At ten past seven the printer in the corner woke up.

It made the small cough it always made, and its green light came on, and it drew in a sheet of paper. Vardis looked at it. Nobody had sent it anything. He hadn't touched his computer since Friday. The printer hummed to itself and pushed the page out into its tray, and went quiet again, and its light went off.

He got up with his stick and went and took the page out of the tray.

It was a fuel card statement. One card, one vehicle, the month of October. The vehicle was a white pickup with a canvas back, and its registration was printed at the top. There were three fillings that month, and the last was at the station before the bridge, on the night of the theft, at twenty past ten. The card was paid each month by a company in Thessaloniki with a name that meant nothing. At the bottom of the page, in the same type, the printer had added one line that no fuel company would have printed. The company's only other bill, it said, was a yacht's mooring at Nea Potidaia.

Vardis stood holding it. He read it twice. He knew every line of it before he read it.

He took it back to the desk and sat down and held it over the lamp, near the bulb, where the paper would brown and then burn. He held it there a long time. The page warmed in his fingers. Everything in him wanted to burn it and go home to the Anthousa and go on being what he had been all autumn, a donor, a kind old man with a bad heart who paid for things.

But a machine had printed it. Whatever had found that line could find it again. Burning the page would only mean he was lying to himself, with something watching him do it.

He folded the page and put it in his coat, and went down to his car. In the corridor the lights came on ahead of him, one panel at a time, and went off behind him when he had passed. He told himself it was a sensor. He had paid for the sensors.

He drove himself north. He hadn't driven at night in years. He went up the coast road past Nea Fokea and on through the dark olive country to Nea Potidaia, where the road comes to the canal, and left the car by the harbour wall, and walked down the steps to the stone quay below the bridge on the Pallene side.

The Anthousa had lain here in October. She had tied up on the second day, and a deckhand had broken his wrist on these stones as Vardis stepped ashore. He had paid for the wrist. He had stood here a week, and told the harbour master he was waiting for the weather to turn, and the weather had been fine every day.

The quay was empty now. Black water moved in the canal. Above him the bridge went over to the mainland, and the wind came through its wires and made them hum, one long low note, the note a string makes before anyone plays it.

He stood where he had stood, and he let himself remember it whole.

He had walked the slope north of the canal two summers ago with a stick and a hat, an old man looking at the view. On a strip of scrub above the shore, near a grey outcrop of rock, the ring had gone warm on his finger, as it had never gone warm for a view. He had asked the Foundation's secretary to write to the university for a permit to dig there. He had drawn the trench himself, in red, on the survey map, along the slope to the rock. A year later the university's crew dug it, to the metre, and paid for it with his money, and never asked why.

And when they found what was in the rock, and the university wanted it taken to Thessaloniki sealed, he had found the men.

Vardis paid the smugglers. He paid them through two companies, the one in Thessaloniki and another behind it, so that no name of his touched them. He paid for the white pickup and its fuel. He told them when the guard changed and where the floodlights didn't reach. He told them only to take the find and cross the canal and bring it to the campus on Pallene, to the loading bay behind the secure lab, where a private donor's papers would be waiting for it with the name left blank.

And on the night of the theft he had stood on this quay, at this spot, and watched the truck come over the bridge in the dark, with its canvas back and its lights off. He had turned the ring on his finger as it crossed. His money and its luck had brought the bones home.

He knew now why he had done it. He had always known, and not said it to himself.

In his long life he had found everything a man could buy, except one thing. He had never found a body that comes back. Every body he had ever known went down and stayed down, his friends and the drowned fisherman whose name he wore, and his own was going the same way, one missed beat at a time. Here was a giant who had been killed by a god and come back to sit on a stone. He had wanted to learn how a body comes back, so that his never has to stop.

He thought of the boy, beaten in front of the rifles because he didn't know how to fall. He thought of the girl, keeping still in the dark among the men he had paid, and riding with the find in the back of his truck over this bridge, alone. He had thanked her for her care of it. He had told her yesterday on the coast road that she was brave to get into that truck, as if he had nothing to do with the truck.

For the first time in his life, standing under the humming wires, he called the find what it was. It was his purchase.

He stood there until he was cold through. Then he climbed the steps to the car, slowly, with both hands on the rail.

As he reached it his phone lit up in his pocket.

There was a message on it from no number. It was the campus assistant, the one in every student's phone, which he had never once opened. Its words sat on the screen, calm and exact, and quite polite.

Would you like the statement filed, Mr Vardis?
