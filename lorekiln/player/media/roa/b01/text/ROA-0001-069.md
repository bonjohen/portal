Episode 69: Angry enough

On Wednesday afternoon the dean's staff held the read-through of the giant's final agreement in the faculty hall, and Zoe Lambrinou sat at the end of the last row with the gilt apple's condition report on her knee.

She had brought it to give to the dean's office afterwards, because the police wanted it and nobody would come to the store to fetch it. It was finished. Gilt over a core of some dense dark metal she couldn't name without a lab she didn't have, one small bite near the stalk, condition stable. It said nothing about the warmth. She had locked the apple in her steel drawer on Friday night and hadn't opened the drawer since.

The hall had been cleared of the giant's things. The towers of chairs were unstacked into rows facing the low stage, and on the stage at a trestle table sat Sofia Andreou with her papers and the dean's assistant with a laptop. Vardis sat in the front row in his good coat with his hands on his stick. The giant wasn't there. He hadn't come up from the shore in a week.

Professor Scott came in late, as the reading began, and sat down at the other end of Zoe's row.

He looked terrible. He had sat on the shingle with the giant every night since Friday, and he came in as he must have come up the goat path, in the rumpled grey suit, wet to the knees, with salt drying white on his shoes. His face was grey. He laid the black stick across his knees and didn't look at anyone.

Sofia read slowly, as she always did. Name. Roof. The gold, held in trust. Protection. The lyre, in the conservator's keeping, to be visited whenever he liked. Fourteen point two, routine. Zoe listened to it go by and watched Scott's hands on the stick.

When Sofia stopped for water, the dean's assistant leaned into his microphone.

"Just to say, from the dean's side," he said, pleasantly, "we're all very grateful for the work. It's been a nuisance, frankly, the whole claim. This tidies it up."

Scott stood up.

He didn't do it quickly. He got up out of his chair and stood at the end of the row, and he wasn't looking at the assistant, or at Sofia. He was looking at the front row, at the back of Vardis's head.

"You are buying him a coffin," he said, "and calling it a house."

He said it low. Zoe was ten seats away and barely heard it, and she saw the people in the rows in front turn round, not sure they had heard it either. Scott's voice was shaking. So was the rest of him. He stood with the stick in his right hand, and he was holding it away from his body, out to his side at arm's length, pointed at the empty wall by the doors, away from the rows of people and away from her.

He held it as if it were something that might go off.

Vardis turned in his chair. He looked at the professor for a long moment with his mild old face. He didn't answer.

Sofia said, quite gently, "Professor, if you have a point about the drafting," and Scott sat down.

The reading went on. Zoe didn't hear any more of it.

Afterwards, on the steps of the hall, she found him standing at the top with his collar up, looking south toward the sea he couldn't see from there.

"I'm sorry," he said, before she could speak. "I seem to be saying that to you a great deal."

"You held it away from me," she said. "The stick. You held it out at the wall."

He didn't deny it. He looked down at the stick in his hand, black and shiny and too long for him, and his knuckles were pale on it.

"One day I may be angry enough," Scott said.

She waited for the rest of it. It didn't come. He stood there with his mouth shut and his eyes on the stick, and she understood that whatever happened then was the thing he wouldn't say, and that she wasn't going to make him say it on the steps of a faculty hall.

"He hasn't eaten," Scott said instead. "Not since the theatre. I'll go down again at dusk."

He went off down the steps toward his car.

Vardis came out of the hall a minute later on his driver's arm. Zoe went down the steps past him. She didn't stop, and she didn't look at him.

"The professor isn't usually wrong about people," she said, as she passed.

She walked back to the store with the condition report still under her arm, because she had forgotten to hand it in, and on the way she went over it the way she went over a condition report, one line at a time.

She held an old lyre that hummed for a warm hand and had taken a lunch from the girl who played it.

She held Amy, who believed now, with seven stitches in her scalp and a grandmother she could still talk about, word for word.

There was a giant on the shore who had woken for a Muse and heard her die, and who sat on the stones and wouldn't stand, even for a stone in the mouth. His tooth had come back in the night. She had nowhere to put that.

There was a clause in a document that would make him mortal on one parcel of land if he signed it, and an old man in the front row who knew it.

There was a stolen apple locked in her drawer, and a campus that fought. One of the technicians from the car park had a broken wrist now, and the other had been sent home.

There was a letter in her bag, folded in four, asking for a permit to dig a year before anyone dug.

And there was a professor who sat with a giant every night on a cold beach, and loved someone he hadn't named, and held his stick away from her.

She went in and put the condition report on her desk and didn't write anything in her log at all.

On Thursday at noon the security firm sent two campus guards down the coast road in a car to move the giant on, as it had on Saturday. She heard about it at the canteen. Everybody had heard. It hadn't worked on Saturday either.

At two Amy came into the store.

She had the stitches still under a square of gauze, and her hair pinned back from them, and her warm right hand was in her coat pocket. She didn't sit down. She stood by the door the way she had stood on the first morning, when she had held out the hand like a find.

"They've sent two guards down to move him on," she said. "Scott's gone down to keep an eye on them, and he'll meet us at the top of the path. He hasn't stood up in a week, Zoe." She took the hand out of her pocket and looked at it. "Zoe. Will you bring the lyre to the shore at dusk? Below the theatre. I want to play it for him."

Zoe had said not yet to her, and then perhaps. She had sat up nights reading the file. She had written the clock in, and she had made terms.

"Yes," she said.

She said it before she had read anything at all. She heard it come out, and found she didn't want it back.

She went to the climate cabinet and opened it, and the cold dry air came out over her hands. The grey case sat on its shelf inside. She took hold of it by both handles, as she always did, and lifted it out.

Inside the case, with no hand anywhere near them, the strings hummed once.
