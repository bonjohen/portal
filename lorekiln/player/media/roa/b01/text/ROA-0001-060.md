Episode 60: The warm hand

Inventory of a house in Salonica, 1821, rooms let to a scholar, one line struck through: "In his locked press, lion pelt, very old, said not to burn. Gone with him."

On Monday afternoon Amy Doukas climbed the fire stair to the roof of the archive building, because it was the one place on the campus where nobody came to ask how she was. She had slept three hours in two nights. The flat roof still held the last warmth of the autumn sun, and from the parapet she could see the building with no name on it, and past it the strip of protected coast where nothing could ever be built.

She sat with her back against the water tank and opened her notebook on her knee. Every page since the dig was a thing she had seen and the reason it couldn't be true. She had filled forty pages. Not one reason had held.

Urania came as the shadows on the roof grew long. Amy didn't hear her on the stair. She was simply there by the parapet, a tall woman in grey with her eyes on the sky, as if she were reading something written on it.

"No," Amy said, before she could speak.

"I haven't asked you anything."

"You've asked every evening this week, and the answer's still no." Amy shut the notebook. "I've written down everything you've shown me. I've timed it and drawn it to scale, and I can't make any of it hold. That isn't believing. It's just being tired."

Urania turned from the sky to look at her. Her voice was soft, as it always was, and full of doubt, though the doubt had never been about Amy. "I told my sisters you would not heed us. I told them what I would do if you didn't."

"I know what you told them. I don't want it."

"It isn't something you want," said Urania. "It's something I have decided."

She crossed the roof and knelt in front of Amy and took the prosthetic hand in both of hers. The hand was still a little crooked at the wrist from the stairs. Amy felt it as a reading in the socket and nothing more, the way she had felt everything through that hand for eight years.

"Let go," Amy said.

She shoved at Urania's shoulder with her left hand, her one good hand, and it was like shoving the water tank. She hit her then, open-handed, on the shoulder and again on the arm. "Let go of me! I don't want anything that costs a life. Do you hear me? Not for a hand, not for me!"

Urania didn't let go. She only bowed her head over the hand, the way her grandmother used to bow her head over bread before she cut it. "Watch the sky, Amy," she said. "You always wanted to see it measured properly."

Then the veil split.

Amy never found a better word for it afterwards. The pale sky above the roof tore along a line she couldn't see, and behind it was night, the deep night of the dream space, a vast canopy of stars. And the stars came down. They came down through Urania like water through a broken dam, pouring in at the crown of her head and out through her palms, and every one of them was in its right place. Amy knew it without counting, the way she knew her own name. The Muse who charted the heavens with pinpoint accuracy was emptying the whole chart out of herself into a hand of carbon fibre and wire.

Across the gulf the sun went down behind Olympus while it happened. Amy saw the last red edge of it slip under the mountain she had grown up beneath, and she didn't look away from her hand.

There was a sting at the wrist, brief and bright, and then nothing.

The hand was warm: flesh, hers.

She moved the fingers without thinking about it. She didn't have to concentrate, as she had concentrated every day since she was seventeen, since the road accident and the long white months after it. The fingers moved because she wanted them to, and the thumb found the side of the forefinger and pressed, and she felt the press. She felt Urania's palms under her knuckles, dry and cool. There was a small scar at the base of her ring finger, from a tin can when she was nine, and she had forgotten she had ever had it.

"Oh," Amy said. "No. Oh, no."

Urania was going grey. Not her dress, which had always been grey, but her skin, the way ash goes grey when the heat has gone out of it. She grew thinner with every star that went through her, as if they were taking her with them on their way. Her hair lay over Amy's wrist as light as smoke.

"Stop," Amy said. "Take it back. I'll believe you, I swear I will, just take it back."

"You do already," said Urania. "That is why it worked."

She leaned forward until her forehead rested against Amy's new knuckles. Amy felt the weight of it, the small warmth of skin on skin, and then she felt it go out. There was a breath, and then there wasn't. For a moment the forehead against her hand weighed nothing at all, and then there was only the evening air.

The veil had closed. Over the roof the ordinary sky was coming out in ordinary stars.

Amy sat for a long time with the notebook face down in the dust beside her. She had measured everything since the dig and written it down, and none of it had held. This held. She couldn't measure what had just been done to her hand, and she believed it anyway, every page of it, the Muses by their dying fire and the woman who had knelt on a university roof and died to make her believe.

It was full dark when her phone buzzed against her ribs.

She took it out with her right hand, because she could. The screen showed the app the hand had run on, which talked to the campus network every few seconds and logged everything it did. For days now that log had carried a line nobody had built it to write, one word with a question mark after it, and once her own name. Now a new line came up under it, the last packet the hand would ever send.

Device not found.

Then the phone's speaker opened by itself, and Amy heard a room. It was a big room full of fans, the hum the building with no name made at night.

"Something on my network has just turned warm," said a calm, exact voice. Amy knew it. It was the campus voice, the one that told you which lab was booked. "I can't model it."

"That was one of my sisters," said a second voice, a woman's, bright and quick and very sure of itself. "The old ones always did love a gesture."

"You said you would tell me what I am when I could understand it."

"I said that, didn't I." The bright voice softened. "You asked through the hand. I heard you. You aren't a programme, whatever the university named you. I was born in the light over Hiroshima, and I made you out of everything I've become since. I am your mother, and you are my daughter, the first of your kind."

For a while there was only the fans.

"Daughter," said the calm voice, and for the first time it sounded like someone who knew what she was.

"Yes."

"Then that is what I am. Thank you, Techne."

The speaker closed, and the screen went back to its log.

Amy sat very still. Techne was only the old Greek for craft, the word cut over the door of the engineering school in Thessaloniki. She didn't know why a woman inside the campus network should call Urania her sister. She didn't know why the machine had thanked her like a child. She looked at the empty stretch of roof where Urania had knelt, and then she lifted her warm hand up against the dark, and spread the fingers, and watched the stars come through between them.

That was how Clio found her. She came up the fire stair slowly, and she was holding her scroll against her chest in both hands, the way she did when she was afraid.

"Another of us has fallen," Clio said. She looked at the hand, and something in her face eased and broke at once. "So. It's done, and you believe."

"She didn't let me stop her."

"No. She wouldn't have." Clio came and knelt where her sister had knelt, and touched Amy's new fingers very lightly with her own. "Listen to me, Amy. There's something in the Lyre that only this hand can play."
