Episode 29: The line on the map

It was Friday afternoon before Amy Doukas went to the conservation store to ask Zoe Lambrinou to come and look at the crack.

It was a dull grey day, cool after the hot wind of the weekend, and the store was colder still. Zoe was at the bench in her blue gloves with a box of potsherds and a magnifier, and she didn't look up until Amy had told her the numbers. Nought point four on Wednesday morning. One point six this morning. Widening, and getting faster.

Zoe put the magnifier down.

"That's not drying," she said. "Drying slows down."

"I know."

"Then what is it?"

"I don't know," Amy said. "That's why I'm here."

Zoe looked at her a moment longer, the way she looked at a file. Then she nodded at the shelves. "There's a consolidant on the top shelf behind you, the brown bottle with the green label. Get it down for me. I'll come to the lab first thing tomorrow, at eight, and I'll do it myself. Nobody touches that clay with a brush before I do."

Amy went to the shelves. The brown bottle stood at the back of the top shelf, and the shelf below it, at chest height, was the one with the old lyre on it, alone. She had kept away from it last time without knowing why. She reached up past it for the bottle with her right hand, because her left was holding her notebook.

As the prosthetic passed over the lyre, the lyre hummed.

It was one low note, round and full, and it went on longer than any note had a right to. Nobody had touched a string. Amy stood with her arm up and the note in her ears, and in the hand that had never felt anything, in the grey fingers and the palm she had to watch to make them close, she felt a tingle. It ran from the fingertips into the wrist, faint and quick, like the feeling coming back into a foot that has been asleep.

As if the hand had nerves.

She took it down slowly and looked at it. The note faded. The tingle faded after it, more slowly, and then it was only a hand again, a careful machine at the end of her arm.

Zoe had turned round on her stool. She looked at the lyre on its shelf, and then at Amy's hand, and then at the lyre again.

"It does that," she said briskly. She got up and took the brown bottle down herself and set it on the bench. "It's done it before, on its own. I've heard it from the door, locking up." She paused. "It hasn't done it for anyone else."

"Has anyone else reached past it?"

"Everybody reaches past it. It's on the shelf with the consolidants." Zoe pulled her glove straight. "It hasn't done it for anyone."

Amy stood with her hand at her side. She wanted, very badly, to tell this careful woman everything. The professor in his rooms by the sea, and what he said he was, and the date she had written in the wrong column. Zoe would listen. Zoe would read it all the way through before she said a word, and then she would say something exact. Amy opened her mouth, and thought of Scott holding the door, asking her please.

"Thank you," she said instead. "Eight o'clock."

She was surprised how much it cost her.

At dusk she went down the road between the pines to the secure lab and stood in the blue corridor, at the glass, where the pale woman had stood on Sunday night. She stood exactly there, a hand's width from the pane.

There was the faintest fog on the glass, at head height. It was there, and then she moved her head and it wasn't, and then it was again, so thin she couldn't have photographed it. She put her good hand up near it and felt nothing, no warmth, no breath.

"He isn't awake," she said aloud, to nobody. Her voice sounded strange in the empty corridor. She didn't know who she had said it to.

Through the glass the lab was lit and empty. The long table stood under the gantry with the grey shape on it, and the crack ran down its back like a line drawn in pencil. On the far wall, beside the door to the locker room, hung a frame she had walked past a dozen times without reading. She read it now. It was a copy of the campus deed, two pages behind glass, with the parcel map below them, the campus drawn in a thick black line along the shore. Someone had marked the lab on the map in red. The long table stood inside that line by a few metres.

She sat down on the floor of the corridor with her back to the wall to wait for the night shift, and she opened her notebook at the first page.

She had started it on the morning after the theft, in the graduate block, and she had written only what she could stand behind. Two weeks had gone by. She read it back now, and the whole fortnight was there in a few lines, in her own small hand.

Truck, canal, gate pass. Received 03:10, donor line blank. Letters in the clay, battles and love, read by Prof. Scott, unchecked. Ribs, one millimetre in a week, and more since. Forty seconds, every trial, to the tenth. A professor who says he is two thousand years old. A boy who can feel, in a lion skin. And under that, very small, "red marks, both shoulders, bag?"

She looked at the question mark for a long time. She had let it go on Wednesday. She had let it go because he was cool, and grinning, and because she didn't want it to be anything.

At eight the steel door banged, and Lefteris Iliadis came along the corridor in his hooded jacket with the woman from imaging behind him, carrying her mug. He saw Amy on the floor and grinned.

"The observer," he said. "Observing."

"Point A is at one point six."

"I know. I read your notes." He unlocked the door into the lab. "Coffee's on you tonight."

Soon after eight there were steps on the road again, and Stavros Petridis came down between the pines in the lion's pelt. His face was its ordinary colour. He wasn't red at all, and he was smiling.

"I'm cool," he told them in the corridor, before anyone asked. "Can I sit the night with you? I won't touch anything. I just want to be here."

Lefteris looked at the woman from imaging, and the woman from imaging shrugged.

They went in. Amy took her calipers out of her bag and checked the battery, and wrote the time at the top of a clean page, and waited for ten o'clock.

Just before ten she bent over the long table to set the calipers at point A.

Before the jaws touched it, with nobody's hand on it at all, the crack along the spine ticked, once, a small dry sound like a fingernail on a tooth, and was wider.
