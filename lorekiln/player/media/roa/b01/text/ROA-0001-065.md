Episode 65: What we had for lunch

On Monday afternoon, an hour after the three notes, Amy Doukas stood at the conservation store's sink rinsing the cups for a second pot of tea, and asked Zoe Lambrinou what they'd had for lunch.

She asked it cheerfully, over her shoulder, with the tap running.

"Stavros wants to know if the canteen's still doing those pies," she said. "And I can't remember what we had. Isn't that silly? Was it the pies?"

Zoe was at her desk with the apple's condition report. She looked up.

"Bread," she said. "White cheese. Two pears."

"Really?" Amy turned the tap off and stood with a wet cup in her hand, frowning at it. "Where did we eat?"

"Here. At the bench by the window."

"Did we?" Amy laughed. It was a light, easy laugh, without a trace of worry in it. "I must be more tired than I thought. I've been sleeping like a stone since Sunday, too." She dried the cup and put it on the rack. "Bread and cheese. Fine. I'll tell him no pies."

Zoe watched her. They had eaten together at that bench not two hours before. Amy had turned a pear in her warm hand and told the story of her grandmother's stove, and her voice had gone soft on the same words as on Tuesday. Now she couldn't remember the food, or the window.

Zoe turned back to her screen. She opened the lyre's page in her log, under the two times she had written before lunch was cleared away, 13:24 and 13:25, and wrote the time again. 14:31. Then, in plain words, as she would have written any observation: The student asks what we had for lunch. She can't remember the food or where we ate it. Lunch was at 12:30, at the bench by the window.

She didn't add anything. She didn't test Amy further. She didn't say a word about it.

When Amy had gone back to her samples, Zoe got up and went to the drawer where she kept the lyre's papers, and took out the catalogue card, and laid it on the desk beside the laptop.

Lyre, tortoiseshell and horn, provenance unknown. Strings modern. Do not play.

Eleven words, in her own hand. Three of them, she had once told Amy, were a joke. She read them again now, slowly, and they didn't seem like a joke at all. She thought of the four hours missing from her own log in October, each one spent standing at this bench with her hands on those strings, by the camera's account, and nothing of any of them left in her. She had blamed the clock. She had written check the clock in another ink, as if the clock were the thing at fault.

She thought it through as she would have written a report for the insurers, in short plain lines, because that was the only way she knew how to think about anything that frightened her.

Since Tuesday a student had come into her store with a hand back from the dead. It was flesh and warm and had a pulse, and she had held it.

The lyre answered that hand, as it answered no one else's. It had played for it, three clean notes, and then gone dead, and woken again. And it seemed to take something for the music, the way a lock takes a key and keeps it.

On Friday a lawyer had read a clause aloud in a room full of shouting people, and called it routine, and the man beside Zoe had whispered that it would make a giant mortal on one parcel of land.

There was a stolen apple on her bench that grew warm when nobody touched it. Since it came the whole campus had been shouting, and that afternoon a man she knew had been shoved into a wing mirror over a parking space.

And there was a professor whose hand went white on his stick when people quarrelled near her.

She could see where it went from here. Amy would play more, because the giant needed something and Amy wanted to give it, and every time she played she would lose more. The giant would sign the clause, because nobody but Scott would tell him what it did. The apple would go on warming under her lamp. And one day Scott would be angry, really angry, the fourth time in his life, and she didn't know what he thought would happen then.

She wrote none of that in her log. It wasn't an observation.

At six, when Amy had gone, Scott came to the store door.

He didn't come in. He stood on the step with the dark behind him and the black stick in his hand, as he did most evenings now on his way down to the old quarter, and asked, as he always asked, whether everything was all right.

"No," said Zoe.

She told him at the door. She told it as she had written it, with the times: the terms, the three notes, the strings going dead and waking a minute later, and the cups at the sink, and the lunch Amy couldn't remember. She didn't dress it up, and she didn't leave anything out.

Scott went grey.

She had seen him tired often, but she had never seen this. The colour went out of his face from the forehead down, as if somebody had drawn a blind, and he put his free hand on the door frame.

"Three notes," he said.

"Three. I made her stop at three."

"Thank heaven you did." He shut his eyes a moment. "The old women warned her. Clio did, by their fire, weeks ago. Amy told me afterwards. She'd written it down word for word, the way she does." He opened his eyes again. "Music drawn through that instrument is never free. That's what Clio said to her. It will want something back for every song."

"Want what?"

"Clio wouldn't say."

"Did you know?"

"I knew it would cost something," Scott said. "Everything of theirs does. I didn't know what." He looked past her, into the store, at the grey case on the bench. "I didn't know it would be the small things first."

Zoe stood with her hand on the door and the warm light of the store behind her, and thought about the giant.

She hadn't seen him since the day the men with shields came up the road. Scott said he slept in the faculty hall with his face to the wall, and lay on the long table on Tuesdays without a word, and hardly ate, because something he loved had died. If anything could reach him it might be a sound like the one she had heard at twenty past one, low and clear as cold water. She hoped it could. She hoped it would be what a grieving giant needed, and that someone could give it to him.

A lunch was nothing, a pear and some bread, and people forgot their lunches every day. She told herself so twice. Then she looked at the grey case on the bench, with more than three strings in it, and wished she had never let the girl near it.

"What would the full price be?" she said. "For all of it?"

Scott didn't answer. He said goodnight, gently, and went off down the road toward the sea.

She locked up at seven. The staff car park was dark except for its weak orange lamps, and her car was at the far end of it, and she walked down the row with her keys in her hand.

The two technicians were still there.

They stood by the white van with the broken mirror, where they had stood at half past one, and they were still shouting, hoarse now, five hours on. She slowed. One of them had stepped back from the other, to the open boot of the red car.

When he turned round, he had a tyre iron in his hand.
