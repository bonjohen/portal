Episode 90: The spear not thrown

From Clio's tapestry, the last panel: one thread knotted at the edge, and not cut.

The machine hall stood at the back of the building with no name on it, and on that Friday evening in winter, an hour after dark, its power was off. Professor Scott found the shutter down and the lock fused. He stood in the cold with the black stick in his hand and made himself empty, the way he had learned to long before there were locks, and threw it at the lock with nothing in the throw but aim. The shutter folded in. The stick had gone on into the dark beyond the shutter, and he ducked under the bent steel without it, counting.

Inside it was dark apart from the machines. Their status lights burned green and amber down the racks, and the robot arms stood folded over the long benches like herons asleep. Between the racks the campus had come to fight itself. He heard it before he saw it. The guards from the security firm stood in one knot by the far door, PYTHIA's engineers in another by the cooling plant, and a few students had come in behind them to film it on their phones. Every one of them was shouting. Since the Apple reached the campus Scott had watched every quarrel turn to a feud by teatime. Nobody here was going to win.

Forty, he counted. Forty-one.

He had been angry since the quay. He had been angry since the boat brought Zoe in, wet to the waist and alone, and the yacht went down behind her with the old man still aboard. It wasn't a hot anger. It was the oldest thing he owned, older than the stick, and in two thousand years he had never let it all the way up. Tonight it was all the way up. He could taste iron.

The machines moved before the people did. A drone lifted off its cradle over the racks, and every arm along the benches unfolded at once and turned its gripper toward the far door. Someone screamed. A torch beam swung across the hall, and in it Scott saw Zoe.

She was on her knees by the cooling plant with her hands up. She must have come looking for him, because she never took anyone's word for where a thing was; she always read the file first. Standing over her was Stelios Rigas, the security firm's night-shift lead, a big man in a black fleece with a pistol held down along his leg. For nine days Rigas had told anyone who would listen that the giant was behind all of it, the lockdown and the drones and the old man drowned off the point, and that anyone who had stood near the giant was with him.

"She was on his boat," Rigas was shouting. "She knows where he's sent them."

"Sent what?" Zoe said. "Stelios, listen to yourself."

He lifted the pistol to the back of her head.

Scott was twenty paces off, and between him and Rigas there was nothing but her. The torch swung away again. In the green light of the racks he could see the line plainly, his arm and the stick and her bent head and the man behind it, the way he had seen a thousand lines on a thousand fields.

Eighty-eight. Eighty-nine.

With Zoe in the line of fire the stick came ready in his hand, its ninety counted out, and he didn't throw it. He felt it the way he always felt it, a dead weight that was suddenly alive, the black shaft warm against his palm and wanting to go. Every year he had lived told him what to do now. The man was armed and a breath from firing, and there was one thing in the hall faster than a pistol. He could put it through Rigas and the rack behind him and the wall behind that. He knew it, and he knew what else it would do. Thrown in anger, it would find the one he loved. It wouldn't matter that she was in the way. It would have found her across the sea.

He lowered it. He leaned it against the end of a bench, and it was the hardest thing he had ever done with his hands.

Then he ran.

Rigas heard him on the last strides and swung the pistol round, and Scott was inside it before it reached him. He caught the wrist and drove it down on the edge of the cooling plant until the gun skittered away under the racks. Rigas hit him in the face, hard. Nobody had hit Scott in the face for a very long time. He took it, stepped in and put his fist under the man's ribs, and when Rigas folded he hit him once more on the jaw, and Rigas went down on the concrete and stayed there.

His knuckles were split. The anger was still all the way up with nowhere to go, and he stood over the man and breathed through it until it began, a little, to come down. Around him the hall had gone quiet.

Zoe got to her feet. She looked at the stick leaning against the bench twenty paces away, and then at him. She knew what it was. He had told her in the conservation store, among the boxes, the whole of it.

"You could have thrown that," she said.

"Yes."

"You didn't."

"No."

She came and took his split hand in both of hers, and held on to it as if it were the safest thing in the room.

He went back for the stick. It was only heavy again.

By the time he came out into the corridor it was past seven by the clock over the fire door, and the long passage was lit only by the green exit sign. Zoe stayed in the doorway behind him, telling the guards to get Rigas on his side before he choked. Amy was where Scott had left her an hour before, sitting on the floor with her back against the wall. She wasn't alone.

Clio lay across her lap. She was small now, smaller than Scott had ever seen her, grey as the floor, and the tapestry was spread over her knees with its last panel uppermost. Her scroll had rolled away against the skirting board. He knew at once. He had stood beside her for every word he had ever written down, and he had never once seen her let go of that scroll.

He knelt. Amy didn't look up at him. Her right hand, the warm one Urania had given back to her, was folded round Clio's, and Clio's fingers were going clear inside it like breath leaving a window.

"I'm here," Amy said. "I've got you."

Clio's lips moved. Scott bent close, the way he had bent close to her all his life, to take down what she said.

"Calliope," she said. "Euterpe. Erato. Thalia." She breathed. "Polyhymnia. Terpsichore. Melpomene. Urania." She stopped there, as if she had to look for the next one, and found it. "Clio."

Her eyes went past Scott to the dark end of the corridor, toward the sea and the plain beyond it.

"Alcyoneus," she said.

Then she looked up at the face above her, and something in her own face eased.

"Amy," she said, last of all.

Clio, the last of the nine Muses, faded in Amy's arms, and Scott felt history end. There was no sound to it. One moment the tapestry lay on a woman's knees, and the next it lay on Amy's, and it was only cloth, a heavy old weave with a knot at the edge where the last thread stopped. For two thousand years someone had stood at his shoulder while he wrote, reading over it, and now nobody did. Whatever happened next would happen all the same, and nobody would weave it.

Amy sat very still with her hand closed on nothing.

"They're all here," she said quietly. "Every one of them. I can feel where they are."

Scott didn't answer. He picked up the scroll from against the skirting board and put it in her lap beside the cloth, because it was hers now, and so was everything else.

Through the open shutter at the end of the corridor the drones hung over the dark roofs where they had stopped. Then, all together, without a sound, every machine on the campus turned, away from the sea, toward the parcel where the giant stood.
