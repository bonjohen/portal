Episode 4: Nobody Chose It

At eight the next morning the graduate room on the fourth floor in Thessaloniki smelled of burnt coffee and wet umbrellas, and Amy Doukas had it to herself. Her field tablet lay on the desk with its cable in, waiting to sync. The dig's records didn't live in Thessaloniki. They lived on the research campus down on Pallene, in a white building with no name on it, on the system everybody called PYTHIA.

Amy watched the folders build on the screen, the survey grid and the finds register with nine years of Petrakis in it. Then a folder opened that she hadn't asked for. It held the programme files, the dull paperwork of the system itself, and at the top sat a small text file called project_info.

She opened it, because she always opened things.

It was four lines long. The programme name was PYTHIA, named in 2021. The funding ran to 2032. The third line said codename, and after it, in capital letters, THYGATER. Under that, someone had typed a comment and signed it S. Malamas: "Nobody on the team chose this codename. Removed it twice. It keeps coming back. Logging as a sync fault."

Amy read the word again. It was Greek in Latin letters, old Greek, the kind Scott put on the board. She smiled at that, the way she smiled at the board. Then the tablet chimed that it was done, and the minibus was leaving at nine. She closed the file, typed a note on her phone, PYTHIA, sync fault, so she would remember to tell somebody, and went to find her boots.

The minibus went south down the coast road in a grey drizzle. Stavros Petridis drove. He drove the way he did everything, cheerfully and a little too fast, with his window down although it was cold, and a thermometer clipped in his shirt pocket like a pen.

"Play me the death," he said. "The quiz is on it."

"The quiz was on Polycrates."

"The next one. Scott always does the next one on whatever we didn't listen to." He laughed before he had finished saying it.

So Amy put last year's recordings of the module on her phone, found the week on the giant's death and turned the speaker up over the engine. Scott's voice came into the minibus, dry and unhurried, as if he were standing in the aisle beside her seat.

"You have to understand the problem Heracles had," Scott said. "Alcyoneus could not die. Not while he stood on his own soil. You could cut him and he stood, you could shoot him and he stood, because the ground he was born from held him up. So Heracles didn't try to kill him there. He drove him off it. He pushed him back over the line, step by step, onto ground that wasn't his, and there he was only big."

Stavros nodded at the windscreen. "So you don't beat him. You move him."

"Shh," Amy said.

"And then," said Scott's voice, "the part the books leave out. The dogs got at the body. And his people went out after the dogs, at night, with lamps, and they gathered every piece of him. Every piece. A finger, a tooth. As if each one mattered." There was a pause on the recording, the stick tapping on the floor. "You may ask yourselves why."

Amy let the rest play and didn't listen. She was thinking that people in grief did strange, careful things with a body. It was not evidence of anything.

"I'd want my pieces back," Stavros said, and laughed again, alone, at a joke she hadn't caught.

The camp stood on the mainland just north of the canal at Nea Potidaia, on a strip of scrub above the shore. By four in the afternoon the drizzle had stopped. There were six tents, a long trench under scaffold and two floodlights on poles, not yet lit. From the edge of the trench Amy could see the bridge over the canal and the low line of Pallene beyond it.

Yannis Petrakis came out of the finds tent with a clipboard and looked at her hand before he looked at her face.

"Doukas," he said. "Nights."

"I'm on the trench."

"You're on finds. Nights. Logging and bagging. Somebody has to keep the paperwork honest while the rest of them sleep."

Amy had worked out her figures on the bus, because she had expected this. "I bagged and logged forty-two contexts in a day at Dion," she said. "With this hand. The trench crew logged thirty. You'll lose time putting me in a tent."

"I lose time arguing with graduate students," Petrakis said. "Nights." He went back into the tent.

Stavros, coming past with a box of tools against his chest, gave her a look that was all sympathy and no help. "Nights are good," he said. "Nights are cool." He walked off to the trench whistling.

At dusk three women came up the track from the coast road, in black, the way the old women of Litochoro dressed for a funeral. Amy took them for mourners from the village. They walked the edge of the trench in single file, and nobody at the supper table looked up. One of them held a rolled paper against her chest. The tallest kept glancing at the first stars as if she were checking them against a chart.

The tallest stopped beside Amy and looked at her right hand.

"This is the one," she said to the others, in a Greek so old it sounded like church.

"She doesn't believe a word of anything," said the youngest, who had the saddest face Amy had ever seen.

"Then she'll learn." The tallest laid two fingers on the back of the prosthetic, where nobody touched it. "Hear us, child. If you won't heed us, I'll give you this hand back myself, and it will be the last thing I ever do."

Amy said she was sorry for their loss. They thanked her as if she had said something kind and went back down the track into the dark, and by the time she sat down to supper it felt like something she had dreamed.

At ten o'clock, when the floodlights came on with a crack and a hum, Amy was alone in the finds tent with the register and a flask of tea. She logged the day's sherds. She checked Petrakis's numbering and found two errors and didn't enjoy it as much as she'd hoped. At midnight the camp was quiet, the generator steady, the guard's radio muttering somewhere near the track.

She stepped out of the tent to rest her eyes.

The cold came off the sea. She stood in the light between the tents with her flask in her good hand, and for one breath she was not standing there at all. She was high above it. She saw the whole camp laid out below her, the six pale tents, the trench like a cut in brown cloth, the two floodlights throwing their circles, and in the middle of it a small figure in a grey coat holding a flask, who was her.

Then she was on the ground again with her own feet under her, and the flask was warm, and the generator hummed.

Amy stood very still. She counted her breaths, which were fast. She had slept four hours the night before and none on the bus. A tired brain built pictures out of what it already knew. She knew where the tents were. She had walked between them. That was all it was, a map her head had drawn for her and handed back. She drank some tea and decided not to write it down.

At two she took the torch and walked the trench edge, because walking kept her awake. The floodlights ended at the far end of the scaffold, and past them the scrub went dark and the ground rose into a shoulder of grey rock. She almost turned back there.

Then the torch beam showed her breath, a thin white smoke, and it blew back into her face, though there was no wind. She held out her good hand. Cold air was coming out of the rock, steady as a draught under a door.

She knelt. Low down in the rock face, half hidden by a root, there was a crack the width of three fingers, and it breathed. It was on none of Petrakis's maps. She was sure of that, because she had read all of them.

Amy put a peg in the ground beside it and tied a strip of orange tape to the peg. Her prosthetic hand fumbled the knot twice, and she made herself slow down and do it properly. Then she sat back on her heels in the dark and laughed, quietly, once, at herself. Nobody else was awake to see it, and nobody else was awake to find it either.
