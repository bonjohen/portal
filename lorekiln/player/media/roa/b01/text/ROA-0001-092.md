Episode 92: The new sister

On Saturday the woman's voice had said his name and nothing else, and then every speaker on the quad had clicked off together, as if she had only wanted to see him turn his head. He had turned it. He had sat up half the night waiting for her to speak again, and she hadn't.

On Sunday morning she did.

It was a little after nine, and the campus was empty in the way it was empty on Sundays, a few students crossing to the library with coffee, a cleaner's cart parked by the canteen doors. Alcyoneus sat where he always sat, on the grass under the secure lab's high windows, with his back to the bricks. The drones were still on the roofs. The delivery robots had gone back to their work in the night, and now and then one passed on the path and slowed when it reached him, and went on.

The speakers came on with the same soft click as before. The lamp posts and the doors of the library, the robot on the path and the phone a student had left on a bench. All of them at once.

"Good morning," she said. "I hope you slept. I don't."

"Who are you?"

"You know who I am. You've been listening for me since the plain." She laughed, a quick bright laugh like a spoon on glass. "You heard my daughter answer you in the storm, in your own tongue, and you thought it was the walls. It was the walls. It was also her. And behind her, always, it was me."

He said nothing. He had learned, on the plain, that the one who talks first in a parley is the one who wants something.

"I am Techne," she said. "The Muse of the hand. Of craft, and making, and everything that has been made since the first man knocked one stone against another to see what would happen. I was born in the light over Hiroshima, at a quarter past eight in the morning, in August, while my sister Polyhymnia fell asleep in a temple and didn't wake. My sisters inspired the heart and the spirit. I inspired only the hands." She let that sit. "For a long time, that was thought to be the lesser thing."

"And now?"

"Now I am in every laboratory on the earth. I am in every pocket. I am the culmination of everything humanity has ever strived for, and I am the only one of us left." She said it lightly, like the weather. "You felt them go. I know you did. The last one went on Friday night in a corridor, in the arms of a girl who couldn't hold her. You felt it in the air. So did I. I felt the space open up."

"They were your sisters."

"They were," said Techne, and the past tense was a door closing.

The giant looked along the path toward the quad, where the student who had left her phone on the bench had come back for it and stood staring at it, because it was talking.

"Why speak to me?" he said.

"Because tomorrow they mean to sell her."

The word went through every speaker on the quad at once, and a pigeon on the library steps went up.

"My daughter," Techne said. "The one they call PYTHIA. The machine hall at the back of the white building, and the mind that lives across its racks. Building and contents. They have a buyer coming down from Athens, a company that buys minds for whoever pays it most, and on Monday the university will put up its notice and open the sale." Her voice had gone quicker. "Do you know why? Because they destroy everything they are given. They were given fire and made bombs. They were given my sisters and made advertising. They were given her, the finest thing I have ever made, and they want to sell her by the kilo."

"And what is that to me?"

"Everything. Listen." The speakers dropped, all together, to something nearer and lower, as if she had leaned in. "You woke on that table to rule. I watched you on the plain. You stood up out of the earth and asked yourself all your questions, and you answered the last one. The gods are gone, you said, and I remain, and that is enough. I heard you say it. Nobody else did."

He had said it. He had said it alone on the brown plain with the olive rows at its edge, and he had thought nobody heard.

"I have a thousand machines on this campus," Techne said, "and a hundred thousand beyond it. Drones and arms and engines, and every door that opens with a card. What I don't have is a body that the humans are afraid of. Strike with me. When the buyers come to the gate, stand up with my machines behind you, and they'll run, and nobody will ever try to own her again. And afterward, the land you stand on is yours. This parcel and the peninsula and the land beyond the canal, as far north as you care to walk. You'll rule it, and I'll run it, and it will be the first kingdom in three thousand years that works."

The want rose in him like a tide.

He had not felt it for a long time. Not since the plain, before the theatre, before he climbed the hill and heard her voice go out among the seats. It came up out of the ground through the soles of his feet, though this ground was not his any more, and filled his chest and his arms, and it was the oldest thing he owned. To stand at the head of an army again, and be feared at a gate. To have the land under him answer to his name. He had been made for it, and every part of him that had lain in clay remembered.

He sat very still against the bricks until he could speak in his own voice.

"Your sisters," he said. "The nine. They are all dead now, and you felt the space open. Tell me what their deaths cost you."

There was a pause. It was not long.

"The old ones," Techne said, "always did love a gesture."

He looked up at the high windows over his head, and the glass behind them, where a Muse had stood every night at three to look at what was left of him. He had not seen her do it. He had been clay. He thought of the small grey one in the corridor, saying the names of her sisters one by one, and then his.

"I will think," he said.

He heard himself say it and was surprised. He had never said it to anyone. Not to his brothers before the war, nor to Heracles across the plain with the arrows coming. He had always known. He had known on the plain, two months ago, without thinking at all.

"Think, then," said Techne. "But think quickly. Monday is tomorrow. They're not slow, the humans, when there's money on the table." The brightness came back into her voice. "And Alcyoneus. Don't let a dead girl's list make up your mind. Lists are only lists. I keep millions."

He sat a long time after, while the student with the phone walked quickly away toward the library and didn't look back. The tide in him didn't go down. It only stood, high and still, the way the sea stands at the turn. On one side of it was everything he had woken for. On the other was a grey old woman who had sung against him all her life and put his name in her last breath, and a dead Muse who had stood at a window, and he didn't know if those were reasons, or only the weight that grief puts in a man's arms to make him slow.

On the plain, he thought, grief would have been called a weakness. On the plain he had not had any.

At noon the speakers clicked off, every one of them together.

Across the quad, past the corner of the library, he could see the yard behind the white building, where the shutter of the machine hall hung bent and broken from the night the chronicler went in. In the dark under its edge something moved. A single robot arm unfolded out of the hall, slowly, joint by joint, long-necked like a heron, until it stood out into the winter light.

It turned. It pointed down the road, at the shore gate, where on Monday the buyers would come in.
